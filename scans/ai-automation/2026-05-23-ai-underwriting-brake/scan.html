The Underwriting Brake: How AI Exclusions Are Becoming the Real Constraint on Enterprise Automation

While boards debate compute, talent and the EU AI Act, the commercial insurance market is quietly rewriting standard liability policies to exclude AI-related losses — making insurability, not capability, an emerging constraint on agentic-AI deployment across the 2026-2028 renewal cycles.

The consensus on enterprise AI in 2026 is acceleration: boards are told the binding constraints are compute, talent, data and an unsettled regulatory map. Beneath that, a quieter actor is setting terms. Through standard-form exclusion endorsements, sweeping new policy language and a thin affirmative-cover market, the commercial insurance industry is deciding which AI-related losses a deploying enterprise can transfer and which it must retain. The board question is no longer only how fast it can deploy AI, but whether the resulting risk is insurable at all — and that answer arrives at renewal, before regulators finish the rules.

Signal Identification

This is a risk-transfer repricing — a structural shift in how the cost of AI failure is allocated, not a capability story. That AI is risky is consensus; the signal is that the insurance market is resolving AI's risk allocation faster than courts or regulators, and largely by exclusion — making insurability a deployment-gating variable upstream of strategy.

Time horizon: 2–4 years (exclusion endorsements live now; insurability gates deployment decisions across the 2026-2028 renewals) Plausibility band: Medium–High Geographic / Jurisdictional Scope: Primary: the United States, where standardised policy forms and state filings drive the change. Spillover: the UK, the London market and the EU. Sectors exposed: Enterprises deploying customer-facing or decision-making AI — technology, financial services, healthcare, professional services, marketing; AI vendors; risk, legal and board functions.

What's Changing

The concrete change is in policy wording. The Insurance Services Office, whose standard forms are used across the US market, has introduced three generative-AI exclusion endorsements for commercial general liability cover; individual carriers go further still. Berkley has filed "absolute" AI exclusions in directors-and-officers, errors-and-omissions and fiduciary lines, barring any claim "based upon, arising out of, or attributable to" the use, deployment or development of AI (Policyholder Pulse, 13/04/2026).

Its breadth makes this more than housekeeping. A May 2026 study coded 55 AI threat classes against 26 insurance products and mapped a four-tier "insurability frontier": perils affirmatively insured, perils sitting as unpriced "silent-AI" exposure inside legacy cyber, E&O, D&O and media policies, perils actively excluded, and perils outside private insurance altogether (arXiv, 06/05/2026). Foundation-model concentration is the new problem — one upstream failure can correlate losses across thousands of insureds.

Insurers are retreating because the loss data to price AI incidents does not exist; even major AI developers cannot buy the cover they want, and some weigh self-insurance funded by investors (Tufts Now, 12/05/2026). The claims arrive not as breaches but through ordinary chatbot and call-analytics interactions, so an AI claim "simply arrives looking for a home" across many liability lines (Insurance Journal, 20/05/2026).

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in three stages. Through 2026 the change is one of language: exclusion endorsements are filed and inserted at renewal, while most cover stays silent on AI and the gap goes unnoticed until a claim tests it. Across 2026-2027 comes the renewal reckoning: as exclusions spread, enterprises find exposures they assumed were covered carved out, with affirmative capacity too thin to replace them. By 2027-2028, AI risk is repriced and underwritten on demonstrated controls rather than blanket exclusion or silence.

Three stresses concentrate the strain. A coverage gap opens where standard cover is withdrawn but affirmative cover is scarce, leaving risk unpriced on enterprise balance sheets. Contested wording follows — exclusions broad enough to swallow cover for any AI-touched claim invite dispute, and courts construe exclusions narrowly against the insurer (Policyholder Pulse, 13/04/2026). And correlation caps the market: foundation-model concentration limits how much affirmative capacity can prudently be written. Two adaptations follow. AI governance becomes an underwriting artefact — model inventories, model cards and drift monitoring, the evidence the NAIC's AI Systems Evaluation Tool now asks examiners to review (NAIC, 24/03/2026). And risk transfer migrates to specialist carriers and vendor warranties, or is retained.

Why This Matters

For boards, CFOs and chief risk officers, insurability now belongs inside the AI deployment decision, not in a renewal conversation held after the fact. A firm that deploys an agentic system assuming its liability tower will respond may find, at renewal, the exposure excluded and silently returned to its balance sheet. The decision architecture that needs revision treats AI as a technology-function matter and insurance as a procurement afterthought. State regulators supervise how insurers use AI, not whether AI losses are covered (NAIC, March 2026) — so coverage is being settled by carriers.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the exclusion wave is live but the insurability inflection lands across the 2026-2028 renewals; map AI exposure across the policy tower and build underwritable governance now, committing to alternative risk transfer when a broad exclusion reaches the firm's own cover.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that this is how insurance digests every new risk class, and the cyber precedent shows the arc ends well: silent exposure, then exclusions, then affirmative products. Affirmative AI capacity is already emerging, the cyber parallel explicit (Insurance Business, 21/05/2026); exclusions drafted too broadly are vulnerable to the doctrine against illusory coverage, and much AI risk already falls under existing "silent" policies (Policyholder Pulse, 13/04/2026).

That arc is real, but two features differ. AI is diffusing into every liability line at once and faster than cyber did, so the gap period — exclusions live, affirmative capacity thin — is itself the brake. And foundation-model concentration is a structural ceiling cyber never faced at scale (arXiv, 06/05/2026). Insurability and liability are structurally interdependent, not a problem the market simply grows out of (Connecticut Insurance Law Journal, 06/05/2026).

Implications

This is durable structural change to the risk-transfer architecture, not a transient hard-market spasm. A capability can be bought; permission to transfer its downside cannot. The inflection window is the 2026-2028 renewals, and it rewards preparation asymmetrically: enterprises that can evidence AI governance stay insurable on workable terms, while those that deployed assuming silent coverage absorb the risk. Because insurability and liability move together (Connecticut Insurance Law Journal, 06/05/2026), how courts resolve AI liability will decide whether the affirmative market scales or the exclusion becomes the default.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • Carrier adoption of the ISO generative-AI exclusion endorsements at 2026 general liability renewals.
  • A US state insurance regulator approving or rejecting a broad general-liability AI exclusion filing.
  • A court ruling on whether an "absolute" AI exclusion bars coverage, testing the illusory-coverage doctrine.
  • Aggregate affirmative AI insurance capacity — named carriers and limits — rising materially through 2026.
  • The NAIC finalising its AI Systems Evaluation Tool at the 2026 Fall National Meeting.

Disconfirming Signals

  • The Insurance Services Office withdraws or materially narrows its generative-AI exclusion endorsements.
  • Courts read "absolute" AI exclusions narrowly, so they rarely bar coverage in practice.
  • Affirmative AI capacity scales quickly to limits comparable with the cover withdrawn.
  • Carriers broadly add endorsements confirming AI losses remain covered, rather than excluding them.
  • No correlated foundation-model loss event materialises and underwriters keep AI inside standard policies.

Strategic Questions

  • Should the firm price uninsured AI loss into deployment business cases now, or wait until renewal reveals its coverage position?
  • When does an AI exclusion on the D&O or E&O tower make a planned agentic deployment a board decision?
  • Build the governance evidence needed to stay insurable, or accept self-retention and deploy faster?
  • Who owns the gap between what the firm assumes is covered and what its renewed policies exclude?
