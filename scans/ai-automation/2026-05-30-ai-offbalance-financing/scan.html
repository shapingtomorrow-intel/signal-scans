Off the Books: How AI's Build-Out Quietly Became a Financing Risk

The consensus reads the AI boom as a capital-expenditure story funded from hyperscaler cash flows. The weak signal is that the financing has migrated off balance sheet into special-purpose vehicles, private credit and residual-value-guaranteed leases, concentrating opaque leverage that regulators began naming through 2026.

The consensus narrative on artificial intelligence is a story about capability and capital expenditure: hyperscalers will spend something like $600 billion in 2026, the argument goes, and their balance sheets are strong enough to absorb it. Beneath that headline a quieter shift is underway. A growing share of the build-out is no longer funded from corporate cash flows or recorded as corporate debt; it is pushed into special-purpose vehicles, private-credit facilities and lease structures designed to keep the obligation off the income statement. The inflection question is no longer whether AI demand is real, but what happens to a concentrated, opaque layer of leverage if the revenue meant to service it arrives late. Through 2026 the world's financial-stability authorities started asking that question out loud.

Signal Identification

This is a financial-structure signal: a pivot in how the AI build-out is financed, not a change in the technology itself. It sits beneath the capex narrative, visible mainly in disclosures, lease footnotes and central-bank risk assessments rather than product launches.

Time horizon: 2-4 years (lease commencements 2026-2031; stress crystallises 2027-2029 if AI revenue disappoints) Plausibility band: Medium-High Geographic / Jurisdictional Scope: Primary: the United States, where hyperscaler disclosures, GAAP lease treatment and private-credit origination drive the structure. Spillover: the euro area and the UK, where insurers, pension funds and the London market hold the exposure. Sectors exposed: hyperscalers and Big Tech; private-credit asset managers; insurers and pension funds; banks (through interlinkages); data-centre landlords and commercial real estate; semiconductors; index-tracking institutional investors.

What's Changing

The clearest marker is accounting. Moody's Ratings calculates that the top five US hyperscalers had amassed roughly $969 billion in undiscounted future data-centre lease commitments by end-2025, of which about $662 billion sits off balance sheet because the leases have not yet commenced, a hidden total equal to 113% of the five firms' most recent adjusted debt (Moody's Ratings, reported by Fortune, 25/02/2026). Because AI hardware lasts only four to six years, firms take shorter leases backstopped by off-balance-sheet residual value guarantees; Meta recorded no liability for one such $28 billion guarantee.

The funding source is shifting too. The Financial Stability Board warns that private credit has grown to an estimated $1.5-2.0 trillion, is concentrated in technology, and carries valuation opacity and reliance on private ratings; it counts around $220 billion of drawn and undrawn bank credit lines to private-credit funds, with commercial estimates reaching $270-500 billion (Financial Stability Board, 06/05/2026). The IMF flags that parts of the AI value chain increasingly rely on circular financing, where the same firms fund each other's demand (IMF Global Financial Stability Report, 14/04/2026).

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in three stages. Off-balance-sheet structuring expands while AI revenue is still a promise: SPVs, vendor financing and residual-value-guaranteed leases let firms book capacity without booking debt, and private-credit funds supply capital that public markets would price more harshly. Then the leases commence between 2026 and 2031, moving obligations onto balance sheets just as the four-to-six-year hardware cycle forces reinvestment, so adjusted leverage rises mechanically even if nothing goes wrong. Finally, any shortfall in AI monetisation, a delayed enterprise payback or a depreciation surprise, tests the thin equity cushions and the guarantees at once, because the same handful of counterparties anchor the whole structure.

Stresses concentrate at three points: the residual-value guarantees that convert technology obsolescence into a contingent cash call; the private-credit funds whose technology concentration the FSB and ECB both single out; and the insurers and pension funds holding the senior debt. The ECB's stress scenario shows where the loss lands, with euro-area pension funds losing 5-6% of total assets and insurers around 4%, while banks stay below 1.3% of equity (European Central Bank, 27/05/2026). Two adaptations follow: supervisors move to close private-credit data gaps and reach for look-through disclosure, and lenders reprice residual-value risk and shorten tenors, raising the build-out's true cost of capital.

Why This Matters

For boards, CFOs and institutional investors, the signal reframes AI risk from an operational question into a balance-sheet one. A company exposed to AI through its index weighting, its pension fund, its insurer or its bank may be carrying AI-financing risk it never underwrote, because the leverage is concentrated, correlated and partly invisible. The ECB makes the comparison explicit, putting US private credit at about $1.4 trillion, a scale comparable to the pre-crisis subprime mortgage market. The decision-architecture that needs revision is exposure aggregation: treasury, risk and investment functions should map indirect AI-financing exposure across counterparties rather than assume the hyperscalers' strong headline balance sheets insulate them.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the structure is building now but the stress is contingent on an AI-revenue disappointment that has not yet arrived; set up exposure-mapping and scenario triggers rather than acting on a crisis that is not here.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that this is prudent capital management, not hidden fragility. The hyperscalers generate enormous operating cash flow and carry minimal net leverage; off-balance-sheet structuring spreads financing across institutional investors paid to hold it, and the assets are real, revenue-producing data centres. Moody's itself frames the $662 billion as obligations "yet to be on the balance sheet" rather than concealed, and the IMF judged the stability impact of AI circularity "modest currently" (IMF Global Financial Stability Report, 14/04/2026).

That holds only while AI revenue tracks the build-out. The signal matters because the cushion is thin and the counterparties few: if returns disappoint, evidence of which is accumulating as firms quietly retire underperforming AI deployments (Fortune, 28/05/2026), the same opacity that lowers today's cost of capital will slow tomorrow's price discovery, the mechanism by which modest problems become correlated ones.

Implications

Taken together, the four authorities suggest a durable structural shift rather than a transient financing fashion: the off-balance-sheet layer is being built into the architecture of the AI economy, with the inflection window the 2026-2029 lease-commencement and refinancing cycle. The reading here is that financing structure, not model capability, is becoming the binding constraint and the transmission channel: it determines who absorbs the loss if AI monetisation lags, routing it through private credit into insurers and pension funds rather than banks. Positioned to gain are disciplined lenders who price obsolescence risk correctly and supervisors who secure look-through data early; positioned to lose are yield-seeking institutions holding concentrated, thinly-cushioned senior debt against four-to-six-year assets.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • FSB or IOSCO publication of harmonised private-credit reporting metrics, or a supervisor mandating look-through disclosure of data-centre SPV and residual-value-guarantee exposures.
  • A hyperscaler 10-Q or 10-K disclosing a material increase in residual-value guarantees or commenced-lease liabilities moving onto the balance sheet.
  • A credit-rating action or negative outlook on a hyperscaler, neocloud or data-centre ABS/CMBS citing off-balance-sheet commitments.
  • Widening spreads or a failed/repriced syndication on a large data-centre private-credit facility or SPV.
  • An ECB, BoE or Fed financial-stability publication escalating AI-financing from "monitor" to a named systemic vulnerability with a quantified exposure.

Disconfirming Signals

  • Enterprise AI revenue and measurable ROI accelerate, comfortably covering committed capacity and validating the demand assumption.
  • Hyperscalers fund a rising share of capex from on-balance-sheet cash and equity, shrinking the off-balance-sheet layer.
  • GPU and accelerator useful lives lengthen, easing the depreciation and residual-value pressure that drives short-tenor structuring.
  • Private-credit exposure to technology and data centres diversifies, and equity cushions in new SPV deals thicken materially.
  • Regulators conclude, after look-through data, that interlinkages are smaller and better-cushioned than headline estimates imply.

Strategic Questions

  • Should treasury and risk map indirect AI-financing exposure across counterparties now, or wait for a rating action to force it?
  • At what evidence threshold, lease migration, spread widening or an ROI miss, does this move from Prepare to Decide?
  • Do our pension and insurance providers hold concentrated AI-linked private credit, and have we asked them?
  • If AI monetisation slips two years, which of our exposures reprice first, and can we hedge them?
