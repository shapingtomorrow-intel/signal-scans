After the Precautionary Surge: How a Shrinking Sender Base Is Hollowing Out the Remittance Economy

Record remittance inflows are masking a structural erosion of the migrant sender base; as a precautionary surge exhausts and a new US transfer tax bites, remittance-dependent economies face a 2026-2028 reversal fiscal models are not pricing.

The consensus on remittances is reassuring: the money migrants send home set records again in 2025, proved counter-cyclical through every recent shock, and now exceeds foreign aid for most of the developing world. Beneath that headline sits a quieter shift. The 2025 record was not strength but a one-off precautionary surge — migrants sending more ahead of possible deportation — even as the number of senders began to fall. A shrinking sender base, a new US transfer tax and tightening compliance friction now converge on a flow recipient economies treat as dependable. Are they budgeting for resilient growth that has, in fact, already peaked?

Signal Identification

This is an emerging inflection in a flow treated as structurally stable. The consensus reads record 2025 inflows as resilience; the signal is that they were inflated by precautionary behaviour that cannot repeat, while the sender base — the real engine of remittances — contracts through reduced migration and rising deportations. Taxation and compliance friction accelerate the erosion, producing a plateau-and-decline that fiscal and sovereign-risk models are not pricing.

Time horizon: Near-term to 3 years (precautionary surge exhausting through 2026; structural plateau and decline 2026-2028) Plausibility band: Medium–High Geographic / Jurisdictional Scope: Primary: remittance-dependent Central America (Honduras, Guatemala, El Salvador, Nicaragua) and Mexico; the United States as policy originator. Spillover: South Asia, the Caribbean and the money-transfer industry. Sectors exposed: Sovereign credit and public finance in recipient economies; money-transfer and remittance fintech; banks and foreign exchange; development finance; consumer demand; anti-money-laundering and compliance functions.

What's Changing

The most visible change is fiscal. A new US 1% excise tax on remittance transfers took effect for transfers occurring after 31 December 2025, created by the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act, and Treasury and the IRS issued proposed implementing regulations on 13 April 2026 (Federal Register, 13/04/2026). It falls only on transfers funded with cash, a money order or a cashier's check, exempting bank- and card-funded transfers (Grant Thornton, 28/04/2026) — concentrating the cost on lower-income and undocumented cash senders.

The less visible change is the sender base. In 2025 most of Latin America and the Caribbean recorded the strongest remittance growth in two decades, with nine countries that originate over 90% of regional flows receiving US$158 billion. But that growth came not from new migration: it came from a 27% jump in the average amount remitted, as migrants front-loaded transfers ahead of possible deportation (Inter-American Dialogue, 02/01/2026). The same analysis projects regional growth below 2% in 2026 as that precautionary impulse exhausts.

Mexico, the second-largest recipient worldwide, shows the divergence cleanly. First-quarter 2026 inflows hit a record US$14.45 billion, up 1.4% year-on-year — yet the number of transfers fell 4%, a larger average ticket masking fewer senders (Mexico News Daily, 05/05/2026). Remittances to Mexico had already fallen 4.6% in 2025, the worst annual decline in 16 years.

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in two stages. Through 2026 the precautionary surge unwinds: the Inter-American Dialogue notes the upward cycle in average remittance size, begun in October 2024, runs on a roughly 17-month rhythm and could slow from around April 2026 (Inter-American Dialogue, 02/01/2026) — removing the mechanism behind the 2025 record. As migration falls and deportations of long-settled, remitting migrants continue, the count of active senders declines structurally. Into 2027-2028, headline values stop rising, then fall: a smaller diaspora cannot indefinitely offset its shrinking number with larger transfers.

Three stresses concentrate. Fiscal: in the most exposed Central American economies remittances fund a fifth or more of GDP, so a flat-to-falling flow tightens public finances already short of buffers — the World Bank projects regional growth of just 2.1% in 2026 (World Bank, 08/04/2026), with net energy importers most exposed to global shocks (Americas Society/Council of the Americas, 15/04/2026), and Honduras among several sovereigns under an IMF programme (International Monetary Fund, 11/05/2026). Channel leakage: taxation and FinCEN scrutiny push cash senders to informal routes, eroding measured flows and oversight. Two adaptations follow: governments court diaspora finance through other instruments; the money-transfer industry consolidates and digitises to defend margins.

Why This Matters

For finance ministries, central banks, sovereign lenders and investors exposed to remittance-dependent economies, the error is to read the 2025 record as a baseline. It was a peak produced by precautionary front-loading — behaviour that structurally cannot repeat. Budgets, debt-sustainability analyses and sovereign-risk models built on continued remittance growth rest on a number that has likely already turned. The decision architecture needing revision is the one filing remittances under "stable external inflow" rather than as a policy-exposed variable now trending the wrong way. The window to re-baseline forecasts is the 2026 budget and rating cycle, before the plateau becomes a visible decline.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the mechanisms are documented and the tax is already in force, but the full plateau-and-decline plays out across 2026-2028, leaving a defined window to re-baseline fiscal and sovereign-risk assumptions before the reversal shows in headline data.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that the signal overstates a modest effect. Mexico's Q1 2026 inflows hit a record, not a decline (Mexico News Daily, 05/05/2026), and 99% of transfers to Mexico are now electronic, so the cash-only tax barely touches the biggest corridor. Independent estimates put the tax's macro hit at a few tenths of a percent of GDP, easily routed around through exempt channels.

But the resilience of the headline number is the point. The 2025 record and the Q1 rebound were larger tickets from fewer senders — flattering the total while the base erodes. A smaller diaspora cannot raise average transfers forever; the Dialogue's own projection of sub-2% regional growth in 2026 concedes the surge is ending. The tax is a marginal accelerant; the mechanism is the shrinking sender base, and it does not reverse on a policy timetable.

Implications

This is durable structural change, not a transient dip. Remittances will remain large, but the era of reliable annual growth underwritten by a steadily expanding diaspora is closing. The inflection window is 2026-2028: the precautionary surge exhausts, then the headline value follows the sender count down — a sequencing the Inter-American Dialogue's sub-2% 2026 projection already implies (Inter-American Dialogue, 02/01/2026). Economies that built consumption and fiscal revenue around a permanent, growing transfer are the most exposed; those that diversified external income early are better placed. For the money-transfer industry, value migrates from volume growth to digital cost advantage and consolidation.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • Central banks in Mexico and Central America reporting year-on-year declines in the number of remittance transactions across consecutive 2026 quarters.
  • Full-year 2026 remittance totals for Honduras, Guatemala and El Salvador coming in flat or negative against 2025.
  • First IRS Form 720 filings of the Section 4475 excise tax showing collections, and implied cash volumes, below projection.
  • US states enacting their own remittance taxes, or FinCEN extending verification on cross-border transfers.
  • Rating agencies or the IMF naming remittance softening as a downside risk for remittance-dependent economies.

Disconfirming Signals

  • Remittance values and transaction counts both rising through 2026 across major recipient corridors.
  • The US 1% transfer tax repealed or narrowed, or shown to have negligible channel-shifting effect.
  • A rebound in migration to the US or other high-income destinations rebuilding the sender base.
  • Recipient-country 2026 GDP and consumption data showing no measurable remittance drag.
  • The precautionary larger-ticket behaviour proving durable rather than a one-off, sustaining growth without new senders.

Strategic Questions

  • Should remittance-dependent sovereigns rebuild 2026-2028 fiscal baselines on flat remittances now, or wait for confirmed decline?
  • When does a falling transaction count, not a rising headline value, become the metric driving sovereign-risk assessment?
  • Should money-transfer firms compete for a shrinking cash market or pivot capital to digital and diaspora-finance products?
