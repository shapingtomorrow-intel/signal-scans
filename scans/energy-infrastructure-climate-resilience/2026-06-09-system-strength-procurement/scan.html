When Inertia Stops Being Free: Grids Pivot From Building Power to Buying Stability

The consensus frames the grid challenge as building enough clean generation and wires fast enough. The weak signal beneath it: as synchronous plants retire, system strength and inertia stop arriving as free byproducts, and operators are now procuring them explicitly through grid-forming mandates and stability markets on a 2026-2030 horizon.

The consensus story about decarbonising power is a story about volume: build clean generation fast enough, build the wires to move it, and clear the interconnection queues that hold it back. Beneath that headline a quieter shift is underway. The binding reliability constraint is migrating from how much energy a grid can supply to whether it can stay stable second to second once the spinning machines are gone. Synchronous coal, gas and hydro plants supplied inertia, fault current, reactive power and oscillation damping automatically, as a physical byproduct of operating. Retire them and that support layer disappears. Through early 2026 regulators and operators stopped treating these stability services as free and began buying them explicitly. The question is no longer only how fast the clean fleet is built, but who pays to keep it standing up.

Signal Identification

This is a structural-shift-and-regulatory-pivot signal: the reclassification of grid stability from a free byproduct of thermal generation into a procured commodity, delivered through grid-forming inverter mandates, synchronous-condenser retrofits and dedicated stability markets. It is visible in blackout forensics, system-operator tenders and grid-code revisions rather than in generation-capacity statistics, and it sits beneath the build-more-renewables narrative that dominates energy-transition coverage.

Time horizon: 2-7 years (mandates and first markets 2026-2027; stability priced as a commodity 2027-2030) Plausibility band: Medium-High Geographic / Jurisdictional Scope: Primary: the EU-27 and Great Britain, where grid-code revisions and stability markets are most advanced; Australia (NEM) as a leading precedent. Spillover: the United States and any high-renewables grid retiring synchronous plant. Sectors exposed: Transmission and system operators, BESS developers and inverter OEMs, synchronous-condenser and STATCOM vendors, renewables developers facing connection constraints, infrastructure investors and project financiers.

What's Changing

The forensic verdict on Europe's worst recent outage relocated the problem from supply to stability. The final report of the 49-member expert panel on the 28 April 2025 Iberian blackout (ENTSO-E, 20/03/2026) attributed the collapse to many interacting factors — oscillations, gaps in voltage and reactive-power control, differing voltage-regulation practices and cascading generator disconnections — and called for regulatory frameworks to adapt to the changed physics of the system. ENTSO-E's president framed the lesson bluntly, that the problem was voltage control rather than renewables, and Spain's Operational Procedure 7.4 letting renewables contribute to voltage control was fully implemented on 17 March 2026 (PV Tech, 23/03/2026).

The institutional response is to procure what the thermal fleet used to give away. The IEA's annual flagship notes that as synchronous generators decline, system strength can be reinforced by deploying grid-forming inverters and synchronous condensers, even as more than 2,500 GW of projects sit stalled in connection queues and annual grid investment needs to rise about 50% by 2030 (International Energy Agency, 06/02/2026). Great Britain has already turned the principle into a market: NESO awarded five contracts to four providers to secure 7.3 GVA.s of inertia for October 2026 to September 2027 at an estimated GBP 10.3 million, expected to return up to GBP 44.4 million in consumer savings (NESO, 24/02/2026).

The shift is moving from voluntary to mandatory. With variable renewables at roughly 36% of installed capacity in 2025 and projected near 56% by 2035, ENTSO-E has published technical requirements to harmonise grid-forming capability, with formal regulation expected in 2026 (Wood Mackenzie, 27/01/2026). The economics of the missing services are surfacing too: inertia, fault current and reactive power that spinning machines once gave for nothing are now being re-sourced from synchronous condensers and grid-forming batteries, and how they are paid for varies by jurisdiction, recognised as a paid service in Britain yet treated as free in Sweden (SolarQuarter, 20/05/2026).

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in three stages. In the first, now to roughly 2027, operators name the problem and stand up the first procurement vehicles: Britain's enduring stability markets, the European grid-forming requirements heading into formal regulation, and a parallel push in Australia where the system operator has made grid-forming batteries a priority action. In the second, 2027 to about 2030, stability hardens into a priced commodity: grid-forming control becomes the default for new large batteries, synchronous condensers are retrofitted into weak-grid regions, and availability payments for inertia, fault current and reactive power become a revenue line. In a third stage the constraint eases, as procurement unlocks renewable dispatch that stability limits would otherwise curtail.

Stresses concentrate at three points. Weak-grid regions far from former thermal hubs lose system strength first, throttling the very renewable projects meant to replace that generation. Interconnection economics distort where stability limits, not thermal capacity, gate new connections. And grid-forming control software is often shielded by manufacturer intellectual property, leaving operators short of the validated models they need for system studies. The structural adaptations follow at three levels: operationally, grid-forming becomes the default rather than a premium option; in regulation, mandates and standards harden, from Europe's grid-code revision to comparable inverter ride-through and grid-forming rules in the United States and Australia; financially, stability services acquire explicit prices, turning a once-free externality into a contracted cash flow that reshapes battery and condenser project economics.

Why This Matters

For utility and developer boards, BESS investors and infrastructure financiers, this reframes two decisions. The first is specification and capital: a grid-forming-capable battery costs more up front but earns access to emerging stability revenue and avoids a later forced retrofit, so the question is whether to standardise on grid-forming now or wait for a mandate to force it. The second is location and connection risk: where system strength, not headline capacity, governs dispatch, project siting and revenue forecasts should price stability constraints explicitly. Taken together, the sources suggest the revenue architecture of the power system is being rewritten beneath the capacity headlines.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the mandates and markets are forming now but not yet universal, so set grid-forming specification and stability-revenue scenarios in place and commit on the trigger of a binding grid-code rule in your jurisdiction.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that the signal over-reads a single event. ENTSO-E concluded that even with significantly higher inertia the loss of synchronism would not have been avoided, locating the Iberian fault in voltage control rather than a simple inertia deficit (ENTSO-E, 20/03/2026). Sceptics add that grid-forming batteries mimic inertia only for microseconds, degrade with cycling, and carry IP-opaque control models, so a rush to build dedicated "stability markets" risks paying for a service that targeted operational fixes — better voltage-control settings, reactive-power management — could deliver more cheaply (SolarQuarter, 20/05/2026).

The counter-counter is that the signal is not "inertia alone". It is the recognition that the whole bundle of stability services — voltage, reactive power, fault current, oscillation damping and inertia — must now be procured rather than assumed, which is precisely what the blackout panel recommends and what European, British and Australian operators are building. National-laboratory testing on Kaua'i found grid-forming resources significantly enhanced stability, with a 2023 generator trip producing no oscillation after the switch to grid-forming control (NREL via pv magazine, 13/01/2026). The instrument may evolve; the reclassification of stability from free to bought is the durable shift.

Implications

On the available evidence this catalyses durable change rather than transient evolution. The inflection window is 2026-2030, opening with Europe's grid-forming regulation and Britain's stability markets and widening as comparable rules land elsewhere. Those positioned to gain are grid-forming BESS developers, synchronous-condenser and STATCOM vendors, and providers able to sell validated stability services; those exposed are grid-following-only fleets and late movers who face retrofit costs and connection constraints once system strength, not capacity, governs dispatch. The deeper implication is that a reliability attribute the power system enjoyed for free is becoming a line item — and line items get markets, prices and winners.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • The European Commission's grid-code revision is finalised with binding grid-forming performance requirements for inverter-based resources.
  • NERC's PRC-029-1 frequency and voltage ride-through standard for inverter-based resources takes effect in the United States on 1 October 2026.
  • Australia's NEM operator and Transgrid advance their planned multi-gigawatt grid-forming-battery and synchronous-condenser system-strength portfolio in New South Wales.
  • Further national stability or inertia markets and availability-payment tenders open beyond Great Britain.
  • Grid-forming capability becomes a mandatory line item in large-scale battery procurement and connection-offer specifications.

Disconfirming Signals

  • Regulators defer or dilute grid-forming mandates, leaving capability voluntary past 2027.
  • Operators meet stability needs cheaply by retaining synchronous condensers or gas plant, with no dedicated market emerging.
  • Stability-market clearing prices collapse as inertia and fault-current supply outruns demand, removing the revenue thesis.
  • System operators reframe the post-blackout fix as purely operational tuning of settings rather than procured services.
  • Field reliability problems or model-validation failures stall grid-forming deployment at scale.

Strategic Questions

  • Should new battery assets standardise on grid-forming capability now, or wait for a mandate to force the cost?
  • Do you underwrite stability-service revenue into BESS business cases today, or treat it as unpriced upside?
  • At what threshold does system-strength risk, not capacity, become the binding input to a connection or siting decision?
  • Who in the portfolio is most exposed if stability shifts from a free byproduct to a charged service?
