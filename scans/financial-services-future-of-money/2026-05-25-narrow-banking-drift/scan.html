The Narrow-Banking Drift: How Stablecoins Are Quietly Reshaping Who Creates Credit

Post-GENIUS Act, stablecoins are read as a deposit-share contest between banks and crypto. The deeper signal is a drift toward narrow banking: as deposits convert to fully-reserved tokens, credit creation migrates out of the banking system across 2026-2028.

The consensus on the future of money has hardened into a contest. Since the GENIUS Act took effect in July 2025, banks and stablecoin issuers are described as fighting a "$6 trillion war for deposits" over which institution holds the customer's dollar. That framing misses what matters. Stablecoins do not merely move deposits between institutions; they change what a deposit does. A dollar inside a fractional-reserve bank funds a loan; the same dollar inside a stablecoin sits fully reserved in Treasury bills and cash, financing the government rather than the private economy. The signal is a structural drift toward narrow banking — and the question for boards is not who holds deposits, but who still creates credit.

Signal Identification

This is a shift in monetary plumbing, not a product-competition story. The consensus measures deposit market share; the signal is that stablecoins re-engineer money itself. A payment stablecoin is fully backed by safe assets rather than fractionally lent, so every converted dollar is withdrawn from credit creation — and even banks that capture the flows lend a smaller share of their assets.

What's Changing

Stablecoin market capitalisation reached $317 billion by 6 April 2026, up more than 50% since early 2025, though growth flattened across late 2025 and early 2026 (Federal Reserve Board, 08/04/2026) — small against roughly $8 trillion of US bank deposits, but large enough for the mechanism to surface. Central banks have named it: the BIS observes that stablecoins resemble narrow banks, loosening the link between deposit-taking and lending in a fractional reserve system (Bank for International Settlements, 20/04/2026).

The cost is no longer hypothetical. A Federal Reserve Bank of New York study offers the first transaction-level evidence of liquidity-driven disintermediation: partner banks' loan share of assets contracts relative to peers, as volatile redemptions force larger reserve balances (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, February 2026). The banks winning the deposits lend less of them. The magnitude is contested: the White House Council of Economic Advisers models the effect as trivial today, 0.02% of lending, yet concedes a worst case of $531 billion, a 4.4% contraction in bank loans (White House Council of Economic Advisers, 08/04/2026).

The reserve side compounds it. Stablecoin issuers are now structural buyers of short-dated government debt; Standard Chartered projects the market reaching $2 trillion by end-2028 and roughly $1 trillion of fresh Treasury bill demand (CoinDesk, 23/02/2026). Each converted dollar shifts from private lending toward government financing.

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in two stages. Through 2026-2027 the drift is incremental, masked by a stalled market: stablecoin supply has hovered near $300 billion since late 2025, so the credit effect stays inside the statistical noise. The inflection comes if the market resumes scaling toward the $1-2 trillion projections; the conversion then turns macro-visible, as a growing slice of the money stock becomes fully-reserved "narrow" money financing Treasury bills rather than loans.

Three stresses concentrate. Funding: deposit-poor banks face higher, more wholesale-dependent funding costs. Distribution: the burden lands unevenly, with community and regional banks — whose franchises rest on relationship lending — most exposed; one industry estimate puts potential community-bank losses at $1.3 trillion of deposits and $850 billion of loans (Brookings Institution, 03/03/2026). Enforcement: the GENIUS Act's yield ban, meant as the firewall, is already porous, failing to reach the affiliate and exchange "rewards" arrangements that replicate yield (White House Council of Economic Advisers, 08/04/2026). Two adaptations follow: banks counter with tokenized deposits, bank money on a blockchain that stays inside the fractional-reserve system (Brookings Institution, 03/03/2026); and the BIS expects more credit to migrate to non-bank financial institutions, making lending more procyclical (Bank for International Settlements, 20/04/2026).

Why This Matters

For bank boards, treasurers and regulators, the error is to file stablecoins under "payments strategy" and leave the response to a digital-assets team. The signal is a balance-sheet and credit-supply question. A bank that loses low-cost deposits loses lending capacity, not just a product line — and even one that wins the flows must hold them as reserves rather than loans. The decision architecture to revise treats deposit funding as permanently stable. Regional and community banks should stress-test funding mixes against deposit migration now; regulators should ask whether a payments system drifting toward narrow banking still finances small business at scale.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the conversion mechanism is operating and now evidenced, but its materiality depends on whether the stablecoin market resumes scaling from its stalled $300 billion, leaving a window to model funding and credit exposure before the inflection.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that the credit effect is a rounding error. The CEA models the disintermediation channel as trivial at current scale, and Bruegel argues the fear is "overblown" in principle: stablecoin issuance does not reduce total banking-system deposits, since issuers hold bank deposits or buy securities from counterparties who do, and a central bank can recycle displaced reserves to credit-creating institutions (Bruegel, 20/05/2026). Stablecoin growth has, in any case, stalled near $300 billion since late 2025.

But the equilibrium case concedes the point that matters: frictionless recycling changes who lends, not whether lending happens, and the BIS accepts that the distribution of deposits and credit shifts toward non-bank intermediaries. The signal is not that credit vanishes, but that it migrates out of relationship banks — where the New York Fed already measures it.

Implications

This is durable structural change to the architecture of money, not a transient policy episode. The inflection window is 2026-2028, set by whether the stablecoin market resumes scaling. And the design is a choice: through MiCA, the EU requires issuers to park a large share of reserves back in banks, while the GENIUS Act does not (Bruegel, 20/05/2026). Jurisdictions are quietly deciding how much credit creation to route through banks versus markets; those positioning early shape the outcome, the rest inherit a credit system reshaped by default.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • Stablecoin supply climbing back above its late-2025 plateau of roughly $300-320 billion.
  • Federal Reserve H.8 or call-report data showing loan-to-asset ratios falling at stablecoin-linked banks.
  • Regional and community banks naming stablecoin deposit outflows as a funding risk in filings.
  • The OCC or Congress moving to close the GENIUS Act affiliate and exchange yield "rewards" loophole.
  • The US Treasury lifting the bill share of issuance with explicit reference to stablecoin reserve demand.

Disconfirming Signals

  • Stablecoin supply staying flat or shrinking through 2026-2027, leaving the mechanism sub-scale.
  • Tokenized deposits capturing most digital-money growth, keeping balances inside the fractional-reserve system.
  • Loan-to-asset ratios at stablecoin-linked banks tracking peers at scale, contradicting the New York Fed finding.
  • Central banks visibly recycling displaced reserves to credit-creating institutions with no measurable lending drop.
  • Stablecoin use staying confined to crypto trading and cross-border niches rather than transactional deposits.

Strategic Questions

  • Should regional banks treat low-cost deposit funding as a structural liability now, or wait for outflows to appear?
  • At what stablecoin market size does deposit migration move this signal from Prepare to Decide?
  • Should regulators steer digital money toward tokenized deposits, or accept a drift toward narrow banking?
  • If credit migrates to non-bank lenders, who underwrites small business through the next downturn?
