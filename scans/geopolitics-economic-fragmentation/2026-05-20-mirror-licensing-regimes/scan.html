Suspended, Not Repealed: How Mirror Export-Licensing Regimes Became the Durable Instrument of Economic Fragmentation

Beneath the US-China tariff truce, the real instrument of economic statecraft has shifted from negotiable tariffs to extraterritorial export licensing — an architecture suspended in late 2025 but built to outlast any deal, exposing every globally-traded manufacturer through 2026-2028.

The consensus reading of US-China economic relations in 2026 is de-escalation: a tariff truce struck at Busan, rare-earth shipments resuming, headline tariff rates trimmed. Beneath that calm a quieter, more durable shift has taken hold. The instrument of economic statecraft has moved from the tariff — blunt, reciprocal, negotiable — to the export licence: surgical, extraterritorial, and now mirrored by both powers. China's late-2025 rare-earth escalation was suspended, not repealed; in April 2026 Beijing went further, codifying a permanent supply-chain and counter-extraterritoriality framework. The board question is no longer what the tariff rate will be, but whose licence a product needs.

Signal Identification

This is a regulatory pivot in the instruments of economic statecraft. The signal is not that the US and China are decoupling — that is the consensus — but that they have built mirror-image export-control regimes, each with extraterritorial reach, content-threshold rules and entity blacklists. Tariffs can be traded away at a summit; a codified licensing architecture persists. The result is a dual permission layer settling over global manufacturing.

Time horizon: 2-4 years (suspended controls scheduled to return 10 November 2026; structural entrenchment 2026-2028) Plausibility band: High Geographic / Jurisdictional Scope: Primary: United States and China. Spillover: the EU, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and the Netherlands, and every multinational exposed to both markets. Sectors exposed: Rare-earth and critical-mineral users (automotive, aerospace, defence, semiconductors, electronics, clean-tech, magnets); AI-hardware firms; trade-compliance, legal, procurement and treasury functions; insurers.

What's Changing

Export controls have crossed from a narrow non-proliferation tool into the central instrument of economic statecraft, deployed unilaterally and traded in negotiations; historically they were "never treated as bargaining chips" (CSIS, 10/04/2026). The escalation is documented: after the April 2025 US tariffs China placed medium and heavy rare earths on its control list; the US answered on chip-design software; a June truce gave way to a September US affiliates rule, met two weeks later by China's extraterritorial rare-earth regime.

What is new is the symmetry. China's system now mirrors the US model — end-user blacklists, an Unreliable Entity List and explicit extraterritorial application reaching foreign firms that re-export Chinese-origin items (SIPRI, 29/04/2026). In April 2026 Beijing went further: State Council regulations on industrial and supply-chain security (7 April) and on countering "improper" foreign extraterritorial jurisdiction (13 April), both effective immediately, created a unified retaliatory framework and a "malicious entity list" (Mayer Brown, 05/05/2026). The conduct most likely to trigger Chinese countermeasures is precisely what US, UK and EU law requires.

The leverage is real because supply is concentrated. Export restrictions now cover roughly 45% of global rare-earth exports, 47% of graphite and around 70% of cobalt and manganese, and have risen fivefold since 2009 (OECD, April 2026). China produces about 70% of rare earths and graphite and over 90% of germanium and magnesium, so a licence requirement on a few inputs reaches deep into automotive, aerospace, defence and semiconductor supply chains.

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in three stages. Through 2026 the escalation sits dormant: China's de minimis, foreign-direct-product and 50% rules and the US affiliates rule are suspended until 10 November 2026 (CSIS, 10/04/2026). Stage two is the reactivation window in late 2026, when the suspension lapses and either side can re-arm simply by inaction — supply concentration having barely shifted in the interim (Mining Technology, 27/04/2026). Stage three, 2027-2028, is entrenchment: dual licensing becomes a permanent compliance layer and firms split production into Chinese-origin-free or US-technology-free lines.

Three stresses dominate. Compliance conflict: routine adherence to US export controls can itself trigger a Chinese investigation or malicious-entity listing, with personal exposure for executives and advisers (Mayer Brown, 05/05/2026). Throughput: a 2026 survey found most semiconductor firms facing licence reviews averaging over 180 days (CSIS, 10/04/2026). Credibility: when controls are traded as bargaining chips, allies hesitate to align. Two adaptations follow. Operationally, trade-compliance rises from a back-office function to a board-level risk owning supply-chain bifurcation. Strategically, firms and governments pursue de-risking by design — qualifying non-Chinese rare-earth sources and non-US toolchains even at higher cost.

Why This Matters

For boards, the era when trade exposure could be modelled as a tariff line is over. The binding variable is licensing: which permits a product needs, from which government, and how long they take. A firm sourcing rare-earth magnets or selling advanced chips sits inside two overlapping, sometimes contradictory licensing regimes, where compliance with one can breach the other. The decision-architecture that needs revision is the one that still treats the US-China relationship as a tariff negotiation with a settlement at the end. Procurement, legal, treasury and M&A should be stress-tested against a dual-licensing world, and supply-chain maps should resolve Chinese-origin content to the 0.1% level the rules now reach.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the architecture is codified in both capitals and the 10 November 2026 reactivation is a named trigger; firms should build dual-compliance capability and qualified alternative supply now, and commit fully if the suspension lapses.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that the licensing weapon is itself a bargaining chip, not durable architecture. The October 2025 escalation was suspended within weeks, rare-earth general licences resumed, and tariffs still govern most trade — 72% of world trade still moves on most-favoured-nation terms (WTO, 19/03/2026). Enforcement is porous, too: a USD 2.5 billion chip-smuggling operation exposed in March 2026 showed how readily controls are circumvented (FDD, 20/03/2026).

Both points cut the other way. Suspension is not repeal — the rules stand with a reactivation date, and in the interim China codified a broader framework, so the architecture only grew. Porous enforcement does not retire the instrument; it invites more intrusive licensing, end-user verification and extraterritorial reach. A leaky, switchable weapon is still a weapon firms must hold capability against. The tariff debate has a settlement; the licence does not.

Implications

Taken together, the sources suggest durable structural change in how economic power is exercised. A tariff is a price; a licence is a permission — and permissions, once institutionalised with extraterritorial reach, become standing infrastructure that outlasts any administration or truce. The inflection window is tight, with 10 November 2026 the first hard test. Firms positioned to gain build dual-compliance capability and alternative supply early; exposed are those who read the truce as a settlement and find, when the suspension lapses, the architecture was never dismantled.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • China's suspended de minimis, foreign-direct-product and 50% rules lapse back into force on or after 10 November 2026.
  • Beijing or Washington lists a major multinational for complying with the other power's sanctions or export controls.
  • A surge in dual-compliance and trade-control hiring across manufacturers and semiconductor firms.
  • MOFCOM or BIS licence-review times lengthen further, with documented lost contracts or delayed shipments.
  • A third economy — the EU, Japan or South Korea — adopts its own extraterritorial export-control or anti-coercion instrument.

Disconfirming Signals

  • A US-China summit produces a binding agreement to repeal — not merely suspend — the extraterritorial licensing rules.
  • China withdraws or materially softens its April 2026 supply-chain and counter-extraterritoriality regulations.
  • The 10 November 2026 reactivation date passes without the suspended controls returning.
  • Rare-earth and critical-mineral export restrictions measurably decline as supply diversifies.
  • Both powers return export controls to a narrow, multilateral non-proliferation footing.

Strategic Questions

  • Should the firm map Chinese-origin content to the 0.1% threshold now, or wait to see if the November 2026 controls return?
  • When does running separate US-compliant and China-compliant production lines beat serving both markets from one?
  • Who on the board owns the risk that complying with one government's law breaches another's?
  • Should procurement pay the premium for qualified non-Chinese inputs now, or absorb the licensing risk?
