Preemption by Other Means: How "Managed Federalism" Is Quietly Reshaping Who Sets AI Rules

The federal push against state AI laws reads as a courtroom fight Washington will win or lose. The deeper signal is "managed federalism": the executive branch is chilling state AI lawmaking through funding leverage and litigation, leaving a contested patchwork, not a national standard.

The consensus on US AI regulation has a tidy storyline: a December 2025 executive order set out to sweep away the patchwork of state AI laws, and the open question is whether it survives the courts and whether Congress passes a federal standard. Beneath that binary sits a quieter development: the administration has largely stopped trying to win the preemption argument and started routing around it — using conditional funding, a litigation task force and agency signalling to discourage state AI lawmaking without striking it down. The signal is a new mode of governance, not a new law; for boards, the question is no longer when a national standard arrives, but how to operate while it does not.

Signal Identification

This is a regulatory-method shift, not a single policy. The consensus tracks the legal contest over preemption; the signal is that the federal government is reshaping where AI lawmaking happens through leverage rather than statute. Lawfare calls the emerging mode "managed federalism": state authority is preserved in theory but constrained in practice. The result is not a national rulebook but a chilled, contested patchwork, and a toolkit transferable beyond AI.

What's Changing

The December 2025 executive order is widely called a preemption order, yet it preempts nothing. It directs the Department of Justice to create an AI Litigation Task Force against state AI laws, tells Commerce to identify "onerous" state laws and bar those states from remaining federal broadband (BEAD) funds, and tells agencies to weigh conditioning other grants on non-enforcement (The White House, 11/12/2025). A March 2026 follow-on framework recommends six legislative objectives to Congress. Neither instrument is binding law.

That is the design. An executive order "has no preemptive power whatsoever" — it is not a law — and each pathway it opens "faces serious legal obstacles"; the order is "designed to intimidate" (Brookings Institution, 16/04/2026). The instrument is leverage, not statute. Lawfare names the mode "managed federalism": the executive branch structures the incentives under which states legislate, and AI bills in Utah, Missouri and Louisiana have stalled or narrowed after federal pressure, with no law struck down (Lawfare, 04/05/2026).

Colorado shows the pattern in miniature. After xAI sued and the DOJ intervened — the first federal move to invalidate a state AI law (US Department of Justice, 24/04/2026) — a magistrate judge stayed Colorado's AI Act on 27 April 2026, weeks before its 30 June effective date, while the legislature negotiates a narrower replacement (The National Law Review, 01/05/2026). No court ruled on preemption; the law was simply frozen.

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in two stages. Through 2026-2027 the campaign operates by deterrence: the Commerce evaluation, FTC and FCC proceedings and DOJ litigation raise the cost and uncertainty of state action, so legislatures slow, narrow or shelve AI bills even as the raw count of state proposals keeps climbing. The inflection turns on Congress: if it codifies the framework, the US gets a genuine, if contested, national standard; if it does not, "managed federalism" becomes the durable regime, with state law formally available but practically discouraged and the operative constraints being funding conditions, litigation exposure and federal signalling rather than statutes.

Three stresses concentrate. Compliance: the patchwork is neither swept away nor stable, so "wait for the federal standard" becomes an expensive bet. Constitutional: conditioning broadband money on AI-law repeal invites challenge under the coercive-spending limits the Supreme Court set in NFIB v. Sebelius, leaving the regime legally fragile (Lawfare, 04/05/2026). Strategic: regulatory fragmentation has moved from compliance footnote to enterprise risk shaping where firms build and how they govern AI (KPMG, 18/03/2026). Two adaptations follow: firms default to a highest-common-denominator standard across states, and the leverage toolkit becomes a template other administrations and domains reuse.

Why This Matters

For boards, compliance and government-affairs leaders, and investors, the error is to treat this as a Washington spectacle to watch for a verdict. There may be no verdict. The decision architecture that needs revising assumes a binary — patchwork now, federal standard later — and waits. "Managed federalism" offers neither: a patchwork that is chilled, uneven and contested, plus a federal overlay of funding conditions and litigation risk that is itself unsettled. Companies should plan for that hybrid as the base case through 2027. State legislatures should weigh whether the authority they still formally hold is worth exercising under the shadow of federal pressure.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — the mechanism is operating, but whether it hardens into the durable US regime or is overtaken by legislation or a court ruling resolves across 2026-2027, leaving a window to plan around a contested patchwork rather than an awaited federal standard.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that "managed federalism" is a way station, not a destination. The March framework is a set of recommendations to Congress, and the administration says it "looks forward to working with Congress... to turn this framework into legislation" (The White House, 20/03/2026); a preemption statute would replace leverage with a real standard. Equally, courts could strike the coercive-funding mechanism, collapsing the model to a pure state patchwork.

Both are possible — but both are slow and contested, and neither changes the operating environment now. Congress has repeatedly declined preemption, and litigation moves in years. For the planning horizon, managed federalism is the regime in force whichever way the endgame breaks; and the deeper shift, governing by leverage rather than statute, holds regardless.

Implications

This is a structural change in how regulatory authority is allocated, not a transient episode — and it runs past AI. The inflection window is 2026-2027, set by Congress and the courts. If "managed federalism" holds, contested rules become the steady state, and the advantage shifts to organisations that can operate amid fragmentation rather than those awaiting clarity. The model is also a precedent: the coordination-and-leverage toolkit is not technology-specific, and whoever holds the executive branch can apply it to any contested domain where Congress has not acted (Lawfare, 04/05/2026).

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • Publication of the overdue Commerce evaluation of state AI laws, naming specific "onerous" statutes.
  • A Commerce BEAD Policy Notice tying broadband funding eligibility to state AI-law repeal.
  • An FTC policy statement or FCC proceeding declaring categories of state AI law preempted.
  • Further DOJ AI Litigation Task Force suits against state AI laws beyond Colorado.
  • More states narrowing, delaying or shelving AI bills citing federal funding or litigation risk.

Disconfirming Signals

  • Congress passing a comprehensive federal AI statute with explicit preemption.
  • A federal court striking the order's conditional-funding mechanism as unconstitutionally coercive.
  • States enacting and enforcing substantive AI laws at pace, undeterred by federal pressure.
  • The DOJ AI Litigation Task Force losing its early cases on the merits.
  • The administration quietly dropping the funding-leverage conditions.

Strategic Questions

  • Should companies plan for a contested patchwork as the base case through 2027, or keep waiting for a federal standard?
  • When does federal funding-leverage pressure make uniform internal AI compliance the cheaper option?
  • Should states exercise the AI-regulating authority they formally retain, or defer under federal pressure?
  • If governing-by-leverage becomes routine, which regulated domains should boards expect it in next?
