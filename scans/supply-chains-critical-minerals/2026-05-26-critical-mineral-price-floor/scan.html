From Diversify to Underwrite: The $110/kg Pivot in Western Critical-Minerals Policy

The critical-minerals consensus is about diversifying supply away from China. Beneath it, the West is pivoting to administered prices, with a Pentagon $110/kg NdPr floor now mirrored in a Lynas-Japan offtake through 2038 and US-led plurilateral price-floor talks opening with the EU and Japan.

The consensus on critical minerals is a story of supply: build mines and refineries outside China fast enough to break the chokehold. Beneath that sits a quieter shift. With Chinese oversupply pushing lithium, nickel and rare-earth prices below most Western producers' break-even cost, the United States has stopped trying to compete on quantity and started trying to set the price. The $110/kg neodymium-praseodymium floor the Pentagon gave MP Materials in July 2025 has, in eight months, migrated into a Lynas-Japan offtake through 2038, a January 2026 Section 232 proclamation contemplating minimum import prices, and a USTR plurilateral consultation with the EU and Japan. The policy instrument is changing — from diversification to subsidy.

Signal Identification

This is a regulatory and market-design pivot, not just another industrial-policy package. The consensus tracks supply diversification; the signal is that the binding instrument is now price, and the architecture is moving from project-by-project floors to a multilateral framework. Engineered Chinese oversupply, not scarcity, is the operative risk.

Time horizon: Near-term to 5 years (mechanisms operating now; multilateral floor architecture and Western benchmarks emerging 2026-2030) Plausibility band: Medium–High Geographic / Jurisdictional Scope: Primary: the United States, Australia and Japan as the leading anchor of the $110/kg NdPr floor. Spillover: EU, Canada, Saudi Arabia, Korea and the global commodity-exchange and price-reporting ecosystem. Sectors exposed: Critical-minerals miners and processors; battery, EV and defense manufacturers; commodity exchanges and price-reporting agencies; sovereign and bilateral development finance; corporate offtake counterparties.

What's Changing

The architecture is now explicit. The 15 January 2026 executive order, Adjusting Imports of Processed Critical Minerals, frames price volatility as a structural national-security vulnerability and directs Commerce and the US Trade Representative to consider price-support mechanisms — including price floors — in negotiated trade agreements (Center for Strategic and International Studies, 15/01/2026). It is the policy companion to the Pentagon's $110/kg NdPr floor for MP Materials, signed in July 2025 with a roughly 15% federal equity stake; in February 2026 NdPr prices rallied to roughly $123/kg, sparing US taxpayers a subsidy outlay while leaving the bankability mechanism in place (Mining.com, 18/02/2026).

The number has now travelled. On 10 March 2026 Lynas Rare Earths revised its Japan supply deal through Japan Australia Rare Earths, committing to 5,000 tonnes of NdPr a year at a $110/kg floor until 2038 and 50% of Lynas's heavy rare-earth output (Mining.com, 11/03/2026). The same window opened a USTR public consultation on a plurilateral agreement asking how minimum prices, reference prices and border measures could make Western supply bankable, with EU and Japan negotiations targeted for April (InvestorNews, 15/03/2026).

This sits inside a wider repositioning: the Office of Strategic Capital's mandate has grown from about $1bn to $200bn, a January 2026 bipartisan $2.5bn Strategic Resilience Reserve is designed to pay above market prices and anchor a Western benchmark, and bilateral pacts with Uzbekistan, Saudi Arabia, Japan and Korea Zinc on smelting extend the architecture (FTI Consulting, 13/02/2026). The contrast with Europe sharpens the signal: the European Court of Auditors finds the EU's 2030 Critical Raw Materials Act targets appear out of reach, diversification is not producing tangible results, and 7 of the EU's 14 raw-materials partnerships are with countries of limited stability (European Court of Auditors, 02/02/2026).

Disruption Pathway

The pathway runs in two stages. Through 2026-2027 the architecture extends bilaterally: more $110/kg-anchored offtakes, the USTR plurilateral moving into negotiation, and Pentagon offtakes for the 13 critical minerals it solicited in February. From 2027 the deeper shift lands: CME and ICE rare-earth futures mature, Benchmark Mineral Intelligence builds a non-China price-discovery layer, and the floor references Western benchmarks rather than the Chinese ex-works index it currently anchors on. The inflection arrives when a Western benchmark replaces Asian Metal in a major offtake.

Three pressure points concentrate the stress. Fiscally, floors and stockpiles are expensive contingent liabilities; the $2.5bn Strategic Resilience Reserve is the visible line item. Diplomatically, China retains the lever to flood or restrict any non-listed mineral and to use its processing dominance and 1998 Pricing Law to keep Chinese benchmarks the reference. Industrially, Western projects face permitting, cost-overrun and execution risk (FTI Consulting, 13/02/2026). Two adaptations follow: governments build Western pricing infrastructure (exchanges, benchmarks, transparent reporting), and multilateralise the floors.

Why This Matters

For boards in mining, processing and downstream manufacturing, for sovereign and infrastructure investors, and for defense procurement, the error is to read the 2026 deals as one-off project subsidies. They are early instances of a structural change in how strategic-mineral markets clear. Project finance built on the assumption that Chinese pricing sets the cash-flow curve will under-value Western capacity that floors make bankable and over-value Chinese capacity whose pricing power is being diluted.

Decision-action posture for this signal: Prepare — bilateral floors are operating and the plurilateral framework opens negotiation in April 2026, with the architecture maturing across 2026-2030.

Counter-Argument

The strongest objection is that price floors do not free the West from Chinese price discovery. The MP Materials deal currently uses Asian Metal's ex-works China NdPr index as its reference; Asian Metal and Shanghai Metal Market operate within China's 1998 Pricing Law, which a US Select Committee report says effectively bars publishing prices that deviate from PRC wishes (Mining.com, 21/02/2026). Until Western exchanges and benchmark agencies build a non-China pricing layer, the floor sits on a number China sets.

That objection mostly sharpens the signal rather than refutes it. The Lynas-Japan deal is precisely an attempt to start writing the next reference price, and CME lithium-hydroxide volumes grew 37% year-on-year in 2025 as Western buyers sought alternatives to Chinese pricing. The floor is the bridge; the Western pricing layer is the destination.

Implications

This is a structural change in commodity-market governance, not a transient industrial-policy splurge. The inflection window is 2026-2030, set by how fast bilateral floors become multilateral and how fast Western pricing infrastructure scales. Advantage tilts to Western producers with credible floor coverage and to exchanges taking share from Asian Metal (InvestorNews, 22/02/2026). Critical minerals stop being a quantity to procure and become a market to administer.

Early Indicators to Monitor

  • A USTR plurilateral agreement with the EU, Japan or both announcing minimum reference prices and border measures for one or more critical minerals.
  • A second major bilateral offtake mirroring the $110/kg NdPr floor (e.g. Australia-Korea, US-Canada, EU-Saudi Arabia).
  • A CME or ICE rare-earth futures contract launching with sustained traded volumes.
  • A Pentagon or DoE-backed offtake or floor for cobalt, gallium, tungsten or antimony at named price levels.
  • An offtake using a non-China rare-earth benchmark instead of Asian Metal or SMM.

Disconfirming Signals

  • The USTR consultation closing without a negotiated price-floor framework, with the EU and Japan declining to sign.
  • A Lynas-style $110/kg deal collapsing or being renegotiated downward as floor commitments become liabilities.
  • Chinese rare-earth and lithium prices rallying for a sustained period, reducing the bankability case for floors.
  • US Congress refusing to fund the $2.5bn Strategic Resilience Reserve or pulling back Pentagon equity authority.
  • Western producers receiving floor support but failing to reach commercial scale at competitive cost.

Strategic Questions

  • Should mining-project investors price the $110/kg floor as a permanent feature or a 2025-2028 transitional one?
  • When does a Western rare-earth benchmark become liquid enough to displace Asian Metal as the offtake reference?
  • Should downstream OEMs lock in long-dated, floor-anchored offtakes now, or wait for the multilateral framework?
  • At what fiscal threshold do floors stop being insurance and start being unaffordable underwriting?
