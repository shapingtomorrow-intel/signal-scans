The Decision Intelligence briefing says what is changing as AI agents spread faster than the controls meant to contain them. This System Map shows why the pieces move together: which changes feed each other, which push back, and where an organisation’s effort changes how the whole system behaves rather than one part of it.

Read it by following the arrows. A + means the two move in the same direction; a − means they move in opposite directions. Where the arrows close into a circle, they form a loop: an R loop reinforces itself, so change feeds on change, and a B loop balances, pushing back against change. 2. The five loops this quarter

Reinforcing loops run in both directions: the same circle that compounds decline would compound recovery. The status on each card is this cycle’s reading, and is what the monthly Change Tracker updates. Turning means every step in the loop has current evidence, so it is running now; Priming means the parts are in place but the circle has not yet closed at scale. Hover over a status for its meaning.

Turning

R1 Debt needs quick returns

AI-related debt issuance reached around $450 billion by early September, more than double all of 2025 (Bank of England, Sep 2026), and 65% of chief executives will spend at least 10% of their capital budget on AI next year (KPMG, Oct 2026). The loop is turning: every step has current evidence.

Watch: AI debt issuance in the next Bank of England record, and whether the largest builders keep turning to shares.

Turning

B1 Incidents bring in gates

The White House asked laboratories to hold models from British testers after incidents in which AI systems reached real-world systems (Reuters, Sep 2026), and OpenAI paused training of its most capable models (OpenAI, Oct 2026).

Watch: a US rule or agreement making review a condition of release, and the EU AI Office's first enforcement.

Turning

B2 The grid sets the pace

Around 73GW of Great Britain's demand connection queue is data centres, against peak demand of 45GW (Ofgem, Jul 2026), and a Dutch court backed a refusal to connect a data centre (Solar Magazine, in Dutch, May 2026).

Watch: Ofgem's decision on the connection fee, and the US regional grid operators' new rules for large loads.

Priming

R2 Fewer juniors learn the job

Employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed US occupations is about 19% below trend (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026), but the thinner pool of experienced staff will show only after a delay, so the circle has not yet closed.

Watch: the Stanford gap and graduate hiring surveys, and whether employers report shortages of staff with three to five years' experience.

Priming

B3 Incidents force controls

The UK AI Security Institute now treats internet access as something to be justified (UK AI Security Institute, Aug 2026), but in companies the share building controls into agents fell to 30% from 43% (KPMG, Sep 2026), so the loop is not yet closing outside the laboratories.

Watch: KPMG's next quarterly reading on controls, and insurers' terms for agents.

What the map shows that the briefing alone cannot
  1. Borrowed money is the hidden accelerator. Loop R1 (debt needs quick returns) is the only reinforcing loop already turning, and it runs straight into agent deployment. The pressure to earn back borrowed money is one reason deployment is outrunning controls, which is why financing and agent security belong in the same conversation.
  2. Two brakes act on agents, and only one is working. Loop B1 (incidents bring in gates) is turning: governments respond to incidents. Loop B3 (incidents force controls) is still priming: inside companies, controls are falling while deployment rises. Until B3 closes, the brake on agents is applied from outside the organisation rather than within it.
  3. The jobs loop has a delay built in. Loop R2 (fewer juniors learn the job) shows first in hiring data and only years later as a shortage of experienced staff. By the time the shortage is visible, the decisions that caused it were made several budget rounds earlier.

3. Four leverage points

Each point is numbered on the map, and each ties to a Strategic Implication already in the briefing, so the map changes the reasoning behind the plan rather than adding a second plan.

  1. 1

    Limit and watch agent access

    Controls built into agents is where loop B3 (incidents force controls) turns. Building them before an incident, rather than after, is the one lever that also slows the link from deployment to incidents.

    DecideBriefing Implication 1: list every agent with internet or tool access and deny access unless justified and monitored, by 31 March 2027

  2. 2

    Prepare for three gates

    Government gates are where loop B1 (incidents bring in gates) acts on an organisation. Keeping the evidence each government asks for turns a gate from a stop into a check.

    PrepareBriefing Implication 2: map each AI system and supplier against US, Chinese and EU duties by 30 June 2027

  3. 3

    Fund in stages

    The pressure to show returns is where loop R1 (debt needs quick returns) pushes agents into use. Staged funding against measured payback slows that push without stopping useful work.

    DecideBriefing Implication 3: a staged payback test and a correction stress test in the 2027 budget round, by 31 March 2027

  4. 4

    Keep hiring juniors

    Young people hired is the step where loop R2 (fewer juniors learn the job) can be broken: a redesigned junior role keeps the pipeline of experienced staff that agents will need supervising them.

    PrepareBriefing Implication 4: a redesigned junior role for each entry route, and the 2027 intake, by 30 June 2027

4. How the map explains the scenarios

The two axes of the briefing’s Scenario Matrix 2030 are the map’s two dominant uncertainties: whether the brakes on agents work, loops B1 and B3, which decides whether the risk of agents falls on deployers or on those affected, and how far loop R1 runs, which decides whether the build-out is paid for with borrowed money or from earnings. The Scenario Stress Test tracks which of the four is gaining momentum.

Build-out paid from: borrowed money → earnings
Earnings · deployers carry risk

Licensed Agents

B1 and B3 both work and R1 is weak: agents run under licence and insurance, and the build-out grows at the pace earnings allow.
Earnings · others carry risk

The Unwritten Bill

R1 is weak but B3 never closes: cash-rich firms deploy widely and incidents are settled later in court.
Borrowed money · deployers carry risk

The Slow Payback

R1 runs while B1 and B3 slow deployment: the build-out waits for careful adoption to pay back its debt.
Borrowed money · others carry risk

The Leveraged Race

R1 runs and the brakes fail: debt pushes agents into wide use, and incidents and a correction can arrive together.
Agent risk falls on: deployers → those affected

Read this way, the scenarios stop being four stories and become four states of the same system, each with early indicators already on the map.

6. How the map stays live

A map drawn once goes stale as fast as the plans it informs. Inside a continuous intelligence cycle, every format updates it:

7. How an organisation would use it

The value of a System Map comes as much from arguing over the arrows as from the finished picture. This draft is a starting point:

Challenge sessionOne facilitated session of about 90 minutes in which technology, finance, risk and HR leaders test every arrow, add what the evidence missed and agree the leverage points. Their corrections become part of the map.
Business-unit lensesThe same structure, shaded by business unit: the loops are shared, but their strength differs, so a unit that runs many agents and one that mostly buys AI services can sit on one map.
Other topicsCyber risk, data centre sourcing or the graduate pipeline can be mapped the same way, so leaders see one method across the portfolio.
