Decision Intelligence Preview · ALIANZA TEAM · RELOCATING THE R&D LABORATORIES

The Lab Is Becoming Software With a Loading Dock

For Angela Guevara and the R&D team at Alianza Team, the question looks like where to put the next laboratory. This Preview reframes it: as automation, artificial intelligence and cloud science remake the laboratory itself, the relocation becomes a decision about what kind of research capability the company wants to own, not simply where to own it.

Prepared by Shaping Tomorrow · 2 July 2026 · Read time ~5 minutes

1. What we’re seeing

The signals behind the Alianza Team lab decision

The evidence falls into three groups: how the laboratory itself is changing, where scientific talent and value are concentrating, and how much of a laboratory can now be rented rather than built. What follows is observation, not conclusion; the reading comes in the next section. Angela Guevara's bench sits exactly where these three currents meet.

Alianza Team has already moved. Its new $36.4 million functional-fats plant in Goldsboro, North Carolina opened in mid-March 2026, its first US site, placed for rapid delivery to the Northeastern customers it mainly serves (FoodNavigator-USA, April 2026).
The laboratory is becoming a closed loop. Self-driving laboratories that pair robotics with AI have moved from concept to national-scale deployment, linking discovery directly to production, so the AI designs the experiment, robots run it, instruments read the result and the model updates before the next run (RSC Digital Discovery, March 2026).
Autonomy is fast and cheap on data. Automated labs now run hundreds of experiments a day, and a catalyst search that would conventionally take a century was compressed to weeks, turning what were months of formulation trials into days (AgFunderNews, February 2026).
AI is becoming the core of food R&D. McKinsey tells food and beverage leaders to fully harness technology and AI across R&D or cede ground as value erosion accelerates (McKinsey, April 2026).
Colombia's own science base is maturing. Medellin holds the country's largest single share of science-based firms, in sectors including agrifood and AI, with Ruta N financing applied research, a reminder that the home option is a live, financed alternative rather than a fallback (Ruta N, April 2026).
Much of the lab can now be rented. Cloud labs and regional contract-development organisations let firms run serious experiments remotely and localise development to the market context, which reframes part of the relocation as a build-versus-access decision rather than only a where-to-build one (AgFunderNews, February 2026).
The lipid frontier is capital-bound, not idea-bound. Precision-fermentation fats face capital, supply and regulatory hurdles to reach scale, pushing incumbents toward partnership rather than solo discovery; for a fats and oils company that frontier is not adjacent but the next competitive battleground (FoodNavigator-USA, March 2026).
2. Why it matters

Three variables, two horizons, one operating-model choice

The conventional reading treats relocation as a real-estate and logistics question: find a site with good access to customers and plants, at an acceptable cost, and move the benches there. On that reading, Alianza Team's North Carolina opening is simply a well-judged expansion closer to its Northeastern market, while the home laboratories continue much as before. For a mature lipids business it is a reasonable frame, and it is not wrong so much as incomplete: it prices the building and the logistics, but not the changing nature of the work the building is meant to house.

Four operating models for the relocated lab

Analyst positioning of R&D operating models on ownership and automation, drawing on the self-driving-lab evidence (RSC Digital Discovery, March 2026; AgFunderNews, February 2026) and Alianza Team's siting (FoodNavigator-USA, April 2026). Placement is analyst judgement, not measured data.

The closer reading is that all three signal groups point at the same shift: the laboratory is ceasing to be a fixed stock of benches and becoming a configurable capability. Closed-loop automation changes what happens inside it (RSC Digital Discovery, March 2026); AI changes what the work itself is (McKinsey, April 2026); and cloud labs and regional contract-development organisations change how much of it has to be owned at all (AgFunderNews, February 2026). Seen this way, choosing the building before choosing the operating model is choosing the answer before the question. The address still matters, but it sits downstream of the capability the company decides to hold.

The binding risk the plan should hold as primary is capability lock-in. A relocation specified as a conventional bench fit-out, however well-sited, quietly caps what the laboratory can later become, because automation, data infrastructure and green-modular design are far cheaper to build in than to retrofit; reworking a running laboratory for automation is disruptive and costly, which is why the fit-out is the cheapest moment to decide. For a lipids specialist the exposure is sharpened by the alternative-fat frontier, where the constraint is scale-up capital and partnership rather than discovery (FoodNavigator-USA, March 2026); a laboratory built only for today's formulation work may be poorly placed to engage it tomorrow.

If this framing holds, the relocation plan changes shape. The first decision is not the city but the operating model: how much owned bench, how much owned automation, how much rented capability. The site brief then follows from that choice, and the question of a United States science cluster versus a maturing Colombian base becomes a talent-and-capability question rather than only a cost one. The relocation stops being a single site choice and becomes a portfolio choice across owned, automated and rented capacity.

3. Questions leaders should now be asking

The decisions the relocation plan now has to make explicit

  1. Before we choose a city, what split of owned bench, owned automation and rented capability do we want the new lab to run, and which function owns that decision?
  2. How much of our core lipid formulation are we willing to route through cloud labs or a regional CDMO, given the trade-off between cycle speed and keeping tacit know-how in-house?
  3. What would have to be true about food-formulation automation within two years for a bench-first North Carolina fit-out to look, in hindsight, like a strategic mistake?
  4. Does a maturing Colombian deep-tech base argue for anchoring more R&D at home, or does it mean competitors will soon contest the same talent we would be relying on?
  5. If precision-fermentation fats reach cost parity inside the horizon, is our new lab positioned to help us lead that shift, or only to watch it reach our margins?
  6. Which sustainability and modularity standards do we lock into the fit-out now, knowing they are far cheaper to design in than to retrofit once the lab is running?
  7. How do we sequence the North Carolina site and any home hub so that each builds a capability the other lacks, rather than duplicating benches on two continents?
