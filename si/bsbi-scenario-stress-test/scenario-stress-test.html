Which Moves Hold in Every Future for a Business School to 2030

30 October 2026 · Built on the Decision Intelligence briefing If AI Can Pass the Exam, What Does the Degree Prove? and its System Map · Horizons: 2030 and 2040 Uses the briefing’s scenarios and options, with evidence from the briefing; no other sources. Grades and indicator readings are drafted by analysis.

Scenarios are not forecasts. Their job is to show how differently the next few years could turn out, so that a strategy can be built to survive any of them, and then to be watched, so that the strategy changes when the evidence does.

This page does both. It takes the nine options in the briefing and runs each one through four plausible futures to 2030, plus a wild card to 2040. A move that works in every future is robust and can be committed to now. A move that works well in some futures and poorly in others is a bet, held ready with a clear trigger. A move that makes things worse in any future is fragile and should not be relied on. It then scores leading indicators on the matrix’s two axes, shows how that reading has moved since May, and turns the reading into priorities.

1. Four futures to 2030, and a wild card to 2040

The two axes are the system’s two biggest uncertainties, the same two the System Map identifies: whether employers or schools train new graduates, which is whether fewer first rungs outrun employers reshaping entry roles, and whether the degree or employers’ own tests vouch for skills, which is whether proven skills earn trust faster than the hiring arms race erodes it.

The degree vouches · employers train

Degrees Still Open Doors

Employers keep hiring graduates into reshaped entry roles and train them to supervise AI. Universities have redesigned assessment, so a degree once more shows what a graduate can do.

By 2040: Observed assessment is simply how degrees work, and employers treat a good school’s degree as proof of judgement.

The degree vouches · schools train

The School Is the First Job

Employers hire fewer juniors and expect graduates to arrive ready, but still trust degrees that prove it. Schools that build projects, placements and observed assessment into their programmes win.

By 2040: Degrees look more like apprenticeships with employers, and the school’s projects are where careers begin.

Employers’ tests decide · employers train

Hired on the Test

Employers still run graduate schemes, but decide on their own live assessments. A degree is a ticket to apply, not proof of skill.

By 2040: Employers’ assessment centres are the real exam, and schools compete on how well they prepare students for them.

Employers’ tests decide · schools train

The Missing Rung

Entry roles are scarce and employers hire on portfolios and their own tests. Graduates compete with AI for routine work, and international graduates struggle most.

By 2040: A generation enters work later and by other routes, and degrees that could not show outcomes have lost their students.

Wild card to 2040

The credential bypass

Large employers stop asking for degrees for entry roles and hire on their own tests and on credentials for supervising AI, so the degree becomes optional for a first job.

Built from two of the briefing’s Potential Surprises to 2040: large employers stop asking for degrees (2030 to 2036, Low-Medium) and supervising AI becomes a recognised profession with its own credentials (2030 to 2038, Low).

What the stress test shows
  1. There is a robust core, and it matches three of the briefing’s four Implications. An in-person part in every final-year module, teaching and grading AI supervision, and publishing outcomes with a clear AI policy hold up in all four futures.
  2. Tightening rules and detection is the one fragile move. Where employers are ready to trust degrees again, unreliable detection produces disputed cases that damage the degree’s standing at the worst moment.
  3. Each bet turns on one axis. Live projects and placements are strong where schools train and costly where employers still do; a full assessment rebuild is strong where the degree vouches and weak where employers’ tests decide; short programmes with employer-set assessments are the reverse.

3. What it means for the plan

Commit now

Robust: works in every future.

  • Add an in-person part to every final-year module, agreed with the partner universities (Implication 1).
  • Teach and grade AI supervision in every programme (Implication 2).
  • Publish outcomes and an AI policy, and review each partner’s finances every year (Implication 4).

Prepare, and act on a trigger

Bets: strong in some futures, weak in others.

  • Live employer projects and placements in every degree: pilot now, scale if the reading moves to the side where schools train (Implication 3).
  • A full rebuild of assessment around projects and defences: hold ready if the degree regains employers’ trust.
  • Short, stackable programmes with employer-set assessments: hold ready if employers’ tests come to decide.
  • AI tool training and marketing on AI content: acceptable only alongside the core.

Do not rely on

Fragile: backfires in at least one future.

  • Relying on tighter rules and detection to keep coursework credible.

4. Which future is gaining momentum, and how it has moved since May

Each future is a corner of the matrix, so the question “which future is gaining momentum” becomes two questions the evidence can answer: are employers or schools training new graduates, and is the degree or are employers’ own tests vouching for skills? Twelve leading indicators answer them, six on each axis. Scored at each reading, they give a reading on each axis and a point on the matrix; the trail of points shows where the evidence is heading.

The pale band (the no-lead zone) marks readings within 15 points of an axis, where no future can lead. Hover over a point for its reading.

Who vouches: moving towards employers’ tests−12 in October. Universities are moving to observed assessment and national bodies have set principles, but misconduct in recruitment is at a record and employers now write their own AI rules.
Who trains: back to the centre−4 in October. Employers’ plans to cut entry roles put the reading on the side where schools train by July; stable graduate hiring and a small measured AI effect since August pulled it back.
Leading future: none in OctoberThe July reading sat in the corner of The School Is the First Job, but a future leads only after two readings there, and the October reading moved back as hiring held and the arms race in recruitment grew.
What would settle itA second fall in graduate hiring, or a significant AI effect in German data, moves the reading towards The Missing Rung; national guidance requiring in-person assessment, adopted by partner universities, moves it towards Degrees Still Open Doors.
How the reading is scored
  1. Each indicator is marked Moving (+1), Pending (+½), Not yet (0) or Against (−1), on the evidence published by the date of the reading.
  2. Each carries a weight: 3 if it would settle its axis on its own, 2 if it is strong evidence, 1 if it supports.
  3. An axis reading is the weighted total for that axis, scaled from −100 (employers training, or employers’ tests deciding) to +100 (schools training, or the degree vouching).
  4. A future leads when the reading sits in its corner, at least 15 points from both axes, for two readings running.
  5. A shift is a move of 10 points or more on either axis between readings. It is flagged and reviewed in the next quarterly briefing.
  6. A signpost reached (section 6) overrides the score and triggers its decision directly.

7. How the monitoring runs over time

Every weekSignal Scans attach new evidence to each indicator and signpost, including weak signals that do not yet fit any future.
Every monthThe Change Tracker rescores each indicator, the two axis readings are recalculated, and the trail on the matrix gains a point.
Every quarterThe Decision Intelligence briefing reviews any flagged shift, and redraws the scenarios, the System Map and this stress test.
When a signpost is reachedAction Triggers fire, releasing the bet prepared for it and naming the decision it changes.

8. Running it with your own plan

The options above are the briefing’s. The real value comes from replacing them with the school’s own plans and asking the same question of each: does it hold in every future?

BeforeThe school shares its strategy, programme plans and partner arrangements for the next few years; we grade each against the scenarios and the evidence base.
Session, 2 hours20 minutes in the four futures; 40 minutes in which each group lives in one future and answers “it is 2030: what do we wish we had changed in 2026?”; 40 minutes testing the strategy against the matrix; 20 minutes agreeing the robust core and the trigger for each bet.
AfterThe agreed matrix, indicators and signposts join the programme, and the monitoring cycle above keeps them current.

The four futures, the nine options and the indicators all come from the briefing of 30 October 2026; the wild card combines two of its Potential Surprises to 2040; no new sources have been added.
