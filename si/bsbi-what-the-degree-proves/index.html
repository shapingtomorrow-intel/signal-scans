Why This Matters Now

Almost every university student now uses AI, and most use it for the work they are graded on. An essay or report written at home no longer shows, on its own, what the student can do. Germany’s science council has told universities to redesign their exams, and some Spanish universities are bringing back oral exams.

At the same time, employers are rethinking the routine tasks that used to fill a graduate’s first job. Some are cutting entry-level roles; most still expect to hire graduates, but want them to check and direct AI’s work rather than do what AI now does. So far the economy, more than AI, explains why young people find work harder to get.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: a business degree will be worth its fee when it can show, under observation and in real projects, that a graduate can judge and supervise AI’s work, so assessment and careers support need to change together.

Executive Synthesis

Which of the assessments your students sit this year could an AI tool complete for them?

The evidence here says 94% of UK undergraduates use generative AI for assessed work, and Germany’s science council now asks universities to redesign exam formats.

Students have adopted AI faster than any rule could follow: 94% of UK undergraduates use it for assessed work (Higher Education Policy Institute, Mar 2026). Germany’s science council, the Wissenschaftsrat, says critical thinking cannot be handed to AI and asks universities to redesign their exams (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026). Employers are moving too: one in three recruiters say they will replace some entry-level roles with AI (Poets&Quants, Jun 2026). Yet the measured damage is smaller than the headlines. In Germany the gap for 20 to 29 year olds in jobs most exposed to AI is 1.2 points and not yet statistically significant, though it is growing (IAB, in German, Oct 2026).

Some of this is settled: take-home coursework alone no longer proves who did the thinking, because 12% of UK students now paste AI-written text into it (Higher Education Policy Institute, Mar 2026). Three things are not. Will entry-level jobs shrink or change, when 36% of UK employers cut them last year (Work Foundation, Aug 2026) but 82% of graduate employers expect little or no replacement by AI (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026)? Will employers trust degrees or their own tests, when 92% saw suspected cheating in recruitment (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026)? And will partner universities in England, 36% of which ran deficits last year (The PIE News, May 2026), stay strong partners?

Looking ahead to 2030, UK graduate hiring is forecast to rise 7% in 2026/27 (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026), 96% of HR leaders in one survey expect entry roles to become “AI supervision positions” within five years (Consultancy.uk, on Pearson and Cognizant, Jul 2026), and about half of German firms using AI expect lower pay for new staff over the same period (Personalwirtschaft, on ifo, in German, Aug 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where a business school can still choose its position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that AI, not the economy, is changing graduate entry. Germany’s labour research institute finds the slowdown is the larger driver (IAB, in German, Oct 2026). If the economy recovers, the jobs pressure eases and a school that rebuilt its careers model around AI may have moved too early. The assessment problem would remain either way.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Redesign the assessments that carry the degree. Give every final-year module an in-person part, agreed with partners.
  2. Teach and grade the supervision of AI. Checking and correcting AI’s output should be graded everywhere.
  3. Replace the missing first rung. Build live employer projects and placements into programmes.
  4. Prove the degree’s worth. Publish graduate outcomes and a clear AI policy before the 2027/28 recruitment cycle.

Each is developed in the Strategic Implications, with alternatives in the Options.

1. AI is shifting the value of a degree from what students hand in to what they can show

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Schools and their partner universities on assessment; employers on what a first job needs; applicants on whether the fee is worth paying · Room to act: Wide on assessment design, teaching and careers support; narrower on degree rules, which are shared with partner universities

A degree has always rested on two promises: that the graduate learned something, shown by the work they handed in, and that what they learned leads to a first job. Generative AI weakens both at once. It can produce much of the coursework a business programme grades, and it is taking on the routine research, analysis and drafting that used to fill a graduate’s first year at work. For a private, international business school, whose students pay fees for a route into European careers and whose degrees are shared with partner universities, the question is what a degree must now prove, to whom, and how.

Sources: (Higher Education Policy Institute, Mar 2026); (Science, May 2026); (Euronews, in Spanish, May 2026); (Poets&Quants, Jun 2026); (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026); (Media & Learning Association, on the OECD, Jul 2026); (Work Foundation, Aug 2026); (HFD-Monitor via Forschung & Lehre, in German, Sep 2026); (IAB, in German, Oct 2026); (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026).

  • AI use is now universal, and coursework shows it. 95% of UK undergraduates use AI in some way and 94% use it for assessed work; the share pasting AI-written text straight into assessed work rose to 12%, from 3% in 2024, and 65% say assessment has changed significantly (Higher Education Policy Institute, Mar 2026). Around 90% of German students use it, and the OECD warns that use has outpaced universities’ rules (Media & Learning Association, on the OECD, Jul 2026). A study of 95,513 students in Science finds use and misuse vary widely by subject, and calls for reform designed subject by subject rather than bans or detection (Science, May 2026).
  • The rules are being written now. The Wissenschaftsrat makes ‘intellectual sovereignty’ its test for AI in degrees, asks universities to redesign exam formats and to keep some parts of study free of AI, and argues that detailed rules on AI use are hard to enforce (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026). In Germany’s HFD-Monitor, 95% of university teaching leaders call preparing students for an AI-shaped working world a strategic goal and 53% of universities offer AI learning assistants, but only 14% say adaptive learning is widespread (HFD-Monitor via Forschung & Lehre, in German, Sep 2026). Some Spanish universities are bringing back oral exams because detection tools are unreliable (Euronews, in Spanish, May 2026).
  • The routine work in entry jobs is thinning. In a survey of 750 HR leaders, a third said AI now handles about a third of the work once expected of graduates (Consultancy.uk, on Pearson and Cognizant, Jul 2026), and one in three recruiters in GMAC’s 2026 survey say they will replace some entry-level roles with AI (Poets&Quants, Jun 2026). In the UK, 36% of employers cut entry-level jobs for young people last year and 43% say AI or automation reduced entry roles (Work Foundation, Aug 2026). In the euro area, young workers’ employment fell 18.6% in ICT, 5.3% in professional services and 3.1% in financial services between early 2023 and early 2026 (European Central Bank, Aug 2026), the fields many business graduates enter.
  • But the measured effect on graduates is still small and mostly cyclical. The IAB finds no broad fall in employment in AI-exposed jobs in Germany; for 20 to 29 year olds the gap is 1.2 points, not statistically significant but growing, and the weak economy matters more (IAB, in German, Oct 2026). The European Central Bank says the evidence does not yet allow the youth slump to be blamed on AI (European Central Bank, Aug 2026), and UK graduate hiring rose 4% this year, with 82% of employers expecting little or no replacement of early careers roles by AI (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026). Graduate unemployment in Germany has nonetheless reached a new high of around 335,000 (ZDF, in German, Oct 2026), and in France the employment rate of new grandes écoles graduates fell to 76% (Thotis, on the CGE survey, in French, Jun 2026).
  • Pay and skills are being repriced. About half of German firms using AI expect lower pay within five years for staff with under five years’ experience (Personalwirtschaft, on ifo, in German, Aug 2026); for graduates with little experience the figure is 50.8% (dpa, on ifo, in German, Aug 2026). Recruiters say skills with AI tools rose most in importance, and report shortages in AI capabilities, grit and emotional intelligence (GMAC, Jun 2026), while employers rank strategic thinking, problem-solving and decision-making above AI literacy (GMAC Advisor, Apr 2026). Three quarters of 2025 French grandes écoles graduates already use generative AI at work (Thotis, on the CGE survey, in French, Jun 2026).
  • The market for a private, international degree is growing but more demanding. Private institutions now account for 27% of German higher education institutions and 13% of students (Forschung & Lehre, in German, Jul 2026), and their student numbers nearly doubled between 2015 and 2024 (Association of Private Universities, in German, Jul 2026). Germany hosts around 400,000 international students and doctoral candidates and ranks among the five most attractive host countries (DAAD, in German, May 2026). Applicants increasingly check a degree’s return on investment (GMAC Advisor, Apr 2026), and BSBI’s model of working with UK public universities such as the University of Chichester (BSBI, Jul 2025) ties it to a sector in which 36% of institutions ran deficits last year (The PIE News, May 2026).

Sources: (Work Foundation, Aug 2026); (Poets&Quants, Jun 2026); (Personalwirtschaft, on ifo, in German, Aug 2026); (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026); (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026). Surveys of different employers in different countries, read side by side as directional.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Employers are writing AI rules faster than universities. 82% of UK graduate employers now give guidance on acceptable AI use, up from 55% a year earlier (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026), while in 2025 only 19% of higher education institutions worldwide had a formal AI policy (Media & Learning Association, on the OECD, Jul 2026). If recruitment becomes the place where AI use is defined and tested, the degree’s own rules matter less to employers.
  • Weak signal The oral exam is coming back. Oral exams, ‘practically forgotten for years’, are returning in Spanish universities (Euronews, in Spanish, May 2026), and the Wissenschaftsrat wants AI-free spaces where independent thinking is practised (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026). Live assessment costs staff time, so the schools that make it work at scale will hold an advantage that is hard to copy.

Outlook to 2030

UK graduate hiring is forecast to rise a further 7% in 2026/27 (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026), so the near term looks like change rather than collapse. Within five years, 96% of HR leaders in one survey expect entry roles to evolve into “AI supervision positions” and 94% expect AI to create new entry roles (Consultancy.uk, on Pearson and Cognizant, Jul 2026), and recruiters expect skill with AI tools and strategic thinking to become business graduates’ most valued skills (GMAC, Jun 2026). German firms using AI expect wages to come under pressure over the same five years, mostly for less experienced staff (Personalwirtschaft, on ifo, in German, Aug 2026), and IAB calculations suggest around 800,000 German jobs could disappear by 2040 while a similar number are created by AI (ZDF, in German, Oct 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 business degrees are judged less by coursework and more by what graduates can show under observation and in real projects, and the gap between schools that can prove this and those that cannot is wider than the gap in AI content.

What it means: treat proof of judgement, shown in person and in real work, as the asset a business degree sells, and treat AI content in the curriculum as something every competitor will offer.

Counter-argument

The pressure may be mostly cyclical. The IAB finds the economic slowdown matters more than AI for young workers in Germany (IAB, in German, Oct 2026), the European Central Bank cannot yet attribute the youth slump to AI (European Central Bank, Aug 2026), and 82% of UK graduate employers expect little or no replacement of early careers roles (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026). If the economy recovers, graduate hiring may bounce back on its own. And because AI misuse differs so much by subject (Science, May 2026), a school-wide redesign could spend scarce staff time where coursework still works.

On the System Map: loop R3 (the hiring arms race) is turning, while loops B1 (schools test in person), R1 (fewer first rungs) and R2 (proven skills earn trust) are priming; leverage points 1 to 4 sit on observed assessment, AI supervision, early work experience and applicants’ trust.

System Map

The theme is not a list of separate trends. Four loops connect its parts. Schools test in person (B1): more use of AI weakens coursework as proof, which pushes schools towards observed assessment. Fewer first rungs (R1): AI takes routine graduate tasks, entry roles and early experience shrink, and employers lean further on AI. Proven skills earn trust (R2): observed assessment lets a degree vouch for skills, which helps graduates into jobs and brings applicants. The hiring arms race (R3): applicants use AI, employers add their own tests and rules, and the degree counts for less.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Redesign the assessments that carry the degree so students show their reasoning in person

With 94% of UK undergraduates using generative AI for assessed work and 12% pasting AI-written text into it (Higher Education Policy Institute, Mar 2026), coursework alone cannot carry a degree. The Wissenschaftsrat asks for redesigned exam formats rather than detailed rules on AI use (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026), and the Science study argues for reform designed subject by subject (Science, May 2026). An in-person part, a viva, a defence or a supervised task, in each final-year module would let the degree vouch for the student again.

Action: Deans to map every programme’s assessments by how easily AI could complete them by 31 March 2027, and agree an in-person part for each final-year module with the partner universities by 30 September 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 1, observed assessment, where loop B1 (schools test in person) turns and loop R2 (proven skills earn trust) starts.

Implication 2: Teach and grade the supervision of AI as a core skill

96% of HR leaders in one survey expect entry roles to become “AI supervision positions” within five years (Consultancy.uk, on Pearson and Cognizant, Jul 2026), recruiters report shortages in AI capabilities as well as grit and emotional intelligence (GMAC, Jun 2026), and three quarters of new French grandes écoles graduates already use generative AI at work (Thotis, on the CGE survey, in French, Jun 2026). Using AI tools is easy to claim; checking, correcting and explaining AI’s output is a skill a school can teach, observe and grade.

Action: Add a graded task in which students check, correct and explain AI output to every programme from the September 2027 intake.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 2, AI doing routine graduate tasks, where graduates who can supervise AI keep a place in loop R1 (fewer first rungs).

Implication 3: Rebuild the first rung: give students the experience entry jobs used to provide

36% of UK employers cut entry-level jobs for young people last year and 43% say AI or automation reduced entry roles (Work Foundation, Aug 2026), and about half of German firms using AI expect lower pay for staff with under five years’ experience (Personalwirtschaft, on ifo, in German, Aug 2026). If fewer first jobs teach the basics, a school that builds live employer projects and placements into its degrees supplies what graduates would otherwise lack. The cost is real, and the IAB’s finding that the economy is the main driver so far (IAB, in German, Oct 2026) argues for piloting before scaling.

Action: Careers and employer relations to pilot live employer projects or placements in at least two programmes during 2027, and track first-job outcomes for those cohorts.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 3, early work experience, where loop R1 (fewer first rungs) can be slowed.

Implication 4: Prove the degree’s worth with published outcomes and a clear AI policy, and watch the partners

Applicants increasingly check a degree’s return on investment and half expect AI in the curriculum (GMAC Advisor, Apr 2026), yet only 19% of institutions had a formal AI policy in 2025 (Media & Learning Association, on the OECD, Jul 2026), while 82% of graduate employers now publish their own guidance on AI use (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026). BSBI works with UK public universities (BSBI, Jul 2025) in a sector where 36% of institutions ran deficits last year (The PIE News, May 2026), so the strength of each partner is part of what applicants are buying.

Action: Publish an AI and assessment policy and programme-level graduate outcomes before the 2027/28 recruitment cycle, and review each partner university’s financial position every year from 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 4, applications and fee income, where loop R2 (proven skills earn trust) closes and partners’ finances act from outside.

Degrees Still Open Doors

↗ Shift from todayPasting AI-written text into coursework, done by 12% of UK students today (Higher Education Policy Institute, Mar 2026), stops mattering as in-person assessment becomes the proof employers accept.

In 2030 employers keep hiring graduates into reshaped entry roles and train them to supervise AI. Universities have redesigned assessment so a degree once more shows what a graduate can do, and employers use it as their main filter. Schools compete on the quality of their assessment and teaching.

Early indicators: Graduate hiring holds or grows; national bodies endorse observed assessment; employers cut back their own online tests.

The School Is the First Job

↗ Shift from todayCuts to entry-level jobs, made by 36% of UK employers last year (Work Foundation, Aug 2026), keep spreading, and schools supply the experience first jobs used to give.

In 2030 employers hire fewer juniors and expect graduates to arrive ready, but still trust degrees that prove it. Schools that build projects, placements and observed assessment into their programmes win students and employers; those that do not lose both.

Early indicators: Entry-level vacancies keep falling; employers ask schools for project partnerships; placement-based programmes recruit well.

Hired on the Test

↗ Shift from todaySuspected cheating in recruitment, reported by 92% of UK graduate employers today (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026), pushes employers to trust only their own live tests.

In 2030 employers still run graduate schemes and train their recruits, but they decide on their own live assessments. A degree is a ticket to apply, not proof of skill, and schools compete on preparing students for employers’ tests.

Early indicators: Employers expand in-person assessment centres; degree requirements dropped for some schemes; misconduct in recruitment keeps rising.

The Missing Rung

↗ Shift from todayReplacing entry roles with AI, planned by one in three recruiters today (Poets&Quants, Jun 2026), becomes common while degrees lose their weight as proof.

In 2030 entry roles are scarce and employers hire on portfolios and their own tests. Graduates compete with AI for routine work and with each other for the few roles that remain. International graduates struggle most, and schools that cannot show outcomes lose applicants.

Early indicators: Graduate hiring falls in successive surveys; starting pay falls; a significant AI effect on young workers appears in German data.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes. In October no future leads: the July reading sat in the corner of The School Is the First Job, and the reading has since moved back to the centre on who trains and towards employers’ tests on who vouches.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

Large employers stop asking for degrees and hire graduates on their own tests

Window: 2030 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: A degree is still the first filter for almost every graduate scheme.

92% of UK graduate employers saw suspected cheating in recruitment and 82% now publish their own rules on AI use (Resultsense, on the ISE survey, Oct 2026), and one in three recruiters say they will replace some entry-level roles with AI (Poets&Quants, Jun 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a major graduate employer in Germany or the UK drops the degree requirement for its graduate scheme and hires on its own assessments.
  • By December 2028: the Institute of Student Employers reports that most of its members use in-person assessment as their main hiring test.

What it touches today: What the school promises applicants a degree will do for them, and how careers support prepares students for employers’ tests.

Weak signal

Oral defences become the standard final assessment in European business degrees

Window: 2029 to 2035 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Written coursework and exams have been the default in business degrees for decades.

Oral exams are returning in Spanish universities (Euronews, in Spanish, May 2026), and the Wissenschaftsrat asks for redesigned exam formats and AI-free spaces (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2028: a national quality agency or science council in Europe recommends an in-person part in every degree.
  • By December 2028: a UK university that validates degrees abroad requires an in-person part in its partner programmes.

What it touches today: How much staff time the school budgets for assessment, and how it agrees assessment changes with its partners.

Weak signal

Supervising AI becomes a recognised entry-level profession with its own credentials

Window: 2030 to 2038 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: Entry roles are defined by business function, not by the oversight of AI.

96% of HR leaders in one survey expect entry roles to evolve into “AI supervision positions”, and 94% expect AI to create entry roles that do not exist yet (Consultancy.uk, on Pearson and Cognizant, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: a professional body launches a credential for supervising AI in business roles.
  • By June 2029: a major graduate jobs board lists AI supervision as its own job family.

What it touches today: Which skills the school grades, and whether its programmes could carry an AI supervision credential.

Each option is graded against all four futures and a wild card in the Scenario Stress Test: three hold in every future (add an in-person part to every final-year module; teach and grade AI supervision in every programme; publish outcomes and an AI policy, and review partners), five are bets, strong in some futures and weak in others, and one backfires in at least one future (tighten rules and detection).

Options: Assessment (deans and academic quality)

Option A · Least change

Tighten rules and detection

Keep current assessments, and add AI-use declarations and detection tools.

Trade-off: Least disruption, but detection is unreliable and detailed rules are hard to enforce, so disputed cases could dent the degree’s standing.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Hired on the Test; The Missing Rung

Option B · Middle course

Add an in-person part to every final-year module

Give each final-year module a viva, defence or supervised task, agreed with the partner universities.

Trade-off: Costs staff time and needs partners’ approval, but lets the degree vouch for the student in every future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Rebuild assessment around live projects and defences

Replace most take-home coursework with project portfolios and oral defences across all programmes.

Trade-off: The biggest change: high cost and long approval, but it makes assessment the school’s strongest selling point.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Degrees Still Open Doors; The School Is the First Job

Options: Skills and careers (careers and employer relations)

Option A · Least change

Add AI tool training to the careers offer

Run workshops on using AI tools for applications and work.

Trade-off: Cheap and quick, but it teaches what every competitor offers and employers rank below judgement.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Degrees Still Open Doors; Hired on the Test

Option B · Middle course

Teach and grade AI supervision in every programme

Make checking, correcting and explaining AI’s output a graded task in every programme.

Trade-off: Needs staff development and new tasks, but it builds the skill employers say entry roles will need.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Build live employer projects and placements into every degree

Make a real project or placement with an employer a required part of each programme.

Trade-off: Needs employer partners at scale and is costly where employers still train graduates themselves, but supplies the experience entry jobs used to give.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The School Is the First Job; The Missing Rung

Options: The offer and partners (president, provost and recruitment)

Option A · Least change

Market the current degrees on their AI content

Add AI modules and promote them to applicants, keeping the degrees as they are.

Trade-off: No new cost, but applicants check returns and AI content is easy for rivals to copy.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Degrees Still Open Doors

Option B · Middle course

Publish outcomes and an AI policy, and review partners

Publish graduate outcomes by programme and a clear policy on AI and assessment, and review each partner’s finances every year.

Trade-off: Exposes weak programmes to scrutiny, but earns applicants’ trust and gives early warning on partners.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Launch short, stackable programmes with employer-set assessments

Offer shorter certificates whose final assessments are designed with employers, alongside the degrees.

Trade-off: May draw students from the degrees and needs partners’ agreement, but fits a market where employers’ tests decide.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Hired on the Test; The Missing Rung

What We Are Not Planning For

Teaching

AI tutors replacing business-school teaching before 2030

Only 14% of German universities say adaptive learning is widespread at their institution, although 53% offer AI learning assistants (HFD-Monitor via Forschung & Lehre, in German, Sep 2026), and the Wissenschaftsrat holds that critical thinking cannot be delegated to AI (Wissenschaftsrat, in German, Jul 2026).

↺ Reinstated if a partner university validates a degree taught mainly by AI tutors, or a national body accepts AI-led teaching for a full degree.

Jobs

A collapse in graduate hiring before 2030

UK graduate hiring rose 4% this year and is forecast to rise 7% in 2026/27 (Institute of Student Employers, Oct 2026), and the IAB finds no broad fall in employment in AI-exposed jobs (IAB, in German, Oct 2026).

↺ Reinstated if graduate hiring falls in two successive Institute of Student Employers surveys and the IAB finds a statistically significant AI effect on 20 to 29 year olds.

Demand

International students turning away from Germany before 2030

Germany hosts around 400,000 international students and doctoral candidates and ranks among the five most attractive host countries (DAAD, in German, May 2026).

↺ Reinstated if the number of international students in Germany falls two years running, or Germany shortens the period graduates may stay to look for work.
