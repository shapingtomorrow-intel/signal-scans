Driverless Trucks Arrive in Canada; Federal Rules Don't

Commercial L4 heavy-truck operations are scaling in Ontario and Alberta while the federal CAV Framework is unchanged from February 2025 and AIDA stays dormant; Bill C-8 cyber is the only fresh sector-specific instrument moving.

The Canadian autonomous-land-vehicle conversation has been framed as innovation policy, a slow regulatory build with provincial sandboxes feeding eventual federal harmonisation. Spring 2026 evidence has outpaced the framing. Loblaw and Gatik are running 50 Level 4 trucks across the GTA, Kodiak AI begins Alberta resource-road log-hauls later in 2026, Waabi closed a $750M USD round in January with Export Development Canada and BDC participating, and Ontario's 10-year Automated Commercial Motor Vehicle Pilot Program opens a driverless commercial horizon to August 2035. Federally, the CAV Framework 2.0 has not been updated since February 2025, AIDA died at dissolution, and the federal CAV initiative funding sunsets across the FY26 to FY28 envelope. The strategic question this cycle: where does the federal anchor instrument land, and on what timeline?

Pattern narrative

Pilot-to-deployment is the cycle's dominant moveTransformation Driver

The commercial L4 heavy-truck, provincial pilot leadership, and industrial-policy capital signals compound. Loblaw and Gatik are operating 50 Level 4 trucks across the GTA under Ontario's ACMV framework, Kodiak AI brings autonomous log-hauls to Alberta in 2026, and Waabi's $750M USD raise with Export Development Canada and BDC Capital participation reads as de facto federal innovation backing. PowerCo's St Thomas foundations and the February 2026 federal auto strategy add a $10B+ industrial-policy floor under the manufacturing layer. The transformation is no longer "pilot programmes feeding eventual deployment"; deployment is live, with federal posture trailing provincial action.

The federal anchor gap is wideningEvolving Risk

The federal CAV Framework anchor and horizontal federal AI regulation signals together describe a vacuum that is not closing. CAV Framework 2.0 (February 2025) remains the federal high-water mark and has not been updated; AIDA died at dissolution and no replacement is tabled; the federal CAV initiative funding sunsets across FY26 to FY28. The Treasury Board AI Strategy 2025 to 2027 and the Directive on Automated Decision-Making are the closest substitutes, neither sector-specific to transport nor statute-backed. The risk is that provincial fragmentation hardens before any federal anchor lands, locking in cross-border friction.

The standards window is open nowEmerging Opportunity

Bill C-8 cyber obligations, foreign OEM market entry, and weak consumer purchase intent together open a 12 to 18 month window for federal-provincial AV regulatory coordination. Bill C-8 is the model: anticipatory, sector-specific, outcomes-based, with bite. Chinese OEM entry creates urgency for cyber and data-flow rules that pre-date scale deployment. Weak consumer ownership intent means the policy debate is still about commercial fleets, not retail, where federal harmonisation is technically easier. The opportunity is to use the Bill C-8 template to set commercial AV cyber, data and audit standards before scale entrenches provincial divergence.

Signals gaining momentum

  1. Commercial L4 heavy-truck deployment. A second Canadian commercial scale-up after Gatik (Kodiak Alberta forestry, a Waabi Ontario freight launch with a named shipper, or Aurora Innovation cross-border entry) would force the Branch to brief leadership on whether CAV Framework 2.0 needs a statutory follow-on.
  2. Bill C-8 progress through Parliament. Royal Assent within 12 months would crystallise the operating template for sector-specific anticipatory cyber regulation, directly applicable to a future federal AV anchor instrument.
  3. Interprovincial regulatory equivalence. Quebec or Alberta moving to recognise Ontario's ACMV operating standards would open the first Canadian cross-province AV freight corridor and reframe federal-provincial AV harmonisation work.
  4. Foreign OEM Canadian dealership openings. First BYD, Chery or Geely Canadian retail launch (likely Q4 2026 or Q1 2027) would force a federal ADAS data-flow and cyber standards posture.
  5. Waabi commercial Canadian deployment. A Waabi shift from Texas-only commercial testing to a named Canadian shipper contract would close the Canadian-talent / US-deployment gap and reframe the policy narrative.

Wild Cards to Watch

Fatal incident in an Ontario ACMV-pilot autonomous truck

Disruptive Risk Potential impact: Very High

Surprise characteristics: A fatal collision in the 12 to 24 month deployment window, before federal standards or insurance regimes are settled, with a clear sensor or decision-stack causal trail.

Early warning indicators: A serious-injury near-miss reported in Gatik or Kodiak operations; insurance carrier withdrawal of L4 commercial coverage; a US analog incident (Aurora, Waymo Via, Kodiak US) triggering NHTSA action.

The Branch should plan for a coordinated federal-provincial incident response now. Ontario's minimum $10M public liability cap, the absence of a federal data-recorder mandate, and the fragmentation between Transport Canada, MTO, and operators create a media and regulatory pressure scenario where the federal posture would be set under crisis conditions. The optimistic VTPI counter-position notes consistent overhype of deployment timelines and underweighting of safety friction (Victoria Transport Policy Institute, 01/04/2026).

Federal-provincial AV reciprocity framework tabled within 12 months

Disruptive Opportunity Potential impact: High

Surprise characteristics: Transport Canada brokers a Council of Ministers Responsible for Transportation and Highway Safety agreement on mutual recognition of provincial AV operating standards, opening an Ontario to Quebec or Ontario to Alberta commercial AV freight corridor before federal statute.

Early warning indicators: A bilateral ON-QC or ON-AB AV regulatory memorandum; a Council of Ministers communiqué naming AV mutual recognition as a workstream; a Waabi or Gatik public statement endorsing a federal-led reciprocity mechanism.

A reciprocity framework would solve the binding constraint that pushes Canadian AV companies to test commercially in Texas. Osler's comparative work suggests the UK Automated Vehicles Act unified national approach is one model; a Canadian variant using federal-provincial reciprocity rather than constitutional change would be the politically realistic adaptation (Osler, Hoskin and Harcourt, 03/02/2026).

Wild Card impact and surprise field

Both wild cards sit at high or very high impact. The fatal-incident risk is the more proximate trigger given live ACMV-pilot operations; reciprocity is the higher-leverage upside.

Implications

For Strategic Policy and Innovation Branch advisors, May 2026 sharpens the agenda rather than overturning it. Pilot-to-deployment is no longer hypothetical: 50 Gatik trucks operate in the GTA, Kodiak AI begins Alberta log-hauls in 2026, and Waabi's $750M raise pulls Canadian AV talent into US commercial markets first. Three threads warrant near-term focus: (1) the federal AV anchor instrument question, whether Transport Canada moves CAV Framework 2.0 toward statute or stays in advisory posture; (2) Bill C-8 as the operating template for sector-specific anticipatory regulation that could be lifted into a federal AV cyber and data-audit regime; and (3) interprovincial regulatory equivalence as the binding constraint on Canadian commercial AV scale. Federal-provincial AV-regulator coordination is the highest-leverage open lane this cycle.
