Why This Matters Now

Moving goods by sea is about to get cheaper, because more new ships are arriving than there is cargo to fill them.

The savings will not reach everyone. Europe's busiest ports are full, so cargo waits longer and storage bills rise. The global deal to put a price on shipping's carbon has stalled, so Europe's own rules set the cost instead, and airlines face a similar split. Routes are no longer fixed: ships are returning to the Suez Canal, China now runs a summer service across the Arctic, and rail links across Asia are growing. Driverless trucks carry paying freight, but only on a handful of roads.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: negotiate freight contracts for next year on short terms that let you follow prices down, and count the costs that have moved off the freight bill and onto your own books, from storage to carbon charges.

Executive Synthesis

Which budgets on your desk this week assume cheaper freight means cheaper journeys?

The evidence here says the cost is moving, not falling: into storage, carbon charges and route risk.

Ocean freight is heading into a buyer's market. Since May, 140 container ships have switched back from the Cape of Good Hope to the Suez Canal (gCaptain, Oct 2026), and BIMCO, the shipowners' association, expects the container fleet to grow another 9% in 2027 while demand grows by 0.5% to 4.5%, depending on whether the Strait of Hormuz reopens (gCaptain, Sep 2026). A cheaper rate is not a cheaper journey, though. Yards at Rotterdam and Antwerp regularly run above 90% full (Trans.info, Aug 2026), the cost of holding stock in the US is at its fourth-highest reading in two years (Logistics Managers' Index, Sep 2026), and the price on shipping's carbon is now set in Europe rather than worldwide (International Carbon Action Partnership, Jul 2026).

Some of this is settled: the new ships are already ordered, Europe's carbon price already applies to ships, and Europe's mandate for sustainable aviation fuel is being met (European Union Aviation Safety Agency, Sep 2026). Four things are not. Will carriers idle and scrap enough ships to hold rates up? Will the International Maritime Organization adopt its global carbon price at the meeting that opens on 30 November (Shipping Telegraph, Sep 2026)? Will the return to Suez hold, when war-risk insurance for the southern Red Sea jumped from 0.3% to between 1% and 2% of a ship's value within days of a renewed threat (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026)? And will driverless trucks move beyond the roughly 45 now running under a contested US waiver (FreightWaves, Oct 2026)?

Looking ahead to 2030, Drewry expects shipping capacity to grow between 6% and 9% in 2027 to 2029, faster than demand (CLECAT, relaying Drewry, Jan 2026), Europe's aviation safety agency expects the region to stay on track for its 6% sustainable aviation fuel target by 2030 (European Union Aviation Safety Agency, Sep 2026), Aurora plans more than 30,000 driverless trucks by 2030 (FreightWaves, Sep 2026), and Kazakhstan aims to carry 300,000 twenty-foot container units a year on the Middle Corridor by 2029 (The Times of Central Asia, Apr 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 7 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the return to Suez holds. BIMCO estimates that once routings fully normalise, demand for ships could be 10% lower than if the Cape route stays in use (BIMCO, Sep 2026), so the buyer's market depends on ships staying in the Red Sea. If a renewed threat sends carriers back around Africa before the 2027 contracts are signed, rates stay high and this briefing's central reading fails.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Tender 2027 ocean freight on short, index-linked terms with route options. Capacity grows faster than cargo in 2027, so long fixed rates signed now lock in today's elevated level.
  2. Put storage, delays and the cost of holding stock into landed-cost models. Congested ports and dearer stock-holding are where the freight savings go.
  3. Budget for Europe's carbon rules on ships and aircraft, not for a global price. The global deal is unresolved, while Europe's rules already apply and are widening to smaller ships.

1. More ships than cargo tip ocean freight towards a buyer's market

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Container lines and their alliances, through how many ships they sail, idle or scrap · Room to act: High: cargo owners choose contract length and carriers in the 2027 tender round

Container shipping is moving from scarcity to surplus. Ships that spent two years sailing round Africa are returning to the Suez Canal, which shortens voyages and frees capacity, while a large orderbook keeps delivering new ships. Cargo owners gain bargaining power in the 2027 contract round, and carriers must decide how many ships to idle or scrap. The catch is timing: spot rates on some trades are still far above their February level, so a long contract signed now can lock in a peak.

BIMCO forecasts of September 2026, relayed by (gCaptain, Sep 2026).

  • Carriers are back in Suez: 140 container ships, just over 2m TEU of capacity, have made the switch from the Cape of Good Hope routing, and almost all carriers return to Suez on Asia-Europe sailings in October (gCaptain, Oct 2026).
  • The fleet keeps growing: BIMCO expects capacity to rise 4.6% in 2026 and another 9% in 2027, with more than 14 million TEU on order, about 42% of today's fleet (gCaptain, Sep 2026). Hapag-Lloyd expects no notable scrapping of older ships this year (Hapag-Lloyd, in German, Aug 2026).
  • Rates are drifting down: Drewry's World Container Index fell 2% to $4,351 per 40ft container, and Shanghai to Rotterdam fell 2% to $3,337 (Daily Cargo News, Oct 2026). Analysts in Shanghai expect the return to Suez to cap any rebound in the export container index (China Futures Daily, in Chinese, Oct 2026).
  • Air freight is the exception: air cargo capacity fell 0.1% in August while demand rose 4.4% and yields rose for the first time since April (International Air Transport Association, Sep 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Drewry expects capacity growth of about 3% in 2026, then between 6% and 9% in 2027 to 2029, which it says would outpace demand unless cargo volumes rise substantially (CLECAT, relaying Drewry, Jan 2026). BIMCO expects increased supply growth to weaken markets during 2027 (gCaptain, Sep 2026). On this briefing's reading, ocean rates fall through 2027 and recover only as older ships leave the fleet towards 2030.

What it means: Plan for falling ocean rates in 2027, but keep contracts short enough to benefit and flexible enough to survive a new disruption.

Counter-argument

The surplus may take time to reach prices. Xeneta notes that transpacific spot rates are still up 344% and 335% on their level of 28 February and expects a correction, not a collapse (American Journal of Transportation, Oct 2026), and Maersk raised its full-year profit guidance to $10.5bn to $12.5bn after spot rates rose sharply (Maersk, Aug 2026). Carriers can also cancel sailings to hold rates up, as analysts in Shanghai expect in November (China Futures Daily, in Chinese, Oct 2026).

2. Cargo owners pay for congested ports while nearshoring stalls

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Port authorities, terminal operators and customs authorities · Room to act: Moderate: cargo owners can change ports, stock levels and supplier locations, but not port capacity

The savings from cheaper freight are leaking into ports and warehouses. Europe's largest ports are full, so containers wait longer and terminals earn more from storage, and in the US the cost of holding stock has climbed for a year and a half. Moving production closer to customers, the other answer to fragile supply chains, has slowed: in Mexico, new investment is a small share of the record total. Cargo owners carry the bill on their own balance sheets rather than on the freight invoice.

  • Europe's main ports are full: yards at Rotterdam and Antwerp have regularly run above 90%, vessel delays absorb around 5% of global deep-sea container capacity, and only 28% of sailings from North Europe to the US East Coast arrived on time in July (Trans.info, Aug 2026).
  • Rotterdam's transhipment volume fell 20% in the first half for lack of capacity in the port (Port of Rotterdam Authority, in Dutch, Jul 2026). Hamburg's terminal operator says extra storage fees from longer dwell times lifted its port-logistics revenue (HHLA, in German, Aug 2026).
  • In the US, the Logistics Managers' Index for the cost of holding stock reached 78.6 in August, its fourth-highest reading in two years, and warehousing prices read 75.0 (Logistics Managers' Index, Sep 2026). In Europe, the end of duty-free treatment for parcels under €150 coincided with Europe-Asia air cargo growth slowing to 3.1% (International Air Transport Association, Sep 2026).
  • Nearshoring has slowed: Mexico's foreign direct investment hit a record in the first quarter, but 94.2% was reinvested earnings and new investment only 7.2% (México, ¿cómo vamos?, in Spanish, May 2026), and industrial vacancy in the north rose from 1% in 2023 to 7% in 2025 as firms wait for the review of the US-Mexico-Canada trade agreement (Expansión, in Spanish, Apr 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Storage is becoming a business line. Hamburg's terminal operator earned more from storage fees as containers stayed longer, even as the high load on north German ports hurt its results (HHLA, in German, Aug 2026). If terminals earn more when cargo waits, they have less reason to clear yards quickly, and waiting becomes a priced service.

Outlook to 2030

Rotterdam World Gateway expects the extra capacity from the final phase of its expansion to open before 2030, a project its lender frames as an answer to sustained congestion (Container News, Sep 2026). Until then, on this briefing's reading, European port capacity stays tight while ships keep arriving.

What it means: Measure freight savings net of storage, delays and stock-holding costs, and treat the choice of port as part of the freight decision.

Counter-argument

The bill may shrink. US stock held per unit of sales is falling, with the inventory-to-sales ratio at 1.30 at the end of July against 1.37 a year earlier (US Census Bureau, Sep 2026), and Rotterdam expects extra capacity at several container terminals from the end of this year (Port of Rotterdam Authority, in Dutch, Jul 2026). If both hold, congestion costs ease during 2027.

3. Global carbon deals for ships and planes stall while regional fuel rules bite

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The International Maritime Organization's member states, the European Commission and Parliament, and the UN's aviation body · Room to act: Low for most readers; high for shipowners and airlines choosing fuels and ships

The global rules on carbon for ships and aircraft are stuck, and regional rules have filled the gap. The International Maritime Organization, the UN body for shipping, has failed to agree its Net-Zero Framework, the first global carbon price on shipping, while the European Union widens its own carbon price on ships. In aviation, Europe's fuel mandate is being met, while the UN's global offsetting scheme for airlines becomes compulsory for larger countries in 2027 with too few eligible credits. Carbon costs now depend on where a ship sails or a plane flies.

  • Talks on the Net-Zero Framework drew a blank for the third meeting in a row, and the decision moves to the next major meeting, from 30 November to 3 December, with an adjourned extraordinary session resuming on 4 December (Shipping Telegraph, Sep 2026).
  • The European Commission proposes extending the EU's carbon price to ships between 400 and 5,000 gross tonnes, with a review clause in case a global measure takes effect (International Carbon Action Partnership, Jul 2026); its own text provides for a review only if the International Maritime Organization adopts a global measure (Council of the EU, in French, Jul 2026).
  • Fuel suppliers delivered 1.1 million tonnes of sustainable aviation fuel at EU airports in 2025, 2.8% of the total (European Union Aviation Safety Agency, Sep 2026). Worldwide output is expected to reach about 2.4 million tonnes in 2026, just 0.8% of aviation fuel use (International Air Transport Association, Jun 2026).
  • Shipowners ordered 137 ships able to run on alternative fuels in the first half of 2026, down from 155 a year earlier, and liquefied natural gas took 73 of the orders (gCaptain, reporting DNV, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The UN aviation body estimates demand for eligible offsets in the scheme's second phase, 2027 to 2035, at between 880 and 1350 million tonnes, against around 41 million eligible units issued so far (Environmental Defense Fund, Jul 2026). The European Commission plans a targeted revision of its aviation fuel rules in the second half of 2027, aimed at simplification while keeping the ambition (CLECAT, Oct 2026).

What it means: Budget for Europe's carbon rules as the operative cost for ships and aircraft, and treat a global price as an upside scenario rather than a plan.

Counter-argument

The global deal may yet pass. At the spring meeting the framework survived a US call to end consideration of it entirely, and support grew as five countries that had backed delay switched sides along with ten previously neutral ones (Carbon Brief, May 2026). The airlines' association calls Europe's 2030 targets for synthetic fuel “beyond unrealistic”, so Europe's rules may be eased instead (International Air Transport Association, Jun 2026).

Decision link: Strategic Implication 3.

4. Suez reopens and the Arctic runs on a timetable, so every route is a choice

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Carriers, war-risk insurers and the states along each route · Room to act: Moderate: cargo owners can pay for alternative routes for time-critical or high-value goods

The route from Asia to Europe has become a choice with a price rather than a given. Ships are returning to Suez, but only partly, and the cost of insuring a Red Sea passage can change within days. China ran a full summer season of scheduled container sailings across the Arctic, and rail links through Russia and the Caspian region are growing. Cargo owners now trade days of transit against insurance cost, reliability and the small volumes the alternatives can carry.

Sources: Suez Canal Authority data reported by (FreightWaves, Sep 2026); (gCaptain, Oct 2026); (Science and Technology Daily, in Chinese, Sep 2026); (The Times of Central Asia, Apr 2026).

  • Suez Canal transits reached 1,358 vessels in August against 1,070 a year earlier, and canal revenue climbed 56.7% to $567.1 million, but many carriers still route around Africa (FreightWaves, Sep 2026).
  • China's Arctic Express ran scheduled weekly summer sailings from Ningbo-Zhoushan to Europe, about 20 days against around 40 via Suez (Xinhua, in Chinese, Aug 2026). The season carried more than 14,000 TEU over eight voyages, with delays of four to nine days on some sailings (gCaptain, Oct 2026).
  • China-Europe freight trains ran 14,842 services from January to August, up 15.9% (Science and Technology Daily, in Chinese, Sep 2026), and about 77,000 TEU moved on the Middle Corridor through Kazakhstan and across the Caspian in 2025 (The Times of Central Asia, Apr 2026).
  • War-risk insurance for southern Red Sea voyages jumped from 0.3% to between 1% and 2% of a ship's value after a renewed Houthi threat (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Kazakhstan aims to carry 300,000 TEU on the Middle Corridor by 2029 (The Times of Central Asia, Apr 2026). The World Bank takes a slower view: the corridor's growth depends on reform and investment, not demand alone. With deeper reforms, it says, volumes could quadruple and travel times fall by two-thirds, but by 2040 and after more than $25 billion of investment (World Bank, Sep 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 Asia-Europe trade runs on a menu of priced routes, with Suez still carrying most of it.

What it means: Price route options into contracts now, especially for time-critical or high-value goods, rather than assuming one default lane.

Counter-argument

The old map may simply return. BIMCO expects that once routings fully normalise, demand for ships could be 10% lower than if the Cape route stays in use, which is the old Suez network restored (BIMCO, Sep 2026). The credit insurer Coface estimates that only about 3.5% of trade between East Asia, Northern Europe and North America is likely to use Arctic routes, and that their commercial impact stays marginal for the next five years (The Maritime Executive, Apr 2026).

5. Driverless trucks are commercial on a few corridors, but only just

Longer-Range

Weight: low · Who decides: US federal and state regulators, Germany's vehicle authority and Chinese transport authorities · Room to act: High for operators choosing pilots; low for most cargo owners before 2030

Driverless trucks now carry paying freight, but on very few roads and on thin legal footing. In the US, driverless runs rest on a short-term federal waiver that a court is being asked to block; in Germany, the first cabless truck runs at walking pace behind an escort; in China, operators plan hundreds of driverless heavy trucks on designated routes. Meanwhile Europe's driver shortage deepens as drivers retire. Driverless freight will reach a few corridors and depots well before it reaches most networks, and the driver gap will widen first.

  • The US government told a court that only about 45 self-driving trucks run under the federal waiver that lets them use cab-mounted warning beacons (FreightWaves, Oct 2026); Aurora has applied for a five-year exemption to replace the rolling short-term waiver (Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration, Apr 2026).
  • Germany's transport ministry offers up to €14 million for vehicles with autonomous driving functions in regular use, including road freight (German Transport Ministry, in German, Aug 2026), and Lidl runs a cabless driverless truck in Hesse at no more than 25 km/h, at night, with an escort vehicle (ingenieur.de, in German, Sep 2026).
  • Pony.ai plans 500 to 1000 driverless heavy trucks within two to three years on expressways, bulk-commodity lines and ports (36Kr, in Chinese, Sep 2026), and a researcher at China's highways research institute says deployment at scale could cut operating costs by more than 20% (ifeng.com, in Chinese, Sep 2026).
  • Transport and logistics was the top use for professional service robots, with 117,500 units sold in 2025, up 21% (International Federation of Robotics, Sep 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal China is building roads for driverless freight. China's transport ministry is planning zero-carbon freight corridors of more than ten thousand kilometres, and Tianjin port already runs 117 driverless logistics vehicles (ifeng.com, in Chinese, Sep 2026). Where the state builds the road, driverless freight may scale before the business case is proven elsewhere.

Outlook to 2030

IRU, the world road transport organisation, expects around 660,500 European truck drivers to retire by 2030, on top of around 502,000 unfilled driver posts today (IRU, Jun 2026). Aurora plans more than 30,000 driverless trucks by 2030 (FreightWaves, Sep 2026); on this briefing's reading, driverless freight will still be confined to a few corridors and depots by then, while the driver gap widens.

What it means: Plan the driver workforce for the retirement wave first, and pilot driverless freight only where corridors, insurance and permits line up.

Counter-argument

The economics are not proven. The insurer Apollo prices autonomous trucks slightly above human drivers, and the carrier Werner says “we have a gap” on the business case (FreightWaves, Sep 2026). Pony.ai's driverless-truck revenue grew only 0.6% in 2025 (36Kr, in Chinese, Sep 2026), and a challenge to the US waivers cites a crash near Aledo, Texas (FreightWaves, Oct 2026).

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Tender 2027 ocean freight on short, index-linked terms with route options

Capacity grows 9% in 2027 against much slower demand (gCaptain, Sep 2026), yet some spot rates are still far above their February level (American Journal of Transportation, Oct 2026), and the cost of insuring a Red Sea passage can change within days (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026). Cargo owners should avoid locking 2026's elevated rates into twelve-month contracts for 2027: they should tender shorter terms, link rates to a published index, and price Suez, Cape and alternative routings as options in the contract.

Action: Procurement and logistics heads to issue 2027 ocean tenders by the end of November 2026, with index-linked rates and named route alternatives.

Implication 2: Count storage, delays and stock-holding in the landed cost of every lane

Many lanes, one carbon price

↗ Shift from todayArctic volumes grow well beyond this year's season of 14,000 TEU (gCaptain, Oct 2026) and rail keeps growing, while the International Maritime Organization adopts a global carbon price.

In 2030 a global carbon price applies to ships everywhere, so the cost of emissions no longer depends on where a ship calls. Asia-Europe cargo moves on several routes: Suez for most of it, the Arctic in summer for time-sensitive goods, and rail across Russia and the Caspian region for high-value loads. Cargo owners buy routes much as they buy insurance, paying for speed and reliability. Carriers compete on the design of their networks rather than on a single lane.

Early indicators: The global framework is adopted; the Arctic season lengthens; Middle Corridor volumes rise steeply.

Pick your lane, pick your rulebook

↗ Shift from todayEurope extends its carbon price to ships between 400 and 5,000 gross tonnes (International Carbon Action Partnership, Jul 2026), no global measure is agreed, and alternative routes keep growing.

In 2030 the global deal has failed, and Europe and a few others price carbon on their own terms while routes have multiplied. Each choice of route is also a choice of rulebook: a voyage that avoids European ports avoids Europe's price. Compliance becomes a routing decision, and carriers set carbon surcharges lane by lane. Cargo owners face the most complex freight market in years, with more choice and more ways to get it wrong.

Early indicators: Talks fail in December; Europe widens the scope of its carbon price; transhipment shifts to ports outside the EU.

The old map, with a price

↗ Shift from todaySuez transits climb well past August's 1,358 vessels (FreightWaves, Sep 2026), alternative routes stall, and a global carbon price applies to all ships.

In 2030 Asia-Europe trade has returned to the Suez route, the alternatives remain small, and a single global carbon price covers shipping. Freight is cheap and simple to buy, and the carbon cost is the same on every lane, so it passes through to cargo owners evenly. The surplus of ships has kept rates low, and the advantage goes to cargo owners and to carriers with the most efficient ships.

Early indicators: Suez transits return to pre-crisis levels; the global framework is adopted; Arctic sailings stop growing.

Cheap ships, local rules

↗ Shift from todayContainer rates fall well below the $4,351 per 40ft container of early October (Daily Cargo News, Oct 2026) as Suez becomes the default again, while carbon rules stay regional.

In 2030 ships use Suez again and the surplus has pushed rates down, but there is no global carbon price. Europe's rules make calling at European ports dearer than calling elsewhere, so carriers and cargo owners redesign networks around the cost of carbon rather than the cost of distance. Freight is cheap overall, but its price varies by destination, and Europe's ports compete with nearby ports outside the EU.

Early indicators: Talks fail; rates fall through 2027; transhipment volumes move to ports outside the EU.

Tender shorter terms with rates linked to a published index, so prices follow the market.

Trade-off: Captures falling rates, but exposes budgets if a new disruption pushes rates back up.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The old map, with a price; Cheap ships, local rules

Option C · Biggest shift

Buy routes, not just rates

Contract for capacity and route options across Suez, the Cape, rail and the Arctic, and pay for flexibility.

Trade-off: Costs more and needs new skills, but protects time-critical goods in any disruption.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Many lanes, one carbon price; Pick your lane, pick your rulebook

Options: Fleet and fuel (shipowners, airlines and their lenders)

Option A · Least change

Wait for the global rules

Keep ordering conventional and gas-capable ships and defer fuel bets until the global rules are known.

Trade-off: Avoids stranded bets, but leaves fleets exposed to Europe's rising carbon costs.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The old map, with a price

Option B · Middle course

Pass Europe's costs through

Charge Europe's carbon costs to customers lane by lane and cut fuel use by sailing slower.

Trade-off: Protects margins, but invites customers to route around European ports.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Pick your lane, pick your rulebook; Cheap ships, local rules

Option C · Biggest shift

New fuels and early scrapping

Order ships for new fuels and use the surplus years to retire older ships early.

Trade-off: The biggest cost now, but leaves the cleanest fleet when rules tighten and old ships must go.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Many lanes, one carbon price; The old map, with a price

Options: Port and corridor capacity (governments and port owners)

Option A · Least change

Expand existing terminals

Add capacity at today's main ports, as Rotterdam is doing.

Trade-off: Proven and bankable, but the relief lands late in the decade.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The old map, with a price; Cheap ships, local rules

Option B · Middle course

Charge for waiting and share data

Charge more for containers that wait, and share arrival data so yards clear faster.

Trade-off: Quick and cheap, but moves cost onto cargo owners before it frees any space.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Back alternative corridors

Invest in rail, the Middle Corridor or northern routes to give trade a second path.

Trade-off: Large, slow investment that pays only if trade keeps using several routes.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Many lanes, one carbon price; Pick your lane, pick your rulebook

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

Airlines find fuel mandates cheaper than global offsets

Window: 2031 to 2037 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Airline planning assumes that buying offsets is the cheap way to meet climate rules and blending sustainable fuel the expensive one.

Eligible offsets for the UN's aviation scheme number only around 41 million, against second-phase demand estimated at between 880 and 1350 million tonnes (Environmental Defense Fund, Jul 2026), while Europe's fuel mandate is already being met (European Union Aviation Safety Agency, Sep 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2028: airlines report their first-phase purchases under the UN's aviation offset scheme, and eligible supply falls short of their obligations.
  • By December 2028: an airline group argues publicly that a fuel mandate is cheaper to meet than its offset obligations.

What it touches today: Air-freight contracts and airline fuel strategies agreed now that assume offsets stay cheap.

Emerging

Driverless freight takes over depots and ports before highways

Window: 2031 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Plans and public debate assume driverless trucks arrive first on long, empty highways.

Tianjin port already runs 117 driverless logistics vehicles (ifeng.com, in Chinese, Sep 2026), and Germany's first cabless truck runs a short route to a store at night (ingenieur.de, in German, Sep 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a European retailer or port runs a driverless depot or yard service without an escort vehicle.
  • By December 2028: driverless vehicles at a major port outnumber the driverless trucks on that country's highways.

What it touches today: Depot, yard and port staffing plans, and warehouse sites chosen mainly for highway access.

Weak signal

The surplus of ships turns into a shortage

Window: 2032 to 2038 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: The market expects the surplus of 2027 to keep freight cheap for years.

Hapag-Lloyd expects no notable scrapping of older ships this year (Hapag-Lloyd, in German, Aug 2026), while Drewry's forecast has new capacity growing between 6% and 9% in 2027 to 2029 (CLECAT, relaying Drewry, Jan 2026), so the surplus depends on old ships leaving. If stricter carbon rules then force old ships out together, retirements bunch into a few years.

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: container-ship scrapping reaches its highest level in a decade.
  • By December 2029: new container-ship orders fall to their lowest level in ten years while carbon rules tighten.

What it touches today: Charter lengths, ship orders and long-term freight contracts priced on the assumption of cheap capacity.

What We Are Not Planning For

Price collapse

A collapse in container rates to price-war levels in 2026

Rates are falling, but Xeneta expects a correction rather than a collapse, with transpacific spot rates still far above their February level (American Journal of Transportation, Oct 2026), and carriers can cancel sailings to hold rates up (China Futures Daily, in Chinese, Oct 2026).

↺ Reinstated if carriers stop cancelling sailings and Drewry's index falls week after week through the end of 2026.

Route shift

The Arctic replacing Suez for container trade before 2030

The first scheduled season carried just over 14,000 TEU (gCaptain, Oct 2026), and Coface expects Arctic routes to stay marginal and uncompetitive for container ships over the next five years (The Maritime Executive, Apr 2026).

↺ Reinstated if a major carrier alliance schedules Arctic services, or the navigable season extends well beyond the summer.

Technology

Driverless long-haul trucks at scale on European motorways before 2030

Germany's first cabless truck runs at no more than 25 km/h with an escort on a short route under a time-limited permit (ingenieur.de, in German, Sep 2026), and public support so far takes the form of small grants (German Transport Ministry, in German, Aug 2026).

↺ Reinstated if Germany or the EU approves driverless trucks for normal motorway speeds without an escort.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If our ocean freight rates fell next year, how much of the saving would we lose to storage, delays and the cost of holding stock?
  2. Which of our 2027 contracts assume the Suez route, and what would it cost us if insurers repriced the Red Sea with a week's notice?
  3. Which ships, fuels or contracts are we committing to that only make sense if the global carbon price is agreed in December, and which only if it fails?
  4. If driverless vehicles reach depots and ports before highways, which of our sites and staffing plans would change first?
  5. Which of our current activities looks least wise in these futures?
