Why This Matters Now

Medicines are getting cheaper while the care people need as they age, and the insurance that pays for treatment, are getting harder to get.

Copies of the best-known weight-loss drugs have gone on sale in several large countries at a fraction of the original price. At the same time, governments in Europe are making it harder to qualify for help with care in old age, the United States is cutting back health insurance support, and employers face their steepest health-cost rise in two decades. Governments are also using trade deals to push medicine prices around, and AI tools are spreading through surgeries faster than the rules that govern them.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: plan for a world where buying a treatment is easy but paying for care is not. Ask who in your organisation, your workforce or your family would carry the cost of care, and watch the care reforms in Germany and England over the next twelve months.

Executive Synthesis

Which of your plans assume that the state will pay for care when your staff's parents, or you, grow old?

Germany has just raised the bar for help (German Health Ministry, in German, Sep 2026), and England's care review now reports in 2027, not 2026 (Department of Health and Social Care, Aug 2026).

Medicines are becoming cheaper and easier to get, while care and the insurance that pays for it are becoming scarcer. Generic weight-loss drugs went on sale in India after the patent expired in March, with more than 50 products approved (Medical Dialogues, Jul 2026), and Canada approved its first generic for weight loss in June (Health Canada, Jun 2026). Over the same months Germany's cabinet approved a care reform that raises the bar for help with daily living (German Health Ministry, in German, Sep 2026), and in the United States enrolment in marketplace health insurance fell 12% once extra subsidies ended (KFF, Jul 2026). Employers expect their health costs to rise 8.2% next year, the most since 2003 (Mercer, Sep 2026).

Some of this is settled: the subsidies have gone, generics are on sale and US Medicaid work requirements begin in January (Federal Register, Jun 2026). What is not settled is how far governments will push the cost of care onto families, whether cheap weight-loss drugs reach people through insurance or only for cash, whether US pricing deals delay new medicines elsewhere, and who answers for AI errors in the consulting room.

Looking ahead to 2030, the US Congressional Budget Office expects the number of uninsured people to rise from 30 million in 2026 towards 37 million (Congressional Budget Office, Jul 2026), the European Labour Authority says Europe will need about 808,000 more care workers by 2040 just to stand still (European Labour Authority, Apr 2026), and Morgan Stanley expects the share of eligible patients outside the US taking these drugs to rise from 2% to 10% by 2035 (Morgan Stanley, Apr 2026). Further out, one development few expect: eggs and sperm made in a laboratory could reach fertility clinics, and a UK government advisory body has already urged a change in the law to allow for it (UK Regulatory Horizons Council, Jul 2026) (section 9). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 8 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that governments will keep pushing care costs onto families. Germany's own analysts expect the tighter care rules to save only about half the money planned (AOK Research Institute, in German, Sep 2026), and Australia is adding home care places rather than cutting them (The Weekly SOURCE, Sep 2026). If England's care review in 2027 leads to new public money, the squeeze on families eases and the case for the employer options in section 8 weakens.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Find out who in your workforce cares for an older relative, and what it costs you. Governments are tightening who qualifies for care help, so more of the work falls to families and employers.
  2. Set 2027 health-benefit budgets for a rise of 8% to 9% and decide who carries it. Employers expect the steepest rise in two decades, and staff are being moved towards buying their own cover.
  3. Plan for weight-loss drugs used by many more people at far lower prices. Generics, pills and cash-pay channels are widening use well beyond today's patients.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Who pays for care in old age, and who does the caring

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: National governments and, in England, councils and a new pay-bargaining body · Room to act: Moderate: employers, providers and families can plan ahead even where the rules are set

Ageing populations need more care, and the systems that provide it are short of both money and people. The response this autumn is to tighten who qualifies for help and to ask families to pay more, while promising better pay for care workers later. The consequence reaches well beyond the care sector: employers lose staff who look after parents, and households carry costs they have not planned for.

  • Germany's cabinet approved its care reform on 30 September. Childless members will pay more from January, spouses who are insured through a partner from 2028, and the bar for the lower care grades rises (German Health Ministry, in German, Sep 2026). Grade 1 will need 15 assessment points instead of 12.5 (Stiftung Warentest, in German, Sep 2026).
  • England has merged the Casey Commission's work into a single report due by summer 2027, and the government's own account acknowledges unacceptable levels of unmet need and the high costs of care (Department of Health and Social Care, Aug 2026). The first fair pay agreement for care workers takes effect in April 2028, with up to £500 million for its first year (Department of Health and Social Care, Jul 2026).
  • Care vacancies in England fell to 6.2%, but new recruits from abroad fell to 30,000 after the visa route closed and posts held by British workers fell by 40,000. “The sector is projected to require a further 410,000 posts by 2040.” (LocalGov, Jun 2026).
  • In France, 68% of adults say they could not pay for losing their independence alone, and a care-home place costs more than €2,400 a month (France Épargne, in French, Sep 2026). In Japan, bankruptcies among day-care providers for older people rose 68.7% in the first five months of 2026 (Tokyo Shoko Research, in Japanese, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Staff shortages are now closing care businesses. Of the 27 Japanese day-care providers that went bankrupt between January and May, eight failed because they could not find staff, against one a year earlier (Tokyo Shoko Research, in Japanese, Jun 2026). In France, private long-term care insurance is shrinking even as most people say they want a general scheme (France Épargne, in French, Sep 2026). Neither the market nor the workforce is filling the gap on its own.

Outlook to 2030

The European Labour Authority says Europe needs about 808,000 more long-term care workers by 2040 just to keep today's ratio of carers to older people, while the number receiving care rises by more than 41% (European Labour Authority, Apr 2026). Germany will index care benefits to inflation from 2029 (German Health Ministry, in German, Sep 2026) and England's care report lands in 2027. On that path, by 2030 more of the cost and the work of care will sit with families and employers than today.

What it means: Treat care for older relatives as a workforce and budget issue now, not a private matter for staff.

Counter-argument

The squeeze may be smaller than it looks. Germany's AOK research institute expects the tighter rules to save about €2 billion a year, half what was planned, because applicants and assessors adapt (AOK Research Institute, in German, Sep 2026). Australia is adding 32,000 home care places this year and aims for a three-month average wait by November 2027 (The Weekly SOURCE, Sep 2026).

2. Millions are losing health cover and paying more for what is left

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The US Congress and federal agencies, state governments, employers and European health ministries · Room to act: Moderate: states and employers can soften the losses, as some already have

In the United States, the extra help that kept individual health insurance affordable has ended, and new rules will make it harder to keep public cover from January. Employers face their steepest cost rise in two decades. Europe is moving in the same direction more gently, with higher co-payments and deductibles. People who keep their cover pay more for it, and more of the bill falls on households.

  • Enrolment in the US marketplaces fell 12%, from 21.8 million to 19.2 million, after the extra subsidies expired. Ohio and Oklahoma fell by just over 32%, but “New Mexico is the only state to fully replace the expired federal enhanced premiums tax credits with state-funded subsidies.” (KFF, Jul 2026).
  • Insurers have proposed a typical 15% rise for 2027 after 20% this year, and “If these early indications of median premium increases for 2027 hold, typical premiums for insurers participating in the ACA Marketplaces will have jumped by more than one-third over a two-year period.” (Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, Jul 2026).
  • From 1 January 2027, states must require many adults on Medicaid, the US health programme for people on low incomes, to show 80 hours a month of work or similar activity (Federal Register, Jun 2026). In 2025, before these changes, 26.7 million people were uninsured all year (US Census Bureau, Sep 2026).
  • Employers expect health costs per employee to rise 8.2% in 2027, the most since 2003 (Mercer, Sep 2026). In Germany, patient co-payments will rise by 50% as the health insurers need almost €19 billion of savings (German Health Ministry, in German, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Employers are handing staff the money to buy their own cover. More than 20,000 US businesses now give staff a sum to buy individual insurance instead of running a group plan, up 53% on 2025, and take-up among larger employers more than doubled (Insurance Business, Aug 2026). They are moving people into the very market that is losing its subsidies.

Outlook to 2030

The Congressional Budget Office expects the number of uninsured Americans to grow from 30 million in 2026 to 37 million by 2036, mainly because of the 2025 budget law (Congressional Budget Office, Jul 2026). The Netherlands plans to raise its compulsory deductible from 2028 (Zorgwijzer, in Dutch, Sep 2026). On that reading, by 2030 more people in rich countries will pay a larger share of their health costs directly.

What it means: Expect health cover to cost more and cover less each year to 2030, and decide in advance who absorbs it.

Counter-argument

Losses can be softened. New Mexico, which replaced the subsidies with its own money, grew enrolment by 14%, and state-run marketplaces fell only 6% (KFF, Jul 2026). The uninsured rate did not rise in 2025 (US Census Bureau, Sep 2026), so the full effect is still a forecast.

3. Weight-loss drugs are going cheap and global

Near-Term

Weight: high · Who decides: Medicines regulators, generic makers, insurers and employers · Room to act: High for food, insurance and health businesses deciding how to respond

The patents on semaglutide, the medicine in Ozempic and Wegovy, expired this year in several large countries, and cheap copies are arriving. Pills are joining injections. Use is spreading from rich-country patients to a mass market, with effects on what people eat, what employers pay and how health systems plan. Who pays is unsettled: generics widen access abroad while some US employers stop covering the drugs.

  • Health Canada approved its first generic semaglutide for weight loss in June and is reviewing six more; generics in Canada are often 45% to 90% cheaper than the brand (Health Canada, Jun 2026). India has approved more than 50 generic products since March (Medical Dialogues, Jul 2026), and Brazil approved its first generic in August, which by law must cost at least 35% less (InfoMoney, in Portuguese, Aug 2026).
  • IQVIA puts the obesity-medicine market at $66 billion in 2025 and $92 billion in 2026, and notes that “Notably, around two-thirds of volume are users new to any GLP-1 therapy, suggesting that convenience, and lower prices expand the market rather than simply driving switching from injectables.” (IQVIA, Apr 2026). 13.1% of US adults now use these drugs (Food Business News, Jun 2026).
  • WHO has opened a route for generic semaglutide to be approved for purchase by UN agencies and poorer countries (World Health Organization, May 2026).
  • The knock-on effects are already measurable: US grocery sales are expected to fall 1.1% to 2.7% this year, $9 billion to $21 billion (Food Business News, Jun 2026). “Fewer employers are covering GLP-1s to treat obesity, with coverage dropping from 72% in 2025 to 60% in 2026.” (Business Group on Health, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal China is preparing to copy the next generation. Chinese generic semaglutide cannot launch until after mid-2027 because of data protection, but Chinese firms are already preparing copies of the newer pills and injections before the originals are approved in China (Yicai, in Chinese, Jun 2026). IQVIA counts 74 Chinese developers (IQVIA, Apr 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Morgan Stanley expects nearly 30% of eligible patients in the US and 10% elsewhere to be taking these drugs by 2035, up from about 6% and 2%, and sees Brazilian prices falling by up to 70% (Morgan Stanley, Apr 2026). IQVIA's range for the market from 2027, $105 billion to $200 billion (IQVIA, Apr 2026), shows how uncertain the price-volume balance is. By 2030, on this reading, these drugs will be a mass-market product priced like other generics in much of the world.

What it means: Plan for weight-loss drugs used by far more people at far lower prices, and for the effect on your own market.

Counter-argument

Cheap supply is not yet reliable. Dr Reddy's, Torrent and USV recalled batches in India, and Dr Reddy's expects supply in India and Canada to be disrupted until at least late October (Medical Dialogues, Jul 2026). In the US, falling employer cover means access may move to cash payment rather than widen (Business Group on Health, Aug 2026).

4. Medicine prices are now set in trade deals

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The White House and drugmakers, with European and UK governments responding · Room to act: Low for most readers; high for drugmakers choosing where and when to launch

Medicine prices in the United States are now set in deals between the White House and individual drugmakers, backed by a tariff on firms that do not sign. The US government presents the deals as lowering what Americans pay, and they now cover most of the branded market, though lawyers say how they work in practice is still unclear. Because the deals refer to what other rich countries pay, health economists say how quickly Europe takes up new medicines has become a global pricing question. For patients in Europe and elsewhere, the risk is waiting longer for new medicines.

  • The White House says companies covering 90% of the US branded market have signed pricing deals, and claims “These agreements are projected to save Americans more than $600 billion.” (The White House, Sep 2026). The US Medicare and Medicaid agency says 40 states and Puerto Rico had signed up for these prices in Medicaid, saving an estimated $64.3 billion over ten years (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, Sep 2026).
  • A 100% tariff on patented medicines applies from 29 September to firms without a deal, while many specialty medicines from the EU, UK, Japan and other partners pay nothing (Federal Register, Sep 2026). Nine more mid-sized drugmakers signed deals in September, though lawyers say how the deals work in practice is still unclear (Duane Morris, Sep 2026).
  • Across 36 European countries only 44% of new medicines are available, after an average wait of 607 days (Office of Health Economics, Jul 2026). Reuters data relayed by Euronews show new launches in EU markets down about 35% (Euronews, in German, Sep 2026).
  • The UK chose to pay more for new medicines, and the industry body says £1.98bn of new investment followed, though “This recovery is not yet secure.” (The Pharmaceutical Journal, Sep 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A US deal that rewards higher prices abroad. Documents obtained by the campaign group Public Citizen show Pfizer's deal gives the US health department a share of extra revenue if Pfizer raises its prices overseas (Fierce Pharma, Sep 2026). US policy now has a direct interest in what other countries pay.

Outlook to 2030

The Medicaid pricing model launched in January 2026 and runs for five years, to the end of 2030 (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, Sep 2026). On that path, by 2030 the price of a new medicine in Europe will be negotiated with one eye on Washington, and the gap in launch dates between the US and smaller markets is likely to widen.

What it means: Expect new medicines to reach some countries later, and treat US pricing deals as part of every market's access plan.

Counter-argument

The savings may be smaller than claimed. A Lancet study reported by Euronews finds confidential deals with 17 companies cut possible Medicare savings by 71% (Euronews, in German, Sep 2026), and Lilly's deal leaves out obesity drugs, which could cost Medicaid nearly $1.7 billion in savings (Fierce Pharma, Sep 2026).

5. AI has joined the consultation before the rules have

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Medicines regulators, health boards and the insurers that cover doctors · Room to act: High: health boards choose which tools to buy and how to check them

AI note-takers that listen to consultations and write up the record are spreading quickly, because they save time and reduce burnout. The rules have not kept up. Regulators have decided most of these tools are not medical devices, so the clinician remains responsible for every error, and patients are not always told the tool is in use.

  • The UK medicines regulator says AI scribes that only transcribe or summarise are not medical devices, and that “Clinicians remain responsible for reviewing and verifying AI generated transcripts, summaries and other outputs before they are used in patient care.” (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, Jul 2026).
  • In a survey of more than 1,000 UK GPs, about one in seven already used AI scribes; four in five users saved time, but nearly one-third said errors happened often (HealthManagement.org, Jul 2026).
  • A UK national commission on regulating AI in healthcare heard from more than 12,000 people (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, Sep 2026). A law firm's reading of its report says current law creates “liability sinks”, “where frontline staff absorb responsibility for AI failures despite having limited control over product design.” (CMS law firm, Sep 2026), and 77% of health professionals want the rules overhauled.
  • France's largest insurer of doctors tells its members that it is always the doctor who will be held liable (MACSF, in French, May 2026). In the US, over 280 health-AI bills were introduced in the first half of 2026, but only 33 became law (Manatt, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The EU has delayed its AI rules for AI built into medical devices to 2 August 2028 (White & Case, Aug 2026), and the new EU product liability rules apply only to products placed on the market from 9 December 2026 (MACSF, in French, May 2026). On that path, AI will be in most consultations well before 2030, and clinicians will carry the risk until the rules arrive.

What it means: Use AI tools, but decide now who checks them, who tells patients and who pays when they go wrong.

Counter-argument

The benefits are real and measured. At Mass General Brigham, ambient AI was linked to a 21.2-point fall in burnout (Becker’s Hospital Review, May 2026), and the UK commission's staged “L-plate” approvals would let regulators supervise tools in use rather than block them (Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, Sep 2026).

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Treat care for older relatives as a workforce and budget issue

Governments are tightening who qualifies for care help (German Health Ministry, in German, Sep 2026) and England's reforms will not report until 2027 (Department of Health and Social Care, Aug 2026). Employers and care providers should find out how many staff look after an older relative, what that costs in absence and turnover, and what support would keep them: flexible hours, paid carers' leave or help finding care.

Action: HR directors to measure caring responsibilities in their workforce and agree a carer support policy by 30 June 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1

Implication 2: Budget for dearer health cover and decide who carries the rise

Employers expect an 8.2% rise in 2027 (Mercer, Sep 2026), marketplace premiums are rising again (Peterson-KFF Health System Tracker, Jul 2026) and more employers are moving staff onto individual cover (Insurance Business, Aug 2026). Finance and HR teams should set 2027 budgets for a rise of 8% to 9%, decide how much to pass to staff, and check what moving staff to individual cover would mean for those who lose subsidies.

Action: Finance and HR teams to approve 2027 health-benefit budgets with an explicit cost-sharing decision by 31 December 2026.

Prepare Draws on Themes 2 and 3

Implication 3: Plan for weight-loss drugs in mass use at generic prices

Generics are on sale in India and approved in Canada (Health Canada, Jun 2026), pills are adding new users (IQVIA, Apr 2026) and grocery spending is already falling (Food Business News, Jun 2026). Food companies, insurers, employers and health planners should model a 2030 in which use is several times today's and prices are far lower, and decide whether to cover, steer or adapt.

Action: Strategy teams in food, insurance and benefits to run a 2030 weight-loss drug scenario by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 3

Implication 4: Watch where new medicines launch and who answers for AI errors

US pricing deals may delay launches abroad (Euronews, in German, Sep 2026), the EU's buy-European medicines law awaits adoption (Ropes & Gray, Sep 2026), and AI rules for medical devices now apply only from 2028 (White & Case, Aug 2026). Policymakers, drugmakers and health boards should track launch dates in their markets and the liability position of the AI tools they use.

Action: Policy and medical teams to review launch delays and AI liability each quarter from January 2027.

In 2030 governments have found the money for care, perhaps after England's review, but medicines are mostly bought for cash at generic prices. Weight-loss drugs cost little and reach most who want them, while insurance retreats to the most expensive treatments.

Early indicators: New public money for care; more drugs sold direct to patients; insurers drop cover for cheap generics.

↗ Shift from todayUninsured Americans reach the 37 million projected for 2036 (Congressional Budget Office, Jul 2026) six years early, and families pay most care costs themselves.

In 2030 families pay for both care and medicines directly. Cheap generics keep pills affordable, but care is the large bill, and employers and households carry it. Inequality in who gets care widens.

Early indicators: Eligibility tightened again; more uninsured; cash-pay channels for medicines grow.

The managed squeeze

In 2030 the state still funds care and insurance still pays for medicines, but both are rationed to fit budgets: tight eligibility, waiting lists and long delays for new medicines.

Early indicators: Care budgets frozen; launch delays lengthen; insurers restrict access to new drugs.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

Eggs and sperm made in a laboratory reach fertility clinics

Window: 2033 to 2040 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Fertility policy and benefits assume that the number and age of a person's eggs or sperm are fixed limits, so the debate is only about funding IVF and egg freezing.

A UK government advisory body reports that “Many experts consider that IVG could become suitable for clinical use in humans within 5-10 years, although this remains uncertain.” (UK Regulatory Horizons Council, Jul 2026), and has recommended changing the fertility law, which currently gives no lawful route to treatment. The fertility regulator has welcomed the report (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: the UK government brings forward a bill to update its fertility law for new techniques such as lab-made eggs and sperm.
  • By December 2029: a peer-reviewed study reports human eggs or sperm made in a laboratory with normal chromosomes.

What it touches today: Employers adding egg-freezing benefits and clinics investing in egg banks are betting that today's limits last.

Emerging

Brain implants become a paid-for treatment for paralysis, with China setting the first prices

Window: 2031 to 2037 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Brain implants are seen as Silicon Valley experiments years away from public insurance, with the US expected to set the rules.

China's national health insurance agency says most provinces now set an official fee of 6000 to 6600 yuan for fitting an implanted brain-computer interface, and the first approved device is on Shanghai's insurance list (Yicai, in Chinese, Apr 2026). Cover is still thin: Shanghai's city scheme pays 30% of the cost, up to a cap (The Paper, in Chinese, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: China adds an implanted brain-computer interface to its national insurance list, not only Shanghai's.
  • By December 2029: the US medicines regulator approves an implanted brain-computer interface for sale.

What it touches today: Rehabilitation services for spinal-cord injury and stroke being planned now assume no implant option within their lifetime.

Weak signal

Regulators accept slowing ageing as something a medicine can be approved for

Window: 2033 to 2040 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Medicines are licensed to treat or prevent named diseases, and ageing itself is not one of them.

A meeting held by the US medicines regulator's own foundation noted that no medicine is approved for ageing itself, and there is no agreed way to measure whether one slows it (Reagan-Udall Foundation for the FDA, Jul 2026). The regulator's chief scientist has since said ageing and longevity will feature in its next research priorities, due in its 2027 fiscal year (Lifespan.io, Oct 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By September 2027: the US medicines regulator's updated research priorities name ageing and longevity.
  • By December 2029: the US or European medicines regulator publishes draft guidance on how a drug could show it slows ageing.

What it touches today: Drug pipelines, pension funds and life insurers set their assumptions now on the basis that ageing cannot be treated.
