Why This Matters Now

2026 has been kinder than it looked. Insurers paid out less for natural disasters than in any year since 2019, and the world still holds large grain stocks.

That luck may not last into 2027. One of the strongest El Niño events on record is peaking now, and its effects on rain and harvests run well into next year. Food prices are already rising, India and Australia have weaker harvests, and the water stored in reservoirs and rivers from the American West to southern Africa and the Rhine is running low. Yet insurance is getting cheaper. Meanwhile cheap Chinese solar panels, batteries and electric cars are cutting emissions faster than climate deals, even as rising power demand keeps coal in use.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: plan 2027 as a dry, expensive year for food, water and insurance, and use this year’s calm to buy cover, stock and options while they are cheap.

Executive Synthesis

Which of your 2027 plans assume this year’s calm weather, low disaster losses and steady water supplies will continue?

The evidence here says a historic El Niño is peaking now, with effects on harvests and water into mid-2027, while insured losses this year were the lowest since 2019.

2026 has been a lucky year. Insured catastrophe losses of $92 billion in the first nine months were the lowest since 2019 (Gallagher Re, Oct 2026), and world cereal stocks are forecast at 950.0 million tonnes (FAO, Oct 2026). But the conditions for 2027 are worse. Forecasters give an 83% chance of a historic El Niño between October and December (US NOAA Climate Prediction Center, Oct 2026), with effects on harvests well into 2027 (World Meteorological Organization, Sep 2026). Food prices are 5.8% higher than a year ago (FAO, Oct 2026), Lake Mead could end 2026 at 1,034.15 feet in a dry case (US Bureau of Reclamation, Oct 2026), and Swiss Re’s chief executive calls the calm “the luck of the draw” (Insurance Business, Sep 2026).

Some of this is settled: India’s monsoon delivered 87% of normal (India Meteorological Department, Sep 2026), and China’s emissions have plateaued for more than two years (Carbon Brief, Sep 2026). Three things are not. Will El Niño cut enough harvests to outweigh a record Brazilian crop (Conab, in Portuguese, Oct 2026)? Will water shortages spread from farms to power and freight, as on the Rhine this summer (Statistics Netherlands, Sep 2026)? And will cheaper reinsurance, which 60% of market participants expect in 2027 (Artemis, on a Fitch survey, Sep 2026), leave buyers exposed when the luck turns?

Looking ahead to 2030, the World Bank expects food prices to rise another 7% in 2027 (World Bank, Oct 2026), Swiss Re says insured losses could reach $186 billion by 2030 (Insurance Journal, on Swiss Re, Jun 2026), and BloombergNEF projects China’s emissions 17% below their 2023 peak by 2030 (BloombergNEF, May 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that El Niño will hurt supply enough to matter. World grain stocks are large, Brazil expects a record harvest, and El Niño usually suppresses Atlantic hurricanes (SCOR, Sep 2026). If the event fades early, 2027 could be as calm as 2026, and the cost of preparing would be the premium paid for insurance not needed.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Cover 2027 food and commodity needs while stocks are ample.
  2. Test every site and route against a dry 2027.
  3. Use the soft insurance market to lock in cover, not to cut it.

1. A record-strength El Niño is heading for next year's harvests, and food prices have started to rise

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Food buyers, traders and governments in import-dependent countries · Room to act: Moderate: buyers choose when and where they buy; nobody can change the weather

El Niño, the periodic warming of the eastern Pacific, shifts rain around the world: it tends to dry out India, Southeast Asia, Australia and southern Africa and to flood parts of the Americas. This one is among the strongest on record, and it peaks as the 2026 harvests are sold and the 2027 crops are planted. Prices are already rising. The question is whether a world with large grain stocks absorbs the shock, or whether it lands hard on particular crops, regions and buyers.

Sources: (World Meteorological Organization, Sep 2026); (China Meteorological Administration, in Chinese, Sep 2026); (India Meteorological Department, Sep 2026); (FAO, Oct 2026); (US NOAA Climate Prediction Center, Oct 2026); (S&P Global, on ABARES, Oct 2026); (Conab, in Portuguese, Oct 2026); (World Bank, Oct 2026); (China Meteorological Administration, in Chinese, Sep 2026).

  • The US NOAA Climate Prediction Center gives an 83% chance of a historic event, stronger than any since 1950, between October and December, and says a strong-to-very strong El Niño is likely through January to March 2027 (US NOAA Climate Prediction Center, Oct 2026). The WMO says it may be stronger than anything since monitoring began, and that its impacts will be felt long after the peak (World Meteorological Organization, Sep 2026).
  • China’s weather agency expects the peak around November at 3.2 to 3.5 degrees above normal, clearly above the historical maximum, affecting China’s climate into spring and summer 2027, with risks to rice and rapeseed from persistent rain (China Meteorological Administration, in Chinese, Sep 2026).
  • Harvests are already short in places. India’s monsoon delivered 87% of normal, and 17 of its 36 weather subdivisions, 42%, were deficient (India Meteorological Department, Sep 2026); Australia’s wheat crop is forecast at 29.9 million tonnes, down 17% (S&P Global, on ABARES, Oct 2026).
  • Prices have started to move. The FAO food price index averaged 136.0 points in September, 5.8% above a year earlier, with cereals up 5.1% in a month and sugar rising partly on below-normal Indian rain and El Niño (FAO, Oct 2026).
  • The poorest importers are most exposed: the World Food Programme projects at least 49 million more people in acute food insecurity by the end of 2027, across 45 countries, with the largest impacts in Central America and southern Africa (World Food Programme, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Brazil’s cushion is thinner than it looks. A sensitivity exercise shows that a 6% cut in Brazil’s soybean output could lower the world’s soybean stocks-to-use ratio from 28% to 25% (Insper Agro Global, in Portuguese, Jul 2026), and China’s weather agency warns that El Niño will also reach its grain and oilseed supply through global markets (China Meteorological Administration, in Chinese, Sep 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The World Bank expects food commodity prices to rise about 10% in 2026 and another 7% in 2027, as disrupted grain shipments and El Niño threaten harvests (World Bank, Oct 2026), and China’s weather agency expects the event to affect its climate into summer 2027 (China Meteorological Administration, in Chinese, Sep 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 the El Niño of 2026 to 2027 is remembered less for a global shortage than for the crops and regions it hit, and buyers that diversified origins early paid less.

What it means: Treat 2027 as an El Niño year in food and commodity buying, and buy against today’s ample stocks rather than next spring’s prices.

Counter-argument

The world may absorb it. FAO forecasts world cereal stocks at 950.0 million tonnes, a stocks-to-use ratio of 31.7% (FAO, Oct 2026); the US raised its maize stocks to 280.44 million tonnes on a big US crop (DTN, on the USDA, Oct 2026); and Brazil expects a record 367.1 million tonnes of grain (Conab, in Portuguese, Oct 2026). Food prices are still 15.1% below their 2022 peak (FAO, Oct 2026). If El Niño fades early, the 2027 shock could be mild.

On the System Map: loop B1 (high prices bring more planting) is turning, as record plantings in Brazil answer higher prices; El Niño acts on harvests from outside the map, and leverage point 1 sits on food prices.

2. Rivers and reservoirs are running short, and the shortage is reaching power, freight and farms

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Water authorities, utilities and the industries that depend on rivers · Room to act: Moderate: firms choose sites, storage and routes; they cannot make it rain

Rivers and reservoirs are a store of water carried from wet years into dry ones. On several continents that store is being drawn down at once, and the shortage no longer stops at the farm gate: it reaches hydropower, river freight and the industries that depend on both. With El Niño bringing drought to southern Africa, India and Australia, 2027 starts with less in the bank.

Sources: (Zambian Observer, on the Zambezi River Authority, Oct 2026); (Statistics Netherlands, Sep 2026); (Down To Earth, on India’s Central Water Commission, Oct 2026); (Circle of Blue, May 2026); (Spain’s ecological transition ministry, in Spanish, Sep 2026). Bar lengths show the size of each fall or share; the Kariba bar shows the cut of about a third.

  • In the American West, Reclamation’s October study puts Lake Powell in its lowest operating range for 2027, with releases cut to 6.00 to 7.00 million acre-feet, and confirms a shortage at Lake Mead (US Bureau of Reclamation, Oct 2026). In the dry case Mead ends 2026 at 1,034.15 feet (US Bureau of Reclamation, Oct 2026), below the 1,035 feet at which Hoover Dam’s capacity is cut by 70%; its output is already 40% to 50% lower than in 2000 (Circle of Blue, May 2026). Central Arizona’s canal supply could be halved as soon as next year (KJZZ, Oct 2026).
  • In southern Africa, the Zambezi River Authority cut Lake Kariba’s water for power to 23 billion cubic metres for 2027, from 36 billion in 2026, because the rainfall outlook points to El Niño drought (Zambian Observer, on the Zambezi River Authority, Oct 2026).
  • In India, the 178 reservoirs the government monitors held 16.44% less than the ten-year normal on 15 October (Down To Earth, on India’s Central Water Commission, Oct 2026), after a monsoon at 87% of normal (India Meteorological Department, Sep 2026).
  • In Europe, the Rhine at Kaub fell below its 2018 record low of 25 centimetres in August and briefly below zero, hitting refineries, chemicals and steel hardest (dpa, in German, Sep 2026); Dutch barges carried 18.8% less freight in August, and freight to Germany fell 39.7% (Statistics Netherlands, Sep 2026). In France, 92 departments restricted water use on 10 August, 67 of them at crisis level (Eaufrance, in French, Aug 2026), and the EU’s drought observatory expects crop losses (European Drought Observatory, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The hidden water bill of data centres. In Arizona, water used at power plants to serve data centres could rise by as much as 400%, to 14.5 billion gallons a year, and utilities need not report much of it (KJZZ, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Reclamation’s dry case keeps Glen Canyon’s releases at 6.00 million acre-feet in both 2027 and 2028 (US Bureau of Reclamation, Oct 2026). New demand is coming too: the United Nations University projects that AI data centres will use 945 terawatt hours of electricity by 2030, with a water footprint of 9.3 trillion litres (United Nations University, Jun 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 water joins power as a constraint companies plan around site by site, and operators that mapped their exposure in 2026 kept running in the dry years.

What it means: Treat water as a supply-chain risk: map which sites, suppliers and routes depend on a low river or reservoir, and plan for a dry 2027.

Counter-argument

Shortage is not universal. Spain ended its water year with reservoirs 59.2% full (Spain’s ecological transition ministry, in Spanish, Sep 2026), and the Panama Canal raised its draft to 49.0 feet and its capacity to 33 daily slots from 15 October after near-average rain (Panama Canal Authority, in Spanish, Sep 2026). Where storage was refilled in wet years, a single dry season can be absorbed.

On the System Map: loop R1 (dry years drain the buffers) is turning, as low storage cuts supply and drawdown lowers storage further; leverage point 2 sits on water in storage.

3. Insurers had a lucky year, but the risk underneath keeps growing

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Insurers, reinsurers and the boards that buy cover · Room to act: High at the 1 January renewals: buyers choose how much cover to lock in

Insured losses from natural disasters swing from year to year, but the trend underneath rises with the value of what is built in exposed places. 2026 has been a quiet year by that trend. Reinsurers are flush with capital and are cutting prices, at the moment a historic El Niño is changing where floods, droughts and fires are likely. Most losses were never insured in the first place.

Sources: (Gallagher Re, Oct 2026); (Insurance Business, Sep 2026); (Verisk, Sep 2026); (Insurance Journal, on Swiss Re, Jun 2026). The 2026 figure covers nine months; the others are full-year measures.

  • Gallagher Re puts insured losses at $92 billion in the first nine months of 2026, the lowest since 2019; severe convective storms caused nearly 60% of them, including more than $43 billion in the US (Gallagher Re, Oct 2026). Swiss Re puts the first half at $42 billion, 16% below the ten-year average, but says a less costly half does not mean the risk has gone (Swiss Re Institute, Aug 2026).
  • The trend has not changed. Swiss Re says insured losses grow 5% to 7% a year in real terms, implying about $148 billion for 2026, and its chief executive calls the last two years “the luck of the draw” (Insurance Business, Sep 2026). Verisk models an average year at $171 billion, with property exposure growing about 7% a year and rebuilding costs about 5% (Verisk, Sep 2026).
  • Most losses are uninsured. Munich Re puts first-half losses at about $112 billion, of which only $44 billion was insured, a 60% gap (Munich Re, in German, Jul 2026); EIOPA says only around 25% of natural catastrophe losses in the EU have been insured over recent decades (EIOPA, Sep 2026).
  • Prices are falling anyway. In a Fitch survey, 60% of market participants expect reinsurance prices to fall further in 2027 and 58% expect terms to loosen selectively, through lower attachment points or broader event definitions (Artemis, on a Fitch survey, Sep 2026).
  • Losses are rising where they were rare. Insured wildfire losses in Europe have grown 8% to 11% a year in real terms since 1970 (Swiss Re Institute, Aug 2026), and France’s insurers put this summer’s wildfire damage at nearly €500 million, against about €80 million in an earlier comparable episode (France Assureurs, in French, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Cover that pays on the weather, not the damage. Peru’s insurance regulator approved rules for parametric insurance, which pays automatically when a measured threshold such as rainfall or flooding is reached, without checking the damage, as a strong El Niño approaches (El Peruano, in Spanish, Sep 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Swiss Re says insured losses could reach $186 billion by 2030 at the current pace, against a historical norm below $100 billion (Insurance Journal, on Swiss Re, Jun 2026). France expects claims under its natural disaster scheme to average about €4 billion a year by 2050, from about €1 billion a year between 1982 and 2024 (French ecological transition ministry, in French, Jun 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 the cost of cover tracks the trend again after one or two expensive years, and buyers that locked in multi-year cover during the soft market of 2026 and 2027 paid least.

What it means: Price and buy cover for the trend, not for this year’s luck, and use the soft market to lock it in.

Counter-argument

The calm may be partly earned, and El Niño may help. Fitch says reinsurance capital reached new highs in 2026 and softer prices will not materially affect it (Artemis, on Fitch, Sep 2026); SCOR notes that El Niño typically suppresses Atlantic hurricanes and reduces the chance of US landfalls (SCOR, Sep 2026); and France says homes remain insurable in 100% of its communes (French ecological transition ministry, in French, Jun 2026). But SCOR also expects more typhoon, flood and drought losses in the Pacific, the Americas and Australia.

On the System Map: loop B2 (the soft market corrects, late) is priming: falling prices let insured exposure grow, and losses catch up only after a delay; leverage point 3 sits on reinsurance prices.

4. Cheap Chinese clean technology is now doing much of the decarbonising

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Energy buyers, investors and governments in importing countries · Room to act: High: buyers decide what to install and from whom

For years the debate about cutting emissions has been about targets and negotiations. The faster change is now in prices. Chinese factories have made solar panels, batteries and electric vehicles cheap enough to win on cost, at home and in countries that never signed up to buy them for the climate. That is cutting emissions, but not yet fast enough to offset rising power demand and coal burned when gas is dear.

  • Chinese clean-tech exports in 2026 will avoid around 450 million tonnes of CO2 a year, roughly Brazil’s annual emissions and nearly 2% of emissions outside China; the effect grew 26% year on year, and 76% of lifetime avoided emissions fall in the global south (Carbon Brief, Oct 2026).
  • China’s own emissions have plateaued for more than two years and fell 1% in the second quarter of 2026, as oil use dropped 9% and 16% in transport (Carbon Brief, Sep 2026); its solar capacity grew 16.5% and wind 20.4% in a year (China’s National Energy Administration, in Chinese, Oct 2026).
  • Cheap kit is reaching new markets: Chinese solar panel exports to Africa reached 23 GW in the 12 months to June 2026, for only $2.4 billion, and Ember expects 17 GW to be installed across Africa in 2026 (Ember, Aug 2026).
  • Demand is rising too. China’s electricity use grew 3.9% in the first nine months of 2026, with charging for electric vehicles up 55.4% and internet data services up 41.6% (China’s National Energy Administration, in Chinese, Oct 2026), and the IEA expects global power-sector emissions to rise around 1% in 2026 as gas price spikes push some countries back to coal (International Energy Agency, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal After the panels, the batteries. Pakistan’s solar boom is pulling in Chinese lithium-ion batteries, and a Pakistan-China business conference produced 27 memorandums of understanding, one reportedly worth $82 million, as Chinese investors look at local battery and vehicle manufacturing (Dawn, May 2026).

Outlook to 2030

BloombergNEF projects China’s emissions falling 17% from their 2023 peak by 2030, with coal’s share of Chinese power falling from about 54% in 2025 to 19% in 2035 and solar becoming the world’s largest source of electricity by 2032 (BloombergNEF, May 2026). The IEA expects global power-sector emissions to flatten in 2027 (International Energy Agency, Jul 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 the cheapest way to cut emissions in most countries is to buy Chinese equipment, and the debate shifts from whether to decarbonise to whose equipment to trust.

What it means: Treat clean technology as a cost decision as well as a climate one, and check that the supplier will still be there to honour the warranty.

Counter-argument

Cheap kit has not yet turned global emissions down. UNEP puts global greenhouse gas emissions at 58.4 billion tonnes in 2025, roughly flat, with fossil CO2 still rising (Down To Earth, on UNEP, Oct 2026); China’s power-sector emissions rose 3.0% in the first half of 2026 (Carbon Brief, Sep 2026); and in the first quarter China’s grid wasted wind and solar that could have generated an extra 170TWh (Carbon Brief, Jun 2026). The price war is also hurting the makers: none of eight leading Chinese solar manufacturers made a profit in the first nine months (10jqka, in Chinese, Oct 2026).

On the System Map: loop R3 (cheap kit sells more kit) is turning, as falling prices raise installations and installations drive prices down further; leverage point 4 sits on clean-tech prices.

Decide Draws on Theme 2. On the System Map: leverage point 2, water in storage, where loop R1 (dry years drain the buffers) turns.

Priced and Covered

↗ Shift from todayWorld cereal stocks of 950.0 million tonnes today (FAO, Oct 2026) keep trade flowing through the El Niño years, and insurers pay for most of the losses.

In 2030 climate shocks show up mainly as higher prices. Food, water and power are traded across borders, so a poor harvest in one region is met from another, and insurers, priced for the trend, carry most of the losses. Cheap clean technology flows freely. Life is more expensive, but supplies hold and households are rarely left alone with the bill.

Early indicators: Exporters keep trade open in a poor season; insurers raise prices after a costly year but keep writing cover; cereal stocks stay close to FAO’s October forecast of 950.0 million tonnes.

Expensive and Exposed

↗ Shift from todayThe uninsured 60% of disaster losses today (Munich Re, in German, Jul 2026) grows, while markets keep supplies moving.

In 2030 food, water and equipment still move through markets, but after a run of costly years insurers have pulled back from exposed regions, so households, firms and governments carry most of the losses. Prices signal scarcity and supplies arrive, yet each disaster leaves a public bill. Governments become insurers of last resort.

Early indicators: Insurers withdraw from high-risk regions; state disaster schemes raise their surcharges; trade in food and clean technology stays open.

Managed Scarcity

↗ Shift from todayShort-term allocations such as Colorado River cuts of up to 40% today (KJZZ, Oct 2026) spread to more rivers, crops and grids, while insurance holds.

In 2030 governments share out scarce water, food and power by rule: export limits, water allocations and rationed grid connections. Insurers still carry most losses, so firms are protected from disasters but not from shortages. Getting an allocation matters more than paying the price.

Early indicators: More exporters restrict food sales in a poor season; more river basins move to short-term allocations; insurance prices stay stable.

Rationed and Alone

↗ Shift from todayWater for power cut from 36 to 23 billion cubic metres at Kariba for 2027 (Zambian Observer, on the Zambezi River Authority, Oct 2026) becomes a pattern, and insurers retreat at the same time.

In 2030 scarce food and water are rationed by governments and disaster losses fall mostly on those affected. Supplies are uncertain and costly to protect against. Firms build their own buffers: storage, spare suppliers, self-insurance. It is the most expensive world to operate in, and the hardest for poorer countries.

Early indicators: Export bans and water rationing in the same season as large uninsured losses; insurers exit whole regions; countries hoard grain stocks.
