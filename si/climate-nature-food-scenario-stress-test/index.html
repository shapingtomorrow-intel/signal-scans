Scenarios are not forecasts. Their job is to show how differently the next few years could turn out, so that a plan can be built to survive any of them, and then to be watched, so that the plan changes when the evidence does.

This page does both. It takes the nine options in the briefing and runs each one through four plausible futures to 2030, plus a wild card to 2040. A move that works in every future is robust and can be committed to now. A move that works well in some futures and poorly in others is a bet, held ready with a clear trigger. A move that makes things worse in any future is fragile and should not be relied on. It then scores leading indicators on the matrix’s two axes, shows how that reading has moved since September, and turns the reading into investment priorities.

1. Four futures to 2030, and a wild card to 2040

The two axes are the system’s two biggest uncertainties, the same two the System Map identifies: whether the soft insurance market corrects through prices or through insurers pulling back, which decides whether insurers or the public carry climate losses, and whether scarce food and water are shared through prices or rationed by governments.

Markets share · insurers carry

Priced and Covered

Climate shocks show up mainly as higher prices. Food, water and power are traded across borders, so a poor harvest in one region is met from another, and insurers, priced for the trend, carry most of the losses.

By 2040: Climate risk is priced into almost everything, and insurance follows the trend rather than the luck of the year.

Markets share · the public carries

Expensive and Exposed

Food, water and equipment still move through markets, but after a run of costly years insurers have pulled back from exposed regions, so households, firms and governments carry most of the losses.

By 2040: Governments have become the insurers of last resort for whole regions.

Rationing · insurers carry

Managed Scarcity

Governments share out scarce water, food and power by rule: export limits, water allocations and rationed grid connections. Insurers still carry most losses, so firms are protected from disasters but not from shortages.

By 2040: Water and food allocations are as routine a part of planning as permits are today.

Rationing · the public carries

Rationed and Alone

Scarce food and water are rationed by governments and disaster losses fall mostly on those affected. Firms build their own buffers: storage, spare suppliers, self-insurance. It is the most expensive world to operate in.

By 2040: Firms and countries that built their own buffers in the 2020s are the ones still operating normally.

Wild card to 2040

Two shortages at once

In one dry year a major exporter bans rice or wheat sales and the Rhine closes to most freight for a season, so food and industrial supply chains are cut at the same time.

Built from two of the briefing’s Potential Surprises to 2040: a major exporter bans rice or wheat sales (2027 to 2032, Low-Medium) and the Rhine closes to most freight for a whole season (2028 to 2035, Low).

What the stress test shows
  1. There is a robust core, and it matches the briefing’s first three Implications. Covering more of 2027 now with a second origin, testing every site and route against a dry year, and locking in insurance at today’s prices hold up in all four futures.
  2. Each area has one fragile move, and they are the moves that cost least today. Buying as usual, relying on current permits and routes, and taking the soft market’s savings each backfire in at least one future: where governments ration, or where insurers pull back.
  3. Each bet turns on one axis. Strategic stocks and investment in water reuse pay off where scarce goods are rationed; parametric cover and self-insurance pay off where the public carries the losses. The signposts in section 6 settle them.

3. What it means for the plan

Commit now

Robust: works in every future.

  • Cover more of 2027 now, and add origins outside the El Niño belt (Implication 1).
  • Run a dry-2027 test and agree a plan for each critical site and route (Implication 2).
  • Lock in more cover and longer terms at today’s prices (Implication 3).

Prepare, and act on a trigger

Bets: strong in some futures, weak in others.

  • Strategic stocks and long-term contracts with growers: release them if scarce goods move towards rationing.
  • Water reuse, storage and moving the thirstiest operations: release on the same trigger, starting with sites in the Colorado and Zambezi basins.
  • Parametric cover and self-insurance: release them if climate losses move towards the public.

Do not rely on

Fragile: backfires in at least one future.

  • Buying as usual through the season.
  • Relying on current permits and routes.
  • Taking the soft market’s savings.

4. Which future is gaining momentum, and how it has moved since September

Each future is a corner of the matrix, so the question “which future is gaining momentum” becomes two questions the evidence can answer: who is carrying climate losses, insurers or the public, and are scarce goods being shared by markets or by rationing? Fifteen leading indicators answer them, six on the first axis and nine on the second. Scored at each reading, they give a reading on each axis and a point on the matrix; the trail of points shows where the evidence is heading.

How to read it: each circle is one reading, hollow for earlier readings and filled for this briefing. The arrows run from each reading to the next, so they show which way the evidence moved. The pale band (the no-lead zone) marks readings within 15 points of an axis, where no future can lead. Hover over a point for its reading.

Scarce goods: close to the centre+5 in November. Large grain stocks, record plantings and reopened routes favour markets; water allocations, restrictions and disrupted shipments favour rationing.

What would settle itAn export ban on rice or wheat, or a second year of cuts on the Colorado and the Zambezi, moves the reading towards rationing; a mild 2027 harvest season moves it towards markets. A costly catastrophe year followed by insurers pulling back moves it towards the public.
How the reading is scored
  1. Each indicator is marked Moving (+1), Pending (+½), Not yet (0) or Against (−1), on the evidence published by the date of the reading.
  2. Each carries a weight: 3 if it would settle its axis on its own, 2 if it is strong evidence, 1 if it supports.
  3. An axis reading is the weighted total for that axis, scaled from −100 (insurers, or rationing) to +100 (the public, or markets).
  4. A future leads when the reading sits in its corner, at least 15 points from both axes, for two readings running.
  5. A shift is a move of 10 points or more on either axis between readings. It is flagged and reviewed in the next quarterly briefing.
  6. A signpost reached (section 6) overrides the score and triggers its decision directly.

7. How the monitoring runs over time

Every weekSignal Scans attach new evidence to each indicator and signpost, including weak signals that do not yet fit any future.
Every monthThe Change Tracker rescores each indicator, the two axis readings are recalculated, and the trail on the matrix gains a point.
Every quarterThe Decision Intelligence briefing reviews any flagged shift, and redraws the scenarios, the System Map and this stress test.
When a signpost is reachedAction Triggers fire, releasing the bet prepared for it and naming the decision it changes.

8. Running it with your own plan

The options above are the briefing’s. The real value comes from replacing them with the organisation’s own plans and asking the same question of each: does it hold in every future?

BeforeThe organisation shares its sourcing, site, insurance and energy plans for the next few years; we grade each against the scenarios and the evidence base.
Session, 2 hours20 minutes in the four futures; 40 minutes in which each group lives in one future and answers “it is 2030: what do we wish we had done in 2026?”; 40 minutes testing the plan against the matrix; 20 minutes agreeing the robust core and the trigger for each bet.
AfterThe agreed matrix, indicators and signposts join the programme, and the monitoring cycle above keeps them current.
About this page. The four futures, the nine options and the indicators all come from the briefing of 3 November 2026; the wild card combines two of its Potential Surprises to 2040; no new sources have been added. Each grade, each indicator status and each weight is the analysis’s judgement from the cited evidence and is meant to be challenged; the axis readings are then calculated by the fixed rule in section 4. The readings for 25 September and 23 October, the dates of the monthly Change Trackers since the September briefing, were scored after the event from evidence in this briefing’s base published by each date, so an indicator marked “Not yet” at an earlier reading may simply have had no evidence in this base by then; in a live programme each is scored when it falls. The 2040 lines extend each future beyond the briefing’s horizon and are offered for discussion.
