Executive Synthesis

Why this matters now

Six weeks ago the question was the 1.5% security band. Today it is whether European industry can absorb it. Four shifts since the May cycle that should be on the board's next agenda:

  • The bottleneck moved from money to capacity. €38bn of SAFE loans plus €1.5bn of EDIP are in place; the factories, skilled welders, certified components and procurement law to spend them are now what binds. Polish state group PGZ can make 25,000 155mm shells a year at one site.
  • Grey-zone attack is the baseline, not an incident class. GPS jamming across the Baltic is described by Estonia's public broadcaster as a persistent operating condition; France's ANSSI has named hybrid attack as its 2030 central planning scenario.
  • AI is on classified networks today; doctrine arrives in 2027 to 2028. NATO ACT agents are live in battle-management workflows; rules of engagement and accountability trail deployment by 12 to 18 months.
  • Information operations have crossed into the corporate boundary. AI proxy engines now back activist slates more often than ISS or Glass Lewis (Kekst CNC / Harvard Law Forum, April 2026). CISO, Comms and Strategy is one defensive problem.

Six weeks ago, the May 2026 Defence cycle argued that defence had stopped being a sector and that the 1.5% security-related band of the NATO 5% commitment had become the cross-cutting commercial implication. The June cycle reads the same evidence base after six more weeks of contact with reality, and the binding constraint has dropped a layer. Money is no longer the question. Capacity is.

The European Commission approved the first wave of SAFE loans for eight member states in January 2026, entitling them to around 38 billion euros. The Commission then adopted a 1.5 billion euro EDIP work programme in March, of which more than 700 million is targeted at counter-drone systems, missiles and ammunition. Germany's Bundestag passed a Procurement Acceleration Act on 15 January 2026 suspending the lot-splitting obligation until end-2035. France's PLF 2026 raises defence spending by 6.7 billion euros to 68.4 billion, with 2.4 billion specifically for munitions. SIPRI confirmed in April that European NATO military spending rose faster in 2025 than at any time since 1953. Polish state defence group PGZ told Defence24 in April that its current 155 mm shell output is 25,000 corps per year at one site, expanding but constrained by physical capacity. The capital is there. The factories, the skilled workers, the long-lead components and the procurement law to spend it are the constraints now binding.

Money is no longer the question. Capacity is.

This cycle's six themes trace the same descent across every domain. Grey-zone attacks: NATO has now stood up three standing missions (Baltic Sentry in January 2025, Eastern Sentry in September 2025, Arctic Sentry in February 2026), each one converting what was previously an incident category into a permanent operating posture. Capital: European defence, security and resilience startups secured a record 8.7 billion dollars in venture capital in 2025; globally the figure was closer to 49 billion; the question is whether the Pentagon's Defense Autonomous Warfare Group budget jump, from 225.9 million dollars in FY26 to up to 54.6 billion requested in FY27, can absorb that capital into deployed capability or whether the bulk sits as overvalued portfolios. AI: NATO ACT's PM-ADM agents are running on classified networks, the US Department of War's January 2026 AI Strategy Memo names seven Pace-Setting Projects including an Agent Network for battle management, and SIPRI reports OpenAI rescinded its military-use ban in January 2024; rules of engagement and accountability architecture trail deployment by twelve to eighteen months. Information operations: EEAS detected 540 incidents in 2025 targeting 200 organisations including NGOs, academia and traditional media; Kekst CNC's Harvard Law Forum analysis documents that AI proxy engines now back activist slates in shareholder fights more often than ISS or Glass Lewis. Two simultaneous second fronts: Russia and China increased presence in both Arctic and Indo-Pacific while NATO and partners formed counter-postures in each.

The materially new development since the May cycle is not the headlines. It is the visible point at which the financing wave hits the absorptive limits of the supplier base and the regulatory machinery. That point has now been named in Berlin, Paris, Warsaw and Brussels, and the rest of 2026 will be spent fixing it or living with it.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The thesis assumes the European industrial-base bottleneck is the binding constraint and that the political commitment to the 5% target survives the 2029 review. Three lines of evidence could falsify it. First, if the November 2026 SAFE second-wave disbursements run on time and EDIP calls are oversubscribed by ready-to-deliver suppliers, the bottleneck narrative collapses and capital remains the question. Second, if two or more European Allies miss interim milestones at the 2029 review without consequence, the 5% commitment softens into accounting and the industrial demand-pull weakens. Third, if the Pentagon's DAWG request falls back to a fraction of the 54.6 billion figure in the FY27 appropriations cycle, the US demand-pull on the global defence-tech capital wave loosens and Europe's late-stage capital gap widens. The signals to watch are German Bundeswehr first-delivery announcements in H2 2026, the second SAFE wave, and the FY27 US defence appropriations conference.

What changed since May 2026

  1. Spending step-change has hardened into a capacity ceiling. The May framing of "spending step-change and the resilience procurement category" was correct but premature. Six weeks of data say the procurement category exists; the question now is who can deliver into it.
  2. Subsea and cyber attacks have widened into a single operating environment. The May cluster on "critical infrastructure under live attack" understated the geographic scope. GPS jamming across the Baltic is now described by Estonia's public broadcaster as a persistent operating condition; France's ANSSI has named hybrid attacks as its 2030 central planning scenario.
  3. Autonomous systems and the neoprime consolidation have merged into one finance-and-capability problem. The May report treated DAWG and Anduril as separate clusters; the June reading is that the global 49 billion dollar defence-tech capital wave and the DAWG funding jump are two halves of the same question: can the demand side absorb the supply side fast enough.
  4. Information operations have crossed the corporate boundary. The May cycle's information-operations cluster sat in the longer-range tier. The Harvard Law Forum's April 2026 analysis of AI proxy engines moves it forward to near-term: the CISO, comms and strategy boundary is now a single defensive problem.
  5. Geography is back. The May cycle had no dedicated geographic cluster; in June, the simultaneous standing up of Arctic Sentry, the contested Indo-Pacific naval balance and the explicit Russia-China linkage in NATO's own framing makes the two-simultaneous-second-fronts read necessary.

1. NATO 5% meets the industrial-base ceiling

Immediate

Trillions of euros and dollars are now committed across European NATO, the UK and Canada to the 5% of GDP defence and security commitment by 2035. The May cycle named the financing instruments. The June reading is that the binding constraint sits one layer down: skilled labour, certified suppliers, long-lead machine-tool capacity, lot-splitting procurement rules, and the absorption capacity of mid-tier suppliers who would have to triple output to meet committed demand. The EDIP work programme and the SAFE first wave are visible attempts to push capital into the supplier base directly rather than through the prime channel.

From financing decision to deliverable capability: the 2026 sequence

Four financing events in the first four months of 2026 and where the binding constraint has moved to. Source: European Commission, BMVg, SIPRI.

  • NATO Allies committed to invest 5% of GDP annually by 2035, split 3.5% core defence plus 1.5% security-related, with a 2029 review point. NATO Hague Summit Declaration (25 June 2025).
  • The European Commission approved SAFE first-wave plans for eight member states, entitling them to around 38 billion euros, with first payments expected in March 2026. European Commission (15 January 2026).
  • The Commission adopted a 1.5 billion euro EDIP work programme, of which more than 700 million euros is targeted at scaling production of counter-drone systems, missiles and ammunition. European Commission (30 March 2026).
  • SIPRI reported in April that 2025 world military expenditure reached 2,887 billion dollars, and that European NATO spending rose faster in 2025 than at any time since 1953. SIPRI (27 April 2026).
  • The German Bundestag adopted a Procurement Acceleration Act on 15 January 2026, suspending the lot-splitting obligation until end-2035 and raising direct-award thresholds. BMVg (15 January 2026).
  • France's PLF 2026 raises defence spending by 6.7 billion euros versus 2025 to 68.4 billion, with 2.4 billion euros for munitions specifically. Ministere des Armees (24 October 2025).
  • PGZ CEO Adam Leszkiewicz confirmed Polish 155 mm shell-body output currently sits at 25,000 a year at Krasnik, expanding but constrained by physical capacity. Defence24 (22 April 2026).
  • McKinsey frames Europe's roughly 800 billion euro rearmament push as facing five binding bottlenecks (fragmented demand, skilled labour, supply-chain depth, financing predictability, procurement speed) rather than a capital constraint. McKinsey & Company (13 November 2025).

Counter-argument

The capacity-ceiling reading may be premature. Defence supply chains have historically scaled faster than expected when sustained demand signals arrive. If SAFE second-wave disbursements run on schedule, EDIP calls are oversubscribed with ready-to-deliver suppliers, and German first-batch deliveries arrive in H2 2026, the bottleneck thesis softens and capital allocation remains the binding question. Skilled-labour shortages can also be partially addressed through reservist-trained civilian labour, automation in shell-body machining and accelerated apprenticeship pipelines.

2. Grey-zone attacks become the default operating reality

Immediate

Subsea cable incidents, GPS jamming, drone incursions over Poland and Romania, ammonia and gas pipeline interference and AI-augmented cyber operations have ceased to be discrete events. NATO's response over the past thirteen months has been to convert them into a sustained operating environment. Three standing missions now run continuously: Baltic Sentry (January 2025), Eastern Sentry (September 2025) and Arctic Sentry (February 2026). The board-level threat model for corporate critical-infrastructure operators inherits the same posture. Norway's intelligence services in February 2026 named the security situation the most serious since WWII; France's ANSSI in March 2026 named hybrid attacks as the 2030 central planning scenario. The threat is not contingent. It is the operating environment.

  • NATO launched Baltic Sentry in January 2025 to deter and respond to destabilising acts against critical undersea infrastructure in the Baltic Sea. NATO (14 January 2025).
  • Eastern Sentry was launched in September 2025 following repeated Russian airspace incursions over Poland, Romania, Estonia and the Baltic states, providing continuous air-land-sea vigilance from the High North to the Black Sea. NATO (12 September 2025).
  • The European Commission committed 347 million euros and a binding Cable Security Toolbox in February 2026 to counter the rise of intentional damage and sabotage to subsea cables. European Commission (5 February 2026).
  • Norway's Intelligence Service, PST and NSM jointly assessed in February 2026 that Norway faces its most serious security situation since WWII, with Russia combining espionage, sabotage, cyber and influence operations against critical infrastructure. Government of Norway (6 February 2026).
  • ANSSI's Director General warned in March 2026 that France should expect a massive rise by 2030 in hybrid attacks, of which cyber is a major component, with concrete and potentially destructive effects on critical infrastructure. ANSSI (11 March 2026).
  • GPS jamming across the Baltic region has been described by Estonian public broadcaster ERR as a persistent operating condition forcing aviation, defence and critical infrastructure to treat unreliable satellite navigation as the baseline. ERR (26 May 2026).
  • RUSI argues that Kremlin grey-zone aggression against NATO will not abate even if Ukraine fighting ceases, and is likely to increase to destabilise the West. RUSI (29 August 2025).

Counter-argument

The grey-zone framing risks normalising the threat in ways that suppress political appetite for response. Some Baltic cable incidents have been forensically attributed to anchor-drag accidents in busy commercial waters; GPS jamming may peak with Russian active military operations and abate in a ceasefire scenario. If even one of the standing NATO missions is wound down within twelve months (which would require a major shift in the Russian threat picture, but is not impossible), the default-operating-reality reading weakens. The corporate-board implication softens correspondingly. ANSSI's 2030 scenario is also a planning hypothesis, not a forecast.

3. Defence-tech's $49bn capital-to-capability lag

Near-Term

2025 venture deals into defence, security and resilience hit a record. European DSR startups secured 8.7 billion dollars (up 55% year-on-year, with late-stage tripling to 4.7 billion). Globally, the figure was roughly six times that. The Pentagon's Defense Autonomous Warfare Group budget request for FY27 is up to 54.6 billion dollars, against 225.9 million in FY26. Anduril's 20 billion dollar US Army enterprise contract consolidates 120 to 130 prior orders. The question has shifted from whether capital is there to the lag between capital and contracted programmes of record, and whether the Pentagon's reorganisation of the demand side and Europe's late-stage capital gap close in time to convert the wave into deployed capability before the cycle turns.

  • European defence, security and resilience startups secured a record 8.7 billion dollars in venture capital in 2025, up 55% year-on-year with late-stage tripling. NATO Innovation Fund / Dealroom (10 February 2026).
  • France's Fonds Innovation Defense (a 200 million euro Bpifrance-managed vehicle funded by the AID) co-led a 20 million euro round in semiconductor IP firm NanoXplore with prime MBDA in December 2025. Ministere des Armees / AID (18 December 2025).
  • Bain finds the average volume of venture deals in European defence over the past five years has nearly quadrupled versus the prior five years, with PE activity roughly doubling. Bain & Company (27 January 2026).
  • The Pentagon's FY27 budget request shows a more than 24,000% boost for the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group, from 225.9 million dollars in FY26 to up to 54.6 billion requested in FY27. DefenseScoop (21 April 2026).
  • 53.6 billion dollars of the DAWG ask sits in flexible reconciliation funding rather than the base budget, alongside a 1,247% hike for the Office of Strategic Capital loan programme. Breaking Defense (21 April 2026).
  • The US Army's 5-to-10-year, 20 billion dollar ceiling enterprise contract with Anduril consolidates roughly 120 to 130 existing orders under one umbrella. Fortune (22 March 2026).
  • Europe's defence-tech base remains roughly five years behind the US in maturity; in rounds above 200 million dollars, US investors provide more than 60% of the capital. McKinsey (12 February 2025).

Counter-argument

The lag may be a feature, not a bug. Programmes of record historically take three to five years to mature from prototype, and the 2025 capital wave is only one cycle through that pipeline. The Pentagon's DAWG request is a Department of War aspiration, not a Congressional appropriation; if the FY27 conference settles at half the request or less, the demand side does not catch up. European late-stage gaps may also be closed by the next generation of sovereign defence funds (the French AID model in NanoXplore is one early proof), making US-led late-stage rounds less dominant than the McKinsey 2025 reading implies.

4. AI moves from staff slides to battlefield decision-support

Near-Term

Agentic AI has crossed from briefing material into the operational role. NATO Allied Command Transformation's PM-ADM, AIDA and AI FELIX programmes are running on classified networks; the US Department of War's January 2026 AI Strategy Memo names seven Pace-Setting Projects including an Agent Network for battle management and decision support; the US Army's Next Generation Command and Control prototype with Anduril, Palantir, Microsoft and partners is in live experimentation with the 4th Infantry Division. The accountability and rules-of-engagement architecture lags deployment by twelve to eighteen months. SIPRI's mapping of the military AI industry documents that foundation-model providers including OpenAI rescinded their bans on military use in January 2024, and the AI stack for decision-support is now shared between commercial and defence supply chains.

  • NATO ACT's PM-ADM is designed to operate around the clock, processing legacy command and control system data and open sources, with intelligent agents detecting gaps in understanding and proposing ways to fill them. NATO Allied Command Transformation (16 April 2025).
  • The US Department of War's January 2026 AI Strategy Memo directs the department to become an AI-first warfighting force centred on seven Pace-Setting Projects, aggressive data-access mandates, expanded AI compute and accelerated AI talent hiring. Holland & Knight on DoW memoranda (23 February 2026).
  • SIPRI documents that foundation-model providers including OpenAI rescinded their bans on military use, with OpenAI's January 2024 ban removal one of the watershed moments; the AI decision-support stack is now a shared commercial-defence supply chain. SIPRI (23 April 2026).
  • IISS finds AI-enabled military technology is proliferating beyond superpowers into Middle East militaries, exposing the inability of existing governance to manage commercial providers. IISS (15 April 2026).
  • Anduril leads a team (Palantir, Microsoft, Striveworks, Govini, ICE, RII) on the US Army Next Generation Command and Control prototype, integrating AI-enabled C2 for live experimentation with the 4th Infantry Division. Anduril (18 July 2025).
  • Germany's BAAINBw selected the Helsing / Rohde & Schwarz / IBM HIS consortium to build the AI backbone for the Future Combat Air System, embedding meaningful human control into a sixth-generation fighter platform. Shephard Media (1 September 2023).

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal An accountability incident attached to an AI-augmented kinetic decision. Would gain weight if a NATO member or partner force publicly attributes a civilian-harm event to an AI-assisted targeting recommendation, triggering a doctrinal rewrite within six months.
  • Weak signal A frontier model provider re-applying a military-use restriction. Would gain weight if OpenAI, Anthropic or Google reverses on military or dual-use deployment in 2026, fragmenting the commercial-defence supply chain.

Counter-argument

The deployment-versus-doctrine gap may close faster than the 12-to-18-month reading suggests. NATO ACT's existing rules of engagement, the EU AI Act's high-risk classification provisions, and national-level military AI ethics frameworks (the US DoD AI Ethical Principles, France's defence AI strategy) are not nothing. The cluster's load-bearing assumption is that deployment will outpace doctrine; if the FSB's October 2026 AI sound practices report and the EU AI Act enforcement in August 2026 catch up, the doctrinal lag closes and the cluster reads as a transition rather than a problem. SIPRI's framing of the commercial-defence supply chain as shared also tends to overstate accountability difficulties.

5. Information operations cross into corporate environments

Near-Term

The 2024 to 2025 European election cycle established a documented pattern of AI-augmented Russian and Chinese influence operations against democratic processes. Mid-2026 evidence shows the pattern extending into commercial environments: shareholder activism, M&A campaigns and ESG positioning. EEAS's March 2026 Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference report logged 540 incidents in 2025 targeting over 100 countries and 200 organisations including NGOs, academia and traditional media. Harvard Law Forum's April 2026 analysis of AI proxy engines documents that LLMs are recommending activist slates in shareholder fights at materially higher rates than ISS or Glass Lewis. The CISO, comms and strategy boundary is now a single defensive problem space.

  • EEAS detected 540 Foreign Information Manipulation and Interference incidents in 2025 targeting over 100 countries, 200 organisations including traditional media, NGOs and academia, and 100-plus individuals; 29% were attributed to Russia and 6% to China. EEAS (12 March 2026).
  • ANSSI's 2025 cybermenace panorama (published March 2026) finds a technological and organisational fog blurring state actors and cybercriminals, with AI-generated lookalike sites used to host payloads and collect data. ANSSI (11 March 2026).
  • Mandiant's M-Trends 2026 confirms attackers are abusing AI within compromised environments, with the QUIETVAULT credential stealer observed checking targeted machines for local AI command-line tools and executing predefined prompts to search for configuration files. Mandiant / Google Cloud (23 March 2026).
  • Microsoft documents nation-state and criminal actors using generative AI as standard tradecraft for phishing lures, translation, malware scaffolding and synthetic-media influence operations against corporate targets. Microsoft Security Blog (6 March 2026).
  • Harvard Law Forum (Kekst CNC analysis): in tested proxy fights, AI on average demonstrated just 37% support for issuers, substantially lower than ISS (56%) and Glass Lewis (55%) and below the 59% management winning rate in actual proxy contests. Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance (20 April 2026).
  • ENISA Threat Landscape 2025 analysis of 4,875 incidents (July 2024 to June 2025) finds hacktivist activity accounts for roughly 80% of recorded incidents, with state-aligned faketivism increasingly blending DDoS and information manipulation behaviours into a single threat workflow. ENISA (October 2025).

Counter-argument

The corporate-crossover reading risks overstating the operational impact of AI-mediated narrative manipulation. AI proxy advisor adoption is currently low among large institutional investors; the Kekst CNC study tested model behaviour, not actual investment-firm voting decisions. The EEAS 540-incident count includes a long tail of low-impact activity, and most corporate communications and threat-intel functions are already responding to AI-augmented social engineering as an extension of standard cyber-threat programmes rather than as a new category. If the operational reality is incremental rather than discontinuous, the integrated CISO-plus-comms-plus-strategy reading is premature for many boards.

6. Arctic and Indo-Pacific: two simultaneous second fronts

Longer-Range

China, Russia, the United States and regional powers are scaling presence and contesting access in the Arctic and the Indo-Pacific at the same time. NATO's Arctic Sentry, launched in February 2026, is the alliance's third standing multi-domain activity in thirteen months and is led by Joint Force Command Norfolk, with Sweden, Germany, Denmark, the UK and others making operational contributions within days. ASPI's third Pressure Points instalment in June 2026 maps China's coordinated build-out across the Southwest Pacific, Indian Ocean and Australia's maritime approaches. Both theatres are load-bearing for global shipping, undersea cables, energy and critical-mineral routes. The 18-month horizon is for awareness and posture; the 5-year horizon is for sustained capability commitment.

  • NATO launched Arctic Sentry, its third multi-domain activity in a year, explicitly citing Russia's increasing military activity and China's growing interest in the Arctic and led by Joint Force Command Norfolk. NATO (11 February 2026).
  • Sweden, Germany, Denmark, the UK and others made operational contributions to Arctic Sentry within days of launch; the UK doubled its Norway deployment from 1,000 to 2,000 troops, with Defence Secretary Healey calling Russia the greatest threat to Arctic and High North security since the Cold War. U.S. Naval Institute News (16 February 2026).
  • ASPI's third Pressure Points instalment maps China's layered build-out across the Pacific and Indian oceans, arguing that China is seeking to normalize its presence across the Indo-Pacific in ways that incrementally shift the strategic balance in its favor. ASPI / The Strategist (17 June 2026).
  • Japan's 2026 Defense White Paper draft (leaked in June 2026) introduces a new section on emerging modes of warfare calling for expanded UAS and AI capability and characterises the Indo-Pacific as increasingly severe, reaffirming the China threat framing. Xinhua reporting Japanese MoD draft (4 June 2026).
  • Japan's Defense of Japan 2025 white paper (the most recent published edition) assesses the China-Taiwan military balance as rapidly tilting in China's favor and flags Russia-China joint bomber and naval activities near Japan as a grave concern. U.S. Naval Institute News (15 July 2025).
  • Chatham House argues NATO still lacks a High North strategy while Russia has reinvested in Arctic basing despite Ukraine resource drag; Sweden and Finland NATO accession means seven of eight Arctic states are now Allies. Chatham House (October 2025).
  • CSIS argues the rules-based Arctic order is fast unravelling and that the Arctic power balance currently favours Russia by roughly 30% in regional bases. CSIS (January 2026).

Counter-argument

Two-simultaneous-second-fronts is a posture observation, not a strategic prediction. China's Indo-Pacific build-out is described by ASPI as incremental and access-normalising rather than crisis-driven; Russia's Arctic posture depends on the Ukraine war tempo and on whether a ceasefire frees Russian capacity for redeployment. The simultaneity may also reverse: a Russia-Ukraine ceasefire could either free Russian Arctic capacity (increasing the Arctic threat) or signal de-escalation more broadly (reducing it). If NATO concludes the Arctic threat is contained by the seven-of-eight Arctic Allies geometry, Arctic Sentry could revert to seasonal or exercise tempo by 2027, in which case the two-fronts reading collapses to one front plus an exercise. The cluster sits in the longer-range tier because the strategic conviction is genuine but the immediate decisions hinge on shorter-cycle questions.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's signals into the plan. Each follows the four-question discipline (who, by when, what, why), names a concrete action and carries a decision posture.

SI 1: Convert the 1.5% security-related band exposure map into a capacity-aware delivery plan

By end-Q3 2026, the executive committee should refresh the May cycle's exposure mapping with a capacity-aware overlay. For each of the named line items inside the 1.5% security-related band, the question to add is: what is the binding supplier-side constraint on actual delivery (skilled labour, certified suppliers, machine-tool lead time, lot-splitting law)? An exposure-only map is now insufficient; the procurement category exists, and the differentiation will sit between operators who have named the capacity ceiling and can plan around it and operators who have not. Brief the board with a quarterly tracker, not a static map.

Action: by 30 September 2026, refreshed exposure map with binding-constraint column per line item and quarterly tracker, briefed to the board.

SI 2: Position the supply-chain offer for the SAFE second wave and EDIP calls

By the end of Q3 2026, mid-tier suppliers, machine-tool firms, software vendors, certified-component manufacturers and dual-use industrial groups should have their SAFE second-wave and EDIP positioning brief in front of the executive committee. Pre-commit the unwind triggers: a SAFE second-wave delay beyond Q1 2027 or an EDIP call undersubscription would reset the timeline.

Action: by 30 September 2026, SAFE second-wave and EDIP positioning brief on the executive committee agenda, with named programme owners and filing-window milestones.

SI 3: Integrate the cyber, physical-layer and information-operations defensive posture under one named owner

Critical-infrastructure operators, large corporates with cross-border profile and any organisation in the M&A or shareholder-activism pipeline should integrate the CISO, comms and strategy defensive postures by end-Q4 2026. The signals from ANSSI, Norway, EEAS, Mandiant, Microsoft and the Harvard Law Forum point in one direction: cyber attack, physical-layer sabotage or drone incursion, GPS denial and AI-augmented influence operations are now one combined adversary surface. The risk of separating them sits in the seams. Name a single accountable owner, even if functionally cross-functional, who briefs the board quarterly with an integrated threat picture and the named ten organisations or campaigns most likely to be the next surface.

Action: by 31 December 2026, integrated risk register and named accountable owner in place, with quarterly board reporting cadence.

SI 4: Build a defence-tech capital-to-capability allocation thesis with explicit conversion-stage triggers

By the next investment committee, asset managers and corporate-strategy teams should specify a defence-tech allocation thesis broken down by capital-to-capability conversion stage: R&D, prototype, programme of record, scaled delivery. The 49-billion-dollar global 2025 capital wave (8.7 billion in Europe alone) is real; the lag between commitment and contracted programmes of record is where the conviction needs to be tested. The Pentagon's DAWG FY27 ask and Anduril's 20-billion-dollar enterprise contract anchor one demand-side bet; the SAFE and EDIP envelopes anchor a parallel European one. Resilience Decade

Asymmetric Stress

European industrial-base bottlenecks persist while grey-zone tempo escalates. Allies face simultaneously rising threat and slipping capability delivery. Political pressure to soften the 5% target builds, but the 2029 review still holds. Cross-jurisdictional disagreements on prioritisation between hard-power and resilience spend become public.

Indicators: A material grey-zone incident with attributable damage above 1 billion euros; visible Bundeswehr delivery slippage by end-2026; first SAFE second-wave disbursement delay; political calls in two or more European capitals to soften the 5% commitment.

What We Are Not Planning For

Three scenarios held out of the plan because the evidence base does not yet justify resourcing against them. Each carries the reinstatement trigger that would change that judgement.

Exclusion 1: A near-term Russian conventional escalation into a NATO Article 5 contingency

The cycle does not plan for a Russian conventional escalation that triggers an Article 5 invocation inside the 18-month horizon. The evidence base shows escalating grey-zone activity, sustained Ukraine fighting and a clear Russian doctrinal posture, but no Tier 1 or Tier 2 source argues that NATO planners or Russian decision-makers are positioning for conventional inter-state war in the planning window. Resources should sit against the documented grey-zone scenario, not the worst-case conventional one.

Reinstatement trigger: a NATO public statement raising readiness posture beyond current Eastern Sentry / Arctic Sentry levels; or two or more NATO defence ministries publicly raising their conventional-readiness assessment of Russia within a 30-day window.

Exclusion 2: A US withdrawal from the NATO commitment within the planning horizon

The cycle does not plan for a US withdrawal from NATO commitments, despite ongoing debate about burden-sharing. SIPRI's April 2026 framing confirms increasing US pressure to strengthen burden sharing within the alliance, but the operational evidence (US contributions to Arctic Sentry, FY27 DAWG request, continued bilateral defence cooperation with European Allies) does not support planning against withdrawal. Reinstating this exclusion would require a public US administration policy shift, not opinion-polling or media speculation.

Reinstatement trigger: a US administration formal notice of withdrawal from any NATO core function; or a Congressional appropriations action that removes US funding from any standing NATO mission.

Exclusion 3: A coordinated Western tech-industry refusal to supply defence customers

SIPRI's April 2026 mapping documents that foundation-model providers including OpenAI have reversed their earlier military-use restrictions; the cycle does not plan for a coordinated re-imposition of such restrictions across the major commercial AI and cloud suppliers. Individual firms may adjust policy on specific contract types, but a coordinated industry-wide step-back is not in the evidence base. Reinstating would require concerted action by at least three of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft and Amazon.

Reinstatement trigger: public announcement by three or more of the major commercial AI or cloud providers within a 90-day window that they are restricting military-use deployment or refusing specific defence customer categories.
