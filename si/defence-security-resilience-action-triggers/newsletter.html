Three numbers that make the case
0.1%. That is the share of a product's value that Chinese-origin controlled minerals have to reach before it needs an export licence. Not 0.1% of a weapons system. Of anything. Rare earth magnets sit in wind turbines, electric motors, MRI scanners, robots and hard drives, so the rule reaches a very long way past missiles. A year of diversification has barely moved the dependence: the United States still buys 71% of the rare earths it consumes from China.

530. Germany contacted roughly 298,200 young people about volunteering for military service and received about 530 commitments. That is why the constraint on European rearmament has stopped being money and become people, and why reserve service became mandatory in defined cases from 1 July.

2.1%. European and Canadian defence budgets are growing at 2.1% this year, less than half of last year's 4.6%, even as the commitments behind them hardened into dated programmes. Governments that cannot buy their way through a constraint legislate their way through it instead. That is the shift this cycle: the claims are landing on civilian capacity.
Three decisions with dates on them
Decide now
Find out what is inside your magnets, before 9 October
China's export controls come back on 10 November after a year suspended. The test is not what your product does, it is what it contains: any item where Chinese-origin controlled minerals reach 0.1% of the value needs a licence, wherever it was made. Nobody has counted how many companies outside defence are exposed, because nobody outside defence has had a reason to look. Screening takes weeks, so start in September and you have an answer before the deadline rather than after it.
Could you produce a list, by product, of what would need a licence?
Decide now
Check whether the AI exemption you assume you have actually applies
The EU AI Act exempts systems put on the market for military purposes. Most suppliers read that as exempting them. It does not: the carve-out follows the product and its purpose, not the company. Sell the same AI system to a civilian customer and to a defence ministry and you are inside the regulation in full. There is an asymmetry worth knowing: sold for civilian use and later used militarily is out of scope, both at once is in.
Has anyone actually read Article 2(3) against your product list, or are you relying on an exemption nobody has checked?
Prepare
Ask where you sit in a queue that is now set by law, not by price
France has legislated priority for military orders over civil ones and built strategic stockpiles behind it. Germany made reserve service mandatory in defined cases from 1 July, and has a draft law due next month that would route up to a thousand wounded a day through civilian hospitals. If your supplier, your workforce or your local hospital capacity sits inside one of those claims, your 2027 to 2030 lead times were set under rules that no longer apply.
Which of your critical suppliers sits in a country that can now be told who to serve first?
Built for people who will never buy a weapon: manufacturers and their procurement teams, anyone selling an AI-enabled product into more than one kind of customer, operators of ships, aircraft, ports and field assets, and investors holding companies in any of the above.
Where we might be wrong
The market is not behaving as though November is coming. Three months out, heavy rare earth prices were falling rather than firming: dysprosium down 11.6%, terbium down 7.19%. Either traders expect another extension, which is genuinely possible, or reimposition is already priced into contracts we cannot see. We may be early. The reason the recommendation survives the doubt is the asymmetry: finding out what is in your products costs a few weeks of someone's time, and not knowing on 10 November costs whatever the shipment was worth.
