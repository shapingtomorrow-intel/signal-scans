Which Defence Moves Hold in Every Future to 2030

4 November 2026 · Built on the Decision Intelligence briefing Europe Will Have to Defend Itself With Tools It Doesn't Control and its System Map · Horizons: 2030 and 2040 Uses the briefing’s scenarios and options, with evidence from the briefing and the Change Tracker; no other sources. Grades and indicator readings are drafted by analysis.
Scanned and filtered by AI. Stress-tested and judged by humans.

Scenarios are not forecasts. Their job is to show how differently the next few years could turn out, so that a plan can be built to survive any of them, and then to be watched, so that the plan changes when the evidence does.

This page does both. It takes the nine options in the briefing and runs each one through four plausible futures to 2030, plus a wild card to 2040. A move that works in every future is robust and can be committed to now. A move that works well in some futures and poorly in others is a bet, held ready with a clear trigger. A move that makes things worse in any future is fragile and should not be relied on. It then scores leading indicators on the matrix’s two axes, shows how that reading has moved since September, and turns the reading into investment priorities.

1. Four futures to 2030, and a wild card to 2040

The two axes are the system’s two biggest uncertainties, the same two the System Map identifies: whether states fill the gaps the US leaves on their own or together, and whether European defence keeps running on American cloud, software and AI or moves to European tools.

European tools · each state

National Champions

The larger European states run their own cloud, AI and planning tools and fill gaps on their own terms. Smaller states choose a champion to depend on. Control has returned to Europe in national pieces.

By 2040: A handful of national defence technology champions, loosely linked, with smaller states attached to each.

European tools · Europe together

A European Stack

Europe buys enablers jointly and runs critical defence data on European cloud under common rules. Smaller states plug into shared capability, and universities work to agreed terms.

By 2040: European defence runs on shared European infrastructure, as its electricity grid does.

American tools · each state

Bilateral Clients

Each state fills its gaps by buying American systems and services bilaterally, to keep Washington engaged. Capability arrives fast, but every state negotiates alone and long-term thinking still comes from allies.

By 2040: Europe’s defence is a set of bilateral relationships with Washington, renegotiated with each administration.

American tools · Europe together

Common Buyer, Foreign Tools

Europe buys and plans together through NATO and the EU, but mostly American tools. Purchasing is efficient and Europe speaks as one customer, but access still rests on another government’s law.

By 2040: Europe is Washington’s largest single defence customer, with the leverage and the dependence that brings.

Wild card to 2040

Access cut in a crisis

In a political dispute, a European government loses access to an American defence software, cloud or satellite service it relies on, and has to operate without it.

Built from the briefing’s Potential Surprise to 2040: a European government loses access to an American defence software or cloud service in a dispute (2027 to 2033, Low-Medium).

What the stress test shows
  1. There is a robust core, and it matches the briefing’s first three Implications. Filling the top gaps with neighbours, training staff to challenge AI advice, and writing tested exit plans hold up in all four futures.
  2. Two moves are fragile, and both are the easiest today. Adopting allied AI tools as supplied and staying with the largest cloud providers on contract terms each backfire in at least one future: where staff stop checking a foreign tool, or where European rules force a rushed move.
  3. Each bet turns on one axis. A European enablers programme pays off where Europe acts together; national or European tools and an early move to European cloud pay off where Europe’s tools become European. Buying American pays off only where each state goes it alone with American tools. The signposts in section 6 settle them.

3. What it means for the plan

Commit now

Robust: works in every future.

  • Cost the gaps and fill the top three with neighbours (Implication 1).
  • Adopt AI with a trained duty to challenge, and keep planning without it (Implication 2).
  • Map critical functions and write tested exit plans (Implication 3).

Prepare, and act on a trigger

Bets: strong in some futures, weak in others.

  • A European enablers programme: release it if the gap-filling reading moves towards Europe together.
  • National or European AI tools, and an early move to European cloud: release them if the tools reading moves towards European.
  • Buying American to keep Washington engaged: release it only if both readings move towards each state and American tools.

Do not rely on

Fragile: backfires in at least one future.

  • Adopting allied AI tools as supplied.
  • Staying with the largest cloud providers on contract terms alone.

4. Which future is gaining momentum, and how it has moved since September

Each future is a corner of the matrix, so the question “which future is gaining momentum” becomes two questions the evidence can answer: are the gaps the US leaves being filled by each state or by Europe together, and does European defence run on American or European tools? Twelve leading indicators answer them, six on each axis. Scored at each reading, they give a reading on each axis and a point on the matrix; the trail of points shows where the evidence is heading.

Who fills the gap: still each state, but moving−7 in November. National purchases, bilateral deals and national systems still lead; joint projects, a proposed enablers instrument and neighbours’ joint plans are pulling the reading towards the centre.
Whose tools: a little towards American−13 in November. NATO’s clearance of AWS and the spread of Maven pull towards American tools; Dutch rules and Airbus’s move pull the other way, and the EU’s cloud law is still a proposal.
Leading future: none yetNo future has held a corner for two readings. The reading sits between Bilateral Clients and Common Buyer, Foreign Tools.
What would settle itA funded European enablers instrument and joint purchases move the reading towards Europe together; large bilateral US purchases move it back. The EU cloud law adopted with its sovereignty levels moves it towards European tools; an exemption for defence moves it towards American.

How to read it: each circle is one reading, hollow for earlier readings and filled for this briefing. The arrows run from each reading to the next, so they show which way the evidence moved. The pale band (the no-lead zone) marks readings within 15 points of an axis, where no future can lead. Hover over a point for its reading.

How the reading is scored
  1. Each indicator is marked Moving (+1), Pending (+½), Not yet (0) or Against (−1), on the evidence published by the date of the reading.
  2. Each carries a weight: 3 if it would settle its axis on its own, 2 if it is strong evidence, 1 if it supports.
  3. An axis reading is the weighted total for that axis, scaled from −100 (each state, or American) to +100 (Europe together, or European).
  4. A future leads when the reading sits in its corner, at least 15 points from both axes, for two readings running.
  5. A shift is a move of 10 points or more on either axis between readings. It is flagged and reviewed in the next quarterly briefing.
  6. A signpost reached (section 6) overrides the score and triggers its decision directly.

7. How the monitoring runs over time

Every weekSignal Scans attach new evidence to each indicator and signpost, including weak signals that do not yet fit any future.
Every monthThe Change Tracker rescores each indicator, the two axis readings are recalculated, and the trail on the matrix gains a point.
Every quarterThe Decision Intelligence briefing reviews any flagged shift, and redraws the scenarios, the System Map and this stress test.
When a signpost is reachedAction Triggers fire, releasing the bet prepared for it and naming the decision it changes.

8. Running it with your own plan

The options above are the briefing’s. The real value comes from replacing them with the organisation’s own plans and asking the same question of each: does it hold in every future?

BeforeThe organisation shares its capability, technology, research and partnership plans for the next few years; we grade each against the scenarios and the evidence base.
Session, 2 hours20 minutes in the four futures; 40 minutes in which each group lives in one future and answers “it is 2030: what do we wish we had done in 2026?”; 40 minutes testing the plan against the matrix; 20 minutes agreeing the robust core and the trigger for each bet. The session is designed to surface and challenge the assumptions behind current plans.
AfterThe agreed matrix, indicators and signposts join the programme, and the monitoring cycle above keeps them current.
About this page. The four futures, the nine options and the indicators all come from the briefing of 4 November 2026; the wild card comes from one of its Potential Surprises to 2040; no new sources have been added. Each grade, each indicator status and each weight is the analysis’s judgement from the cited evidence and is meant to be challenged; the axis readings are then calculated by the fixed rule in section 4. The readings for 4 September and 2 October, the dates of the monthly Change Trackers since the August briefing, were scored after the event from evidence in this briefing’s base published by each date, so an indicator marked “Not yet” at an earlier reading may simply have had no evidence in this base by then; in a live programme each is scored when it falls. The 2040 lines extend each future beyond the briefing’s horizon and are offered for discussion.
