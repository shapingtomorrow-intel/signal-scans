Nobody Is Recruiting Their Way Out of This Any More
Germany offered its pensioners tax-free pay to keep working. Around 9,000 took it.
Most organisations still treat a workforce shortage as a hiring problem: pay more, widen the search, open a route. Here are the two things almost nobody is writing about. Paying more was tested this year at national scale and barely moved anyone, so the constraint is conditions rather than price. And the countries getting results have stopped recruiting altogether. They are counting, converting and rationing the people already inside their borders, and setting the number and the price by published rule.

Three numbers that make the case
9,000. How many extra people kept working past pension age after Germany exempted the first EUR 2,000 a month of their earnings from tax, from 1 January this year. Set that against a demographic loss of 470,000 in the same year and it is a rounding error. This is the cleanest test anyone has run of the assumption underneath most workforce plans, which is that the missing people can be bought back at a price. They cannot, or at least not at this one. What decides whether an older worker stays is the shape of the job: the hours, the role, whether the employer will have them. Germany reached for the lever a treasury controls, and the lever that matters sits with employers.

337,917. Regularised workers on Spain's social security rolls at the end of August, from nearly 1.2 million applications against an expectation of around 500,000. Foreign nationals supplied 71% of the year's growth in contributors and all of August's seasonally adjusted rise, while affiliation among Spanish nationals fell. Nobody new was admitted. The state grew its workforce by granting legal status to people already living and working there, which is now the largest single source of new contributors in a mature European economy. The catch is in the design: the permits run one year, and they convert to ordinary residence only through employment or registered job search.

Zero. Roughly the number of net new jobs the US economy now needs each month to hold unemployment steady, down from about 250,000 in 2023, because the workforce has stopped growing. A payroll print that read as a stall three years ago now reads as adequate. And the benchmark is unstable in a way the headline never shows: population revisions alone move it by up to 66,000 jobs a month, around 60% of the average monthly gain, while contemporaneous estimates for this year differ by about 50,000. Anyone reading jobs data as a demand signal is comparing this year's numerator against an older year's denominator.
Three decisions with dates on them
Decide now
Count how many of your people hold a right to work that expires
Most workforce plans track headcount and turnover. Almost none track the date each person's permission runs out, because that sits in an immigration file rather than in the plan. This cycle made those dates the binding constraint: Spain's new permits run one year, Europe's temporary protection scheme now ends in March 2028, and a UK shortage-list place lasts eighteen months. A lapsed renewal does not cost you a headcount, it costs you a shift, a ward or a line. The count itself takes a week and tells you whether you have a workforce problem or a calendar problem.
Could you produce that list of dates this month, and would anything on it fall inside your 2027 plan?
Prepare
Write the domestic training plan before you need the visa
Britain has made shortage-list access conditional on an employer plan to maximise use of the domestic workforce, granted for eighteen months, to 28 of 82 occupations and none at full access, with the volume behind that door falling from roughly 10,000 visas a year to about 4,000. The plan is the part nobody has drafted, because nobody has been asked for one before. Employers who start writing when the shortage bites will be drafting a training commitment under time pressure, against competitors who filed one already.
If the window opened next quarter, would you have a document to file, or a project to start?

Say out loud what jobs number your forecasts treat as normal
The US economy now needs roughly zero net new jobs a month to hold unemployment steady, down from about 250,000 in 2023, because the workforce has stopped growing. Worse for planning, the benchmark itself is unstable: population revisions alone move it by up to 66,000 jobs a month, around 60% of the average monthly gain. Almost no forecast states the threshold it is using, so a number that moves by more than its own signal is being carried as an unwritten assumption underneath hiring plans, capacity decisions and rate expectations.

Where we might be wrong
None of this is irreversible, and one of the four is administrative. Japan's 50,000 ceiling is a pandemic-era planning figure held unchanged after an under-delivering first phase, and it could be lifted with a stroke; a government panel opened on 7 August with a basic policy due before 31 March 2027. The fiscal arithmetic behind Britain's wage floor is contested by three of our sources, and the Migration Advisory Committee's own administrative records are replacing the modelling's assumptions with observed data that cut against them. Spain's gain may flatter: Funcas shows the underlying national labour market decelerating beneath the recorded rise, and one-year permits are not a permanent addition to supply. We have also already been wrong once this cycle, in public. Our July briefing judged the domestic participation reserve close to exhausted; the evidence since says it was not, and the September edition grades that finding BROKEN against our own name. The recommendations survive the doubt because they cost little and fail safe: counting expiry dates and writing down an assumption are useful whichever way the rules go.
