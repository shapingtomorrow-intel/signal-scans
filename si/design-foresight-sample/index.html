The cycle's central tension: design and foresight re-forming as one field, with trained human judgement the contested centre.

Executive Synthesis

Design and foresight are converging into a single contested field, and the terms of that convergence are being set now rather than settled. For two decades the two disciplines ran in parallel: foresight produced scenarios and horizon scans for strategy functions, while design produced the artefacts, services and experiences that strategy then commissioned. That separation is dissolving. A reflexive history of the field describes the two practices as a single interdisciplinary territory in which design has always been about the future and foresight has always needed design's craft to make futures legible (IJDAS, June 2025). The Journal of Futures Studies has gone further and named the fusion outright, proposing Design Foresight as a unified methodology (Journal of Futures Studies, December 2025). This is the cycle's central tension: a field is re-forming, and four forces are pulling at its centre at once.

This is the inaugural cycle on this topic, so it establishes the baseline rather than reporting a delta. The four forces re-shaping the field are distinct and should not be collapsed into one. The first is the collapse and renewal of design practice itself: design thinking, the dominant innovation method of the 2010s, has lost its commanding position, with the firm that popularised it, IDEO, reporting revenue down from roughly $300m in 2019 to under $100m in 2023 and a 32% workforce reduction (IJDAS, June 2025). Designers are not retreating; they are moving toward speculative design, design fiction and experiential futures, practices that make a possible future feel real before it exists (designboom, May 2026). The second force is the professionalisation of foresight: governments and intergovernmental bodies are embedding foresight as a standing institutional function rather than an occasional project, from the EU's annual Strategic Foresight Report (European Commission, September 2025) to OECD guidelines for anticipatory governance (OECD, May 2025). The third is the shift in education: UNESCO now frames Futures Literacy as a foundational twenty-first-century capability and is building a Global Anticipatory Policy Coalition (UNESCO, December 2025). The fourth force is artificial intelligence, which most foresight practitioners now use in their workflow (OECD and World Economic Forum, November 2025). AI is a major force this cycle, but it is one of four, and the report reads it as a driver of the field's re-formation, not as the story.

A field is re-forming, and four forces are pulling at its centre at once.

The spine of this cycle is the design-practice story, not the AI story. Clusters 1 and 2 carry the AI angle and are ranked first because their effects land soonest, but they are best read as instruments inside a larger argument: AI is reshaping how design and foresight produce and value futures, and the field's response to it is what matters. The surprising insight for leadership is that the most credible practitioners are not treating AI's defining flaw, hallucination, as something to suppress. They are treating it as a method. Three of the user-supplied papers in this report's evidence base formalise hallucination as a deliberate creative input, reframed through human Trained Judgement into hypercreative signals (Journal of Futures Studies, June 2025). The same body of evidence sets the binding constraint: large language models average the work of many sources, so used naively they narrow the range of futures a field can imagine. This report's posture is that the design-and-foresight field is being re-formed under contested terms, that trained human judgement is the asset that decides whether the re-formation widens or narrows the futures available, and that leadership should treat the choice of method, talent and curriculum as the live decision, not the choice of tool.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The single most consequential assumption in this synthesis is that the design-and-foresight field is genuinely re-forming as one discipline, rather than design and foresight simply borrowing each other's vocabulary while their institutions, buyers and career paths stay separate. The evidence for convergence is still concentrated in academic journals, a handful of consultancies and a small set of speculative-design studios. If, over the next twelve to eighteen months, the major foresight buyers (governments, corporate strategy functions) continue to commission scenario work and design work through separate procurement routes, and if no shared professional body, accreditation or named job title emerges, then this report has over-read a publishing trend as a structural shift. The disconfirming signal to watch is procurement and hiring: convergence is real only if "design foresight" or an equivalent appears as a named role or a named commissioning category, not only as a paper title.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Adopt an explicit method for using AI in futures work, with human judgement named as the control point. The field's leading practitioners have moved from debating whether to use AI to specifying how; an organisation without a stated method is defaulting to naive use, which is the homogenising case.
  2. Decide where the organisation sits on the design-practice shift, from design thinking toward design foresight. Design thinking's contraction is structural, not cyclical; treating the speculative and experiential turn as a fringe interest leaves the organisation buying a method whose value has visibly diluted.
  3. Treat futures literacy as a capability to build, not a service to buy. Foresight is professionalising and AI is raising the floor on routine analysis; the durable advantage moves to organisations that can train AI-critical judgement internally rather than rent it.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Signal Clusters

1. Hallucination as Method: AI's Creative Turn in Foresight

Near-Term

The most credible response to AI's defining flaw is not to suppress it but to method-ise it, and any design or foresight function without a stated method is already behind. Hallucination, the tendency of a large language model to generate plausible but unfounded content, is being reframed by leading practitioners from a defect to be filtered out into a creative input to be staged and harvested. This forces design-and-foresight leadership to confront a near-term positioning question: a team that uses AI naively gets faster but more generic output, while a team that uses it through an explicit, judgement-led process can get genuinely divergent material. The difference is method, not tool, and method is a choice an organisation can make this cycle. The cluster is rated Near-Term rather than Immediate because the choice does not yet change the corporate structure, but it changes competitive position quickly: the quality gap between method-led and naive practice is already visible to discerning buyers.

The Gen-AI Hypercreativity Process: hallucination staged through human judgement

Process as formalised in Draeger and Bevolo, Journal of Futures Studies, June 2025. Three of the four steps are human judgement work.

  • Practitioners have formalised hallucination into a repeatable four-step Gen-AI Hypercreativity Process, in which the machine prompts and the human applies critical, liminal and creative thinking to turn errors into hypercreative signals (Journal of Futures Studies, June 2025).
  • A hybrid leisure-futures method merges generative-AI scenario generation with the constructivist tradition of design futures, deliberately including hallucinations to inspire what the authors call liminal hypercreativity in a forecast (Research in Hospitality Management, March 2025).
  • Most foresight professionals already use AI to scan for signals and support scenario work, and a smaller but growing group uses it as a creative or analytical partner in systems mapping and scenario design, on a survey of 167 practitioners across 55 countries (OECD and World Economic Forum, November 2025).
  • Peer-reviewed work on AI in education frames hallucination as dualistic, a facilitator of divergent thinking as well as an inhibitor, supporting the case that the value depends on how the output is handled rather than on the model alone (Information Systems Frontiers, May 2026).

The deeper point for leadership is where the method locates the human contribution. The hypercreativity process is not a workflow for getting more out of a model; it is a discipline for keeping the practitioner in command of a process the model could otherwise dominate. The machine prompt is the cheap, fast, replaceable step. The three steps that follow, critical, liminal and creative thinking, are where a trained practitioner decides what the output means, which hallucinations are noise and which are signal, and how the material becomes a usable scenario. Read this way, AI does not threaten the foresight practitioner so much as expose which practitioners were doing the easy part of the job. A function that has built genuine interpretive depth gains a fast, tireless generator of raw material. A function that was mostly doing the scanning finds that the scanning is now nearly free, and that the value it must defend is the part it may have under-invested in. This is why the report frames AI as a driver of the field's re-formation rather than as the headline: the technology is the occasion, but the decision it forces is about human capability.

The liminal step is where judgement does its work

The hypercreativity process places its decisive move in the third step, liminal thinking, where the practitioner abductively reorganises raw machine output into clusters and trends, deliberately exercising the suspension of disbelief that fiction requires (Journal of Futures Studies, June 2025). This report's reading is that the step is not a technical operation but a design act: it is where a human decides which hallucinations are noise and which are signal. That is why the method is hard to commoditise. The machine can generate the candidate material in seconds; deciding what it means stays human work, and it stays craft.

Counter-argument

The reframing of hallucination as method is promising but still rests on a thin evidence base. Much of it comes from a single connected group of authors and from small applied trials rather than large controlled studies, and the same practitioner survey that records enthusiasm also records that many users find AI output shallow or derivative, more remix than revelation (World Economic Forum, December 2025). It is possible that the hypercreativity claim works in carefully facilitated workshops but does not survive contact with routine commercial use, where time pressure pushes teams back toward naive prompting. The method may be real and still be hard to scale.

Lead check: This reads as a genuine methodological turn only if, over the next twelve months, the hypercreativity process or an equivalent staged method is taken up and published by practitioner groups outside the originating authors. If no independent group adopts a comparable explicit method and AI use stays dominated by open-ended prompting, the cluster is over-stated.

2. The Homogenisation Risk to Plural Futures

Immediate

If AI use in design and foresight is left to default settings, it will quietly narrow the range of futures an organisation can imagine, and that is a present risk rather than a distant one. The core purpose of both disciplines is plurality: holding open multiple, genuinely different futures so that strategy is tested against more than one. Large language models work by averaging across their training data, so naive use pulls every team toward the same statistically central scenario. This cluster ranks Immediate because the homogenising effect is already measurable and because it directly attacks the proposition design and foresight exist to deliver; an organisation that does not actively design against it is degrading its own core product.

  • An experimental study found that generative AI raises individual creativity but that AI-enabled stories are measurably more similar to each other than stories written by humans alone, increasing individual output at the cost of collective novelty (PNAS Nexus, July 2024).
  • Foresight practitioners surveyed by the OECD and World Economic Forum flag the dominance of English-language and Western training data, which can distort global perspectives and obscure weak signals emerging from other regions and cultures (OECD and World Economic Forum, November 2025).
  • Research argues that wide LLM use exerts a measurable homogenising pressure on human expression and thought, narrowing the range of ideas in circulation rather than only the range of AI output (arXiv, August 2025).
  • A wide-angle horizon scan of the field records a parallel and opposite pull, the shift away from expert-centric foresight toward participatory, citizen-driven and Global-South methods that deliberately widen whose futures are heard (ShapingTomorrow, July 2025).

The homogenisation risk deserves to be stated plainly because it is easy to miss. It does not announce itself as a failure. A team using AI naively will feel more productive, will generate scenario sets faster, and will produce documents that look as varied as ever. What degrades is not the volume or the surface variety but the underlying spread: the futures cluster more tightly around the statistically central case, because that is what an averaging model returns. For a discipline whose entire value rests on confronting decision-makers with genuinely different futures, this is a quiet erosion of the product itself. It is the design-and-foresight equivalent of a slow loss of resolution, invisible until someone compares the output against what an unaided, diverse group of humans would have produced. The report ranks this cluster Immediate not because the effect is dramatic but because it is silent, cumulative and aimed at the core proposition. An organisation cannot decide later to have had plural futures; the plurality is either designed in at the point of use or it is lost.

Plurality has a geography, and AI has an accent

The homogenisation risk is not only statistical, it is cultural. Practitioners surveyed by the OECD and World Economic Forum flag that AI's English-language and Western data bias can obscure weak signals from other regions (OECD and World Economic Forum, November 2025). Against this, a counter-current is visible in the field: participatory, citizen-driven and Global-South methods that widen whose futures get heard (Modern Diplomacy, June 2025). Taken together, the sources suggest the field is being pulled toward the centre by its tools and toward the margins by its values, at the same time.

Counter-argument

Homogenisation is real but it may be more designable-against than the alarm suggests. Recent research shows that targeted prompting interventions, combining chain-of-thought reasoning with diverse personas, can produce idea sets that outperform human baselines on diversity, and a separate line of work proposes structure-aware diversity pursuit as an explicit safety strategy (arXiv, February 2026). On this reading the risk is not intrinsic to AI but intrinsic to lazy use, which makes it a craft problem rather than a structural one. If the field absorbs these techniques quickly, the homogenisation cluster is a transitional concern, not a permanent one.

3. Foresight Professionalises and Industrialises

Immediate

Foresight is moving from a craft practised by specialists into an institutionalised, scaled function, and that changes what an organisation should buy, build and hire. Governments are embedding foresight as a standing capability, consultancies are productising it, and AI is raising the floor on routine scanning. The immediate consequence is that environmental scanning and first-pass scenario drafting are becoming commodities, while the scarce and defensible asset becomes trained interpretive judgement: the ability to decide which signals matter and what they mean. Leadership should treat this as a near-term structural shift in the value chain, not a slow trend.

Where value concentrates as foresight industrialises

Analyst schematic, drawing on OECD anticipatory-governance guidance and BCG foresight research, 2025. Illustrative of direction, not a measured ranking.

  • The OECD has issued guidelines for anticipatory governance, the systematic integration of foresight, experimentation and innovation into policymaking, signalling foresight's shift from occasional project to standing public-sector function (OECD, May 2025).
  • The European Commission's annual Strategic Foresight Report, the 2025 edition titled Resilience 2.0, institutionalises foresight at the centre of EU policymaking through a participative cross-sectoral process (European Commission, September 2025).
  • Reflexive work in the field argues that LLMs can generate signals but cannot build a foresight matrix tool, and that human Trained Judgement remains indispensable to the analysis (IJDAS, June 2025).
  • Major consultancies position foresight as a scalable service, with BCG reporting that organisations with mature foresight capabilities show performance advantages over peers and arguing that foresight must become an internal competency rather than an outsourced project (Boston Consulting Group, January 2025).

Industrialisation is not only a story about volume; it is a story about where craft survives. As foresight scales, the discipline divides into a layer that can be standardised and a layer that resists it. The standardisable layer is large: horizon scans, trend databases, signal libraries, first-pass scenario drafts, the briefing decks that summarise them. AI and scaled consultancies are both moving into that layer fast, and an organisation that defines its foresight capability as producing those artefacts is defining itself out of a defensible position. The layer that resists standardisation is the one this report's evidence keeps returning to: choosing which signals matter for this organisation and this decision, designing the method and the frame through which raw material becomes a judgement, and facilitating the human conversation that turns a scenario into a commitment. That layer is design work as much as analytical work, and it is where the professionalisation of foresight and the reinvention of design genuinely meet. The strategic reading for leadership is that industrialisation is good news for organisations that have invested in interpretive depth and method design, and a quiet threat to those whose foresight function is, in practice, a scanning service.

What a machine cannot build: the tool, not just the output

A useful distinction runs through the field's reflexive literature: an LLM can generate the contents of a foresight exercise, the signals and scenarios, but it cannot construct the foresight matrix tool that organises them (IJDAS, June 2025). On the available evidence this report reads the professionalisation of foresight as a shift in where the defensible work sits: away from producing scenario content, which AI and scaled consultancies increasingly supply, and toward designing the methods, frames and tools through which content becomes a decision. That is design work, and it is one of the clearest places the design-and-foresight fields genuinely fuse rather than merely borrow language.

Counter-argument

The industrialisation reading can be overstated. Foresight has been declared an emerging profession repeatedly over several decades without ever consolidating into one: there is still no single accrediting body, no agreed certification, and the academic infrastructure remains small and unevenly distributed (Ross Dawson, accessed May 2026). Government foresight units are also vulnerable to political cycles and budget pressure, and a wave of institutional enthusiasm can recede. What looks like industrialisation may be a high-water mark of attention rather than a durable structural change.

4. Design's Reinvention: From Design Thinking to Design Foresight

Near-Term

Design's dominant method of the last fifteen years has lost its commanding position, and design functions that treat the speculative and experiential turn as a fringe interest are buying into a diluted proposition. Design thinking has not vanished, but its champion firm has contracted sharply and the discourse has been diluted by superficial adoption. The renewal is visible: design is moving toward speculative design, design fiction and experiential futures, and toward an explicit fusion with foresight now named as a discipline. For design and foresight leadership this is a near-term positioning decision: which capabilities to build, which habits to retire, and how to describe the function's value over the next twelve months.

  • Design thinking is in a documented crisis, with the firm that popularised it, IDEO, reporting revenue down from roughly $300m in 2019 to under $100m in 2023 and a 32% workforce reduction, used in the field's reflexive history as the marker of the method's decline (IJDAS, June 2025).
  • The critique that design thinking became innovation theatre, superficially adopted and weakly implemented, is long-running, and recent reflexive scholarship documents its convergence with the commercial contraction of design consultancy (IJDAS, Connecting the (Distant) Dots, Jun 2025).
  • The Journal of Futures Studies has named the convergence outright, proposing Design Foresight as a unified methodology bridging futurization, widening the set of possible futures, and de-futurization, selecting feasible solutions (Journal of Futures Studies, December 2025).
  • Speculative and experiential practice is moving from gallery provocation toward applied strategic use, with studios such as Superflux working to make a possible future feel real enough that organisations begin building toward it (designboom, May 2026).

It is worth being precise about what is ending and what is beginning, because the two are easily confused. What is ending is design thinking's claim to be the master method of innovation: the empathise-define-ideate-prototype-test sequence, championed from the 2000s as a universal problem-solving engine and sold at scale by specialist consultancies. That claim has not survived contact with a decade of superficial adoption and a hard commercial downturn at its leading firm. What is beginning is not a replacement master method but a redirection of design's energy toward the future tense. Speculative design and design fiction use the craft of designers, the artefact, the prototype, the rendered scene, to make alternative futures concrete enough to argue with. Experiential futures goes further, staging futures as situations that participants inhabit. These are not new inventions; speculative practice has roots two decades deep, and experiential futures has been developed steadily since the late 2000s. What is new is their movement from the gallery and the studio toward applied strategic use, and their formal convergence with foresight under a named methodology. For a design or foresight function, the practical question is not whether to abandon design thinking wholesale, since some of its participatory habits remain useful, but whether to keep describing the function's value in design thinking's terms while the market's attention and the discipline's most interesting work have moved on. The report's reading is that the reinvention is real, that it favours design functions willing to build speculative and experiential capability, and that it is the clearest place to see design and foresight becoming one field rather than two.

Experiential futures: design's distinctive contribution to foresight

Foresight's traditional output is the written scenario; design's contribution is to make a future perceptible. Experiential futures, developed over nearly two decades, combines professional design craft with foresight methods so that participants experience a future rather than only read about it (Institute for the Future, accessed May 2026). This report's reading is that this is the clearest answer to what design brings to the converged field: not faster scenarios, but futures that are felt and therefore acted on. Studios such as Superflux build precisely this, making a possible future real enough that organisations start moving toward it (designboom, May 2026).

Counter-argument

The decline of design thinking can be read less as a collapse and more as a maturation. On this view the method did not fail; it succeeded so completely that it was absorbed into in-house product and service teams, which is why standalone consultancies that sold it have contracted (BLINK UX, August 2025). If that is right, the speculative and experiential turn is a specialist enrichment at the edges rather than a wholesale reinvention, and design thinking remains the working method of most practitioners. The reinvention may be narrower than the discourse implies.

Lead check: This reads as a genuine reinvention of design practice only if speculative, fictional and experiential methods, or a named design-foresight method, appear in mainstream design briefs and job descriptions over the next twelve to eighteen months. If they remain confined to academic journals and a small set of studios while commercial design work stays organised around design-thinking sprints, the cluster is over-stated.

5. Futures Literacy as Curriculum: Teaching AI-Critical Foresight

Longer-Range

The durable advantage in design and foresight is shifting from access to method toward the capacity to teach AI-critical judgement, and that is a multi-year structural factor to track. As AI commoditises routine analysis and foresight professionalises, the scarce input becomes people who can interrogate AI output rather than consume it. Education is responding: futures literacy is being framed as a foundational capability and tested pedagogies show AI's flaws can be turned into teachable competencies. For leadership this is a Longer-Range cluster, but it is the one that determines whether the talent pipeline supports the field's re-formation or constrains it.

  • A five-step prompt-scaffolding pedagogy tested with 99 participants converts LLM hallucination from a limitation into a teachable futures-literacy asset, positioning students as responsible architects of AI-assisted futures rather than passive consumers (IJDAS, June 2025).
  • UNESCO frames Futures Literacy as a foundational capability of the twenty-first century and is building a Global Anticipatory Policy Coalition, drawing on a network of 35 UNESCO Chairs in Futures Studies (UNESCO, December 2025).
  • Peer-reviewed work documents design fiction used as a critical pedagogy for exploring technology futures, linking design practice directly to futures education (Frontiers in Education, February 2026).
  • The World Economic Forum estimates that 39% of key workforce skills will change by 2030, with analytical thinking, creativity and resilience among the fastest-rising, and 59% of the workforce needing training by 2030 (World Economic Forum, January 2025).

The logic that makes this a Longer-Range cluster is also what makes it the most consequential one to get right. The other four clusters describe changes the field is living through now; this one describes whether the field will have the people to navigate them in five and ten years. If AI commoditises routine analysis, if foresight industrialises, and if design reinvents itself around speculative and experiential practice, then the binding constraint on all three becomes the supply of practitioners who can do the non-commoditisable work: interrogating AI rather than consuming it, designing methods rather than running templates, and holding open plural futures rather than accepting the averaged one. That capability is not innate and it is not quick to build. It is a taught competency, and the evidence shows the teaching is being attempted seriously, with tested pedagogies that treat AI's flaws as material to learn from rather than hazards to avoid. The strategic risk is a mismatch of clocks: the field is re-forming over a two-to-three-year horizon, while the curriculum and the talent pipeline move over a five-to-ten-year one. An organisation that waits for the pipeline to mature before acting will be hiring into a shortage. The report's posture is that futures literacy should be tracked now, as a capability risk, precisely because the cost of acting is low today and the cost of having ignored it compounds.

Weak signals

  • Weak signal A dedicated Journal of Futures Studies special issue on the future of futures thinking in design education suggests the design-foresight-education triangle is consolidating into a research field of its own (Journal of Futures Studies, December 2025). This would gain weight if a recognised university launched a named design-foresight degree.
  • Weak signal Participatory and citizen-facing futures programmes are framing futures capability as a civic competency, not only a professional one (Nesta, accessed May 2026). This would gain weight if futures literacy entered a national school curriculum as a stated learning outcome.

Teaching the flaw on purpose

The sharpest move in the curriculum evidence is pedagogical rather than technical. Rather than banning AI or teaching around its hallucination problem, the scaffolding approach teaches students to engage hallucination deliberately, deconstructing problems and directing the model through a structured five-step sequence (IJDAS, June 2025). This report's reading is that this is what AI-critical foresight means in practice: not suspicion of the tool and not surrender to it, but a taught competency in using a flawed instrument well. It is also the clearest curricular expression of the report's central claim, that trained judgement, not the tool, is the asset worth building.

Counter-argument

The curriculum story is genuine but its scale is easy to overstate. The tested pedagogy rests on small student trials in a single programme, and the academic infrastructure for futures and foresight remains modest, with a relatively short list of degree programmes worldwide and uneven geographic spread (Ross Dawson, accessed May 2026). Institutional reform in education is slow, and high-level framing of futures literacy as foundational does not by itself change what most students are taught. The curriculum shift may be real in intent and marginal in reach for several years.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's signals into a plan for a design-and-foresight function. Each names the move, an owner, a horizon and a decision posture. The opportunities in this cycle are expressed here as Prepare and Decide postures, not in a separate section.

SI 1: Adopt a written method for AI in futures work, with human judgement as the control point

The leadership of every design or foresight function should agree, by the end of Q3 2026, a one-page method that specifies how AI is used in futures work and where human judgement overrides the machine. The evidence is that naive prompting produces faster but more generic and more homogenised output, while a staged process can produce genuinely divergent material; the field's leading practitioners have already moved from debating use to specifying it. Without a stated method the function defaults to the homogenising case, degrading the plurality that is its reason to exist. The owner is the function head, not "the team", and the deliverable is a documented method, not an aspiration.

Action: the function head adopts and circulates a one-page AI-in-foresight method, naming the human control points, by end-Q3 2026.

SI 2: Commission a deliberate plurality check on the function's AI-assisted output

The function head should commission, before the next annual planning round, a review of whether the function's AI-assisted scenarios and signals are narrowing toward a statistically central case. The homogenisation effect is measurable and it directly attacks the proposition that design and foresight exist to hold open multiple futures. Recent research shows the effect is partially designable-against through diverse-persona and chain-of-thought prompting, so the review should both diagnose and prescribe. The cost of inaction is a function that looks productive while quietly delivering a single future dressed as several.

Action: the function head commissions a plurality audit of AI-assisted output, with prompting-technique recommendations, before the next annual planning round.

SI 3: Decide the function's position on the design-thinking to design-foresight shift

Design and foresight leadership should decide, within this planning cycle, where the function sits on the move from design thinking toward speculative, experiential and design-foresight practice, and should name the capabilities to build and the habits to retire. Design thinking's contraction is structural rather than cyclical, and a major consultancy and a peer-reviewed journal have both now named a combined Design Foresight method. A function that treats the shift as a fringe interest is selling a proposition whose value has visibly diluted; one that repositions deliberately can claim the renewed territory. The deliverable is a stated capability plan, agreed at function-leadership level.

Action: design-and-foresight leadership agrees a written capability plan naming speculative and experiential methods to build and design-thinking practices to retire, within this planning cycle.

SI 4: Track the futures-literacy talent pipeline as a multi-year capability risk

Strategy and people leadership should add the futures-literacy pipeline to the capability risks reviewed each cycle, monitoring whether the supply of people who can interrogate AI rather than consume it is keeping pace with the function's needs. As routine analysis commoditises, AI-critical interpretive judgement becomes the scarce input, and the academic and training infrastructure that produces it is still modest and uneven. No capital commitment is required this cycle, but the pipeline should be tracked so the organisation is not caught short when it matters. The owner is the people-and-capability lead, reporting into the standing cycle review.

Action: the people-and-capability lead adds futures-literacy pipeline health to the standing cycle capability review, with the first read tabled at the next cycle.

Scenario Matrix

Two uncertainties will shape the design-and-foresight field over the next two to five years more than any other. The first axis is how AI is used in futures work: predominantly as a naive efficiency tool, or predominantly through judgement-led methods that stage it. The second axis is whether trained interpretive judgement is built as a broad capability or stays scarce and concentrated in a few elite practitioners. The four scenarios below are planning aids, not forecasts.

The Craft Renaissance

AI is used through judgement-led methods and the capacity to apply that judgement is widely built. Design and foresight converge into a confident shared discipline: AI handles routine scanning, practitioners across many organisations are trained to stage hallucination and audit for plurality, and speculative and experiential methods are mainstream. The field's value proposition is clear and defensible because the scarce input, trained judgement, is no longer scarce by accident but cultivated by design. Buyers can tell good futures work from generic output, which raises the floor on quality and rewards method. This is the environment the report's posture is built to reach: it requires both an explicit-method choice and a serious investment in curriculum and capability.

Early indicators: named design-foresight roles and degrees appear; practitioner bodies publish staged AI methods; plurality audits become a routine commission.

The Elite Atelier

AI is used through judgement-led methods, but the capacity to apply that judgement stays scarce and concentrated. A small number of high-craft studios and senior practitioners produce genuinely plural, well-staged futures work, command premium fees and set the methodological standard. Everyone else uses AI more naively because they lack the trained judgement to do otherwise. The field bifurcates: excellent work at the top, generic work in the middle, and a widening quality gap between them. Design and foresight remain credible disciplines but as a craft guild rather than a broad profession. The risk is that the field's best thinking becomes inaccessible to the organisations that most need it.

Early indicators: foresight fees polarise; elite studios grow while generalist capability stalls; training supply stays flat against rising demand.

The Great Flattening

AI is used predominantly as a naive efficiency tool and judgement stays scarce. This is the homogenisation case at field scale. Organisations adopt AI to produce scenarios faster and cheaper, but without staged methods or plurality discipline the output converges toward a statistically central future dressed up as several. Foresight looks busy and productive while quietly losing its core function, holding open genuinely different futures. Design follows, with speculative and experiential practice squeezed out as unbillable. The field does not collapse; it hollows. Its outputs still exist but they stop changing decisions, because every organisation is being shown more or less the same future.

Early indicators: scenario sets across organisations visibly converge; speculative-design budgets are cut; AI-generated foresight is bought on price.

The Broad Floor

Judgement is widely built but AI use stays mostly naive. Education and training succeed in spreading futures literacy, so many people can think critically about the future, but the field has not converged on staged methods for using AI well, so practitioners apply good judgement to weak machine inputs. The result is a capable workforce partly wasted: human judgement is present but spends its energy correcting generic AI output rather than building on strong staged material. This is a stable but underperforming environment. It is also the most recoverable, because the hard part, trained people, is already in place; the missing piece is method, which can be adopted quickly once the field agrees on it.

Early indicators: futures-literacy curricula spread; practitioner complaints about shallow AI output rise; no shared staged method gains traction.

What We Are Not Planning For

Three developments are deliberately deprioritised this cycle. Each is named so the judgement is visible and can be revisited.

The full automation of foresight

This report does not plan for a near-term world in which AI replaces the foresight practitioner outright. The evidence base, including the practitioner-authored papers that are most enthusiastic about AI, is consistent that machines generate material but cannot perform the interpretive, liminal and decision-framing work that makes foresight useful. The OECD and World Economic Forum survey records AI as an augmentation, valued for time-saving, not as a substitute for judgement. Treating full automation as a live planning case would misdirect attention away from the real decision, which is method and capability.

Reinstate if: a credible practitioner study demonstrates AI producing decision-grade foresight, including method design and interpretation, without material human input.

A single dominant foresight platform or standard

The report does not plan for the field consolidating around one tool, platform or accredited standard within the cycle horizon. Foresight has resisted such consolidation for decades, the academic infrastructure remains plural and dispersed, and the current moment is one of competing methods rather than a converging one. Planning as though a single standard were imminent would push the function toward a premature bet. The productive posture is to invest in adaptable judgement and explicit method, both of which survive whichever tools rise or fall.

Reinstate if: a major intergovernmental body or professional association launches an accredited foresight standard with visible cross-sector adoption.

A wholesale collapse of design as a discipline

The contraction of design thinking and the downturn at its champion firm are real, but the report does not plan for design itself disappearing as a function. Design is visibly reinventing rather than ending: speculative, experiential and design-foresight practice are growing, and major consultancies are actively marketing combined methods. The signal is reconfiguration, not extinction. Treating design's decline as terminal would lead the organisation to under-invest in exactly the capabilities the renewal rewards.

Reinstate if: speculative, experiential and design-foresight practice show sustained contraction alongside design thinking, with no successor practice gaining commercial traction.

Discussion Points for the Leadership Team

  1. If routine scanning and first-pass scenario drafting are commoditising, which parts of our current foresight work are we still charging for, or staffing for, that a client could now get from a model and a junior analyst? What would we stop doing?
  2. We can have plurality or we can have speed. If a deliberate plurality audit shows our AI-assisted scenarios are converging, are we willing to accept slower, more expensive futures work as the price of genuinely different futures, and who signs off that trade-off?
  3. The report's stress test argues convergence of design and foresight may be a publishing trend rather than a structural shift. If that is right, which of our planned capability investments would we regret, and how would we know early enough to stop?
  4. Design thinking is contracting but it is still the working method many of our people know. If we reposition toward speculative and experiential practice, whose existing skills become less valuable, and how do we manage that honestly rather than quietly?
  5. Drawing on the weak signal of futures literacy entering general education: if AI-critical futures thinking becomes a broad civic competency rather than a specialist skill, what is left that is distinctively ours, and should we be helping build that pipeline or defending a shrinking moat?
