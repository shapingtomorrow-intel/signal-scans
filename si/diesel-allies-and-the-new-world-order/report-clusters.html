Why This Matters Now

Since the war with Iran began in February, far less fuel has come out of the Middle East. Diesel, the fuel that moves lorries, ships, farms and factories, is scarce and expensive everywhere. Countries that make diesel are keeping more of it at home, and the United States is now thinking about doing the same before its November elections.

Europe and parts of Latin America have come to rely on American diesel, so a ban would hit them hardest. It arrives at a time when Europe, Canada, Japan, South Korea and Australia already doubt how far they can rely on Washington, and are signing trade, defence and energy deals with each other instead.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: plan for supplies and partners that can be switched off by another country's politics, and know which second source you would turn to.

Executive Synthesis

Which of your plans for this winter assume the United States will keep selling diesel abroad?

US diesel passed $6.50 a gallon in September with stocks at a four-decade low (Al Jazeera, Sep 2026), President Trump said he may support a ban or cap on exports (RUSI, Sep 2026), and almost half of US diesel exports now go to Europe (Wood Mackenzie, Sep 2026).

The war with Iran has cut the fuel coming out of the Middle East, and diesel is where the shortage bites hardest. Global stocks fell by 507 million barrels between February and August (IEA, Sep 2026). Countries that refine diesel have answered by keeping it at home: Russia has banned most diesel exports since July (Kommersant, in Russian, Sep 2026) and India has taxed them since March (Business Today, Sep 2026). Now the United States, the largest supplier left, is considering the same before its 3 November midterm elections. Energy Secretary Chris Wright has asked refiners to cut exports voluntarily and says a formal ban would backfire (Reuters, Sep 2026), but President Trump has said he may support one (RUSI, Sep 2026).

Europe and Latin America would take most of the damage. The US supplies about 32% of the EU's diesel imports from outside the EU (Euronews, Sep 2026), and Chile relies on American diesel for about two-thirds of what it uses (Bloomberg Línea, in Portuguese, Sep 2026). The episode lands on partners who already doubt Washington's reliability. Only 35% of Canadians now call the US a reliable partner (Pew Research Center, Jun 2026), Canada suspended trade talks in August (Prime Minister of Canada, Aug 2026) and joined the EU's defence buying programme (Council of the EU, Jun 2026), and Japan and South Korea have agreed to share oil and fuel with each other (Asia Economy, in Korean, May 2026).

Looking ahead to 2030, the US Energy Information Administration expects American diesel stocks to stay below their recent five-year low through much of 2027 (US Energy Information Administration, Sep 2026), the EU-Australia trade agreement should be in force around the end of 2027 (New Zealand Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Apr 2026), and the G7 wants no single outside supplier to provide more than 60% of its rare earths and magnets by 2030 (Élysée, in French, Jun 2026). The analysis reads these as signs of an order built from overlapping clubs rather than around one centre. Where to sit in it is a choice readers can still make: section 7 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the United States will act at all. Its own Energy Secretary argues a ban would fill storage and force refineries to cut output (Reuters, Sep 2026), and Wood Mackenzie estimates it would push 700,000 barrels a day into tanks that fill in about a month (Wood Mackenzie, Sep 2026). If the pressure passes after the elections, the shock to partners is smaller, but the lesson about reliance remains.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Plan this winter's fuel supply without US diesel for up to 90 days. A ban of that length has been reported as in preparation, and the elections fall on 3 November.
  2. Test every plan that depends on one country's goodwill. Fuel, tariffs and defence supplies have all been switched on and off by politics this year.
  3. Build a second route to market through the new partnerships. Trade deals among Europe, Canada, Japan, South Korea and Australia are moving from talks to signatures.

1. The US may keep its diesel at home, and Europe and Latin America would pay most

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The White House and Congress, ahead of the 3 November elections · Room to act: Low on the decision; high on preparing for it

With diesel at record prices and elections five weeks away, Washington is weighing whether to keep more of its diesel at home. A formal ban is uncertain and opposed by the industry and the Energy Secretary, but voluntary cuts may already be under way. The risk for readers is less the ban itself than the discovery that a supplier chosen for its reliability can be switched off by domestic politics. Europe and Latin America, which turned to American diesel after losing Russian and Middle Eastern supply, would pay most.

  • US diesel averaged $6.50 a gallon on 25 September, up from $5.61 a month earlier, and stocks fell to 107.9 million barrels on 11 September, the lowest in more than four decades. Diesel exports equal about 40% of what the US uses at home, and 47% of voters told pollsters the cost of living was their top issue (Al Jazeera, Sep 2026).
  • President Trump said on 22 September he may support a ban or cap on diesel exports (RUSI, Sep 2026). Energy Secretary Chris Wright has asked refiners to cut exports voluntarily, and 36 industry groups oppose a formal ban (Reuters, Sep 2026).
  • The European Commission said on 24 September that the EU “observes with concern” the reported plans, adding: “We expect close partners to consult each other before taking measures that affect shared markets” (Reuters, Sep 2026).
  • Europe's share of US diesel exports reached almost 50% in September, up from 30% on average in 2025, and a ban would raise the margin on diesel in North-West Europe by 27% (Wood Mackenzie, Sep 2026). The US supplies about 32% of the EU's diesel imports from outside the EU, and about 36% of France's (Euronews, Sep 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The cuts may already be happening without a ban. Weekly US distillate exports fell 17.5% to 1.331 million barrels a day in the week to 18 September, the lowest since March, according to Mexican financial press reading government data (El Financiero, in Spanish, Sep 2026). A quiet, voluntary squeeze would hurt buyers without the diplomatic cost of a formal ban.

Outlook to 2030

The US Energy Information Administration expects American diesel stocks to fall below 100 million barrels this month and stay below their 2021-2025 low through much of 2027, with retail diesel averaging $4.40 a gallon in 2027 (US Energy Information Administration, Sep 2026). On that reading, the pressure to keep diesel at home outlasts the elections.

What it means: Treat US diesel as a supply that politics can interrupt until at least 2027, and price a second source accordingly.

Counter-argument

A ban may never come. Wood Mackenzie estimates it would push 700,000 barrels a day into storage, fill Gulf Coast tanks in just over a month and force crude cuts of over 2 million barrels a day (Wood Mackenzie, Sep 2026), which would raise US petrol and jet fuel prices before the elections. Latin American exposure is also uneven: Brazil buys under 10% of its diesel from the US (Bloomberg Línea, in Portuguese, Sep 2026), while Mexico imported 44% of its diesel in May and held under six days of stock (El Financiero, in Spanish, Sep 2026).

2. Fuel exporters are putting their home markets first

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Governments of fuel-exporting countries · Room to act: Low on their choices; moderate through stocks and contracts

Since the war with Iran began, the countries that refine and export fuel have one after another decided that their own drivers, farmers and factories come first. Russia, China, India and South Korea have all limited exports, by ban, quota or tax. Import-dependent economies now buy from what exporting governments choose to release, so the decision in another capital matters as much as the price.

  • The Strait of Hormuz carried about 20 million barrels a day before the conflict and an average of 2.7 million in March to May. Members of the International Energy Agency agreed their largest-ever release of emergency stocks, 400 million barrels (IEA, Jun 2026).
  • In early September US diesel passed $200 a barrel, 94% above pre-war levels, with Europe and Asia close behind (IEA, Sep 2026). Middle East diesel exports were about 50% lower year on year from March to August (RUSI, Sep 2026).
  • China told its main refiners on 5 March to stop diesel and petrol exports (swissinfo, in Chinese, Mar 2026), and is holding down prices at home while telling state oil companies to keep the home market supplied (NDRC, in Chinese, Sep 2026). South Korea capped fuel exports at last year's volume (Money Today, in Korean, Apr 2026).
  • Russia has banned diesel exports by producers since 8 July and plans to extend the ban to the end of October (Kommersant, in Russian, Sep 2026). India has taxed fuel exports since 27 March, though it cut the diesel duty from ₹25 to ₹20 a litre in September (Business Today, Sep 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Russia's restrictions on other sellers run to 31 January 2027 (Kommersant, in Russian, Sep 2026). New capacity will ease the squeeze only slowly: Nigeria's Dangote refinery plans to double to 1.4 million barrels a day, with completion scheduled for 2029 (Offshore Technology, Sep 2026). The analysis expects export controls on fuel to remain a normal tool of government to 2030, used whenever home prices rise.

What it means: Supply risk now sits with exporting governments; contracts, stocks and swap arrangements matter more than the cheapest cargo.

Counter-argument

The market has partly corrected itself. Emergency stocks, lower Chinese buying and record US output filled much of the gap, and US crude and fuel exports reached a record 13.1 million barrels in May (IEA, Jun 2026). India is already easing its export tax (Business Today, Sep 2026). If the Strait reopens, most of these curbs may lapse within months.

3. Europe, Canada, Japan, South Korea and Australia are building ties that bypass Washington

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Governments in Brussels, Ottawa, Tokyo, Seoul and Canberra · Room to act: High: new deals open new markets and suppliers

Relying on the United States now carries political risk for its closest partners, and they are responding by building links with each other. Canada has joined Europe's defence buying programme, the EU and Australia have concluded a trade deal, and Japan and South Korea are sharing oil and fuel. None of this replaces the US, which still buys most of Canada's exports, but it gives each partner somewhere else to go.

  • Canada became the first non-European country allowed into procurement under the EU's defence programme, SAFE, on 15 June (Council of the EU, Jun 2026). Chatham House calls the result coupling without fusion: close, but limited by the fact that the US still takes about 70% of Canada's exports (Chatham House, Sep 2026).
  • On 21 August Canada suspended trade talks with the US after last-minute changes that, in Prime Minister Carney's words, called into question the reliability of any deal, as Washington moved to put a 50% tariff on about $28 billion of Canadian goods (Prime Minister of Canada, Aug 2026).
  • The EU and Australia concluded their trade agreement on 24 March after almost 8 years of talks (New Zealand Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Apr 2026). At their June summit the EU and South Korea began a security and defence partnership and signed a digital trade agreement (EEAS, Jun 2026), and Japan's Prime Minister Takaichi asked the EU for closer defence-industrial and critical-minerals cooperation (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan, in Japanese, Jun 2026).
  • Public trust has moved first. Pew finds that “For example, in Canada, 83% described the U.S. as a reliable partner in 2022, compared with 35% today.” A median of 23% across 36 countries have confidence in President Trump on world affairs (Pew Research Center, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Two US allies are pooling fuel. At their May summit South Korea's President Lee and Japan's Prime Minister Takaichi agreed to swap crude oil and fuel, buy crude jointly and build bigger stockpiles (Asia Economy, in Korean, May 2026). Energy sharing between two partners that long relied on the US for security suggests the hedging now reaches essentials.

Outlook to 2030

The EU-Australia agreement should be signed in late 2026 or early 2027 and may enter into force around the end of 2027 (New Zealand Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Apr 2026). Canada says its exports outside the US are on track to double over the next decade (Prime Minister of Canada, Aug 2026). On that path, by 2030 each of these partners should have at least one working alternative to the US for trade, defence supplies or energy.

What it means: Expect partners to keep their US ties but build alternatives beside them, and plan for a world with two routes to most markets.

Counter-argument

The shift is smaller than the headlines. Canada's exports outside the US are up about 36% since 2024, but that is mostly gold; without precious metals they were essentially flat (Bloomberg, via Canadian Mortgage Trends, May 2026). Japan is also complaining about EU steel tariffs (Ministry of Foreign Affairs of Japan, in Japanese, Jun 2026), a reminder that these partners protect their own industries too.

4. A new order is forming from clubs of like-minded countries

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: Groups of governments, not a single institution · Room to act: Moderate: firms can choose which clubs to build around

Rules for trade, minerals and technology are increasingly written by groups of countries that choose to work together, not by universal bodies. The World Trade Organization could not agree even basic items this year, while clubs such as the Pacific trade pact and a new G7 minerals alliance keep expanding. The result is an order with many centres and overlapping memberships, more flexible than the old one but easier to break, especially when elections change governments.

  • At the WTO meeting in March the long-standing pause on customs duties for digital trade expired for the first time since 1998. Instead, 66 members covering about 70% of world trade agreed to start using an e-commerce agreement among themselves (World Economic Forum, Apr 2026).
  • The Pacific trade pact, CPTPP, moved Costa Rica towards membership, will begin talks with the UAE, the Philippines and Indonesia, and reaffirmed its dialogue with the EU (Australian Department of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Jun 2026). At Évian the G7 created a critical-minerals alliance with Australia as a partner (Élysée, in French, Jun 2026).
  • Middle powers in the richer world account for 34% of global GDP, against 27% for the US and 17% for China, large enough to write rules together (East Asia Forum, Jun 2026). Carnegie calls this a middle-power moment, while noting that power remains split between two superpowers (Carnegie Endowment, Jan 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The G7 aims to cut dependence on any single outside supplier of rare earths and magnets to under 60% by 2030 (Élysée, in French, Jun 2026). The IMF expects world trade to grow 3.5% in 2026 and 4.3% in 2027, but warns that fragmentation could accelerate, hurting output and raising prices (IMF, Jul 2026). On the analysis's reading, by 2030 most trade rules that matter to readers will come from clubs they can join or ignore, not from one global rulebook.

What it means: Choose which clubs to build around, and watch the elections that could pull members out of them.

Counter-argument

Clubs may fragment rather than order. Anne-Marie Slaughter argues the line between working in groups and chaos is narrow and needs strong global institutions behind it (IPG Journal, in German, Aug 2026). Not every middle power is building: a network of populist governments works mainly to block shared rules (Carnegie Endowment, Aug 2026), and 2027 elections in France, Italy, Poland and Spain could paralyse the EU's decisions (Carnegie Endowment, Sep 2026).

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Plan this winter's fuel supply without US diesel for up to 90 days

A 90-day US ban has been reported as in preparation (Reuters, Sep 2026), and weekly exports have already fallen (El Financiero, in Spanish, Sep 2026). Fuel buyers and logistics teams in Europe and Latin America should secure a second source now: term contracts with refiners outside the US, a check on stock days, and a plan for which operations get fuel first if cargoes stop. The cost of doing this before the elections is lower than doing it after.

Open clubs

In 2030 fuel trades freely again and partners do business through overlapping clubs: the EU-Australia deal is in force (New Zealand Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Apr 2026), the Pacific trade pact has grown, and the US is one important member among several. Firms with a presence in more than one club do best.

Early indicators: The Strait of Hormuz reopens; the EU-Australia agreement is ratified; the Pacific trade pact admits new members.

Members first

In 2030 governments still ration fuel exports, but partners share supply inside their clubs, as Japan and South Korea began to do (Asia Economy, in Korean, May 2026). Being inside a club with shared stocks matters more than price.

Early indicators: More stock-sharing and swap deals; export curbs renewed each winter; clubs add energy chapters.

The old order, patched

In 2030 the shortage has passed and the US remains the centre partners trade through. The 2026 frictions are remembered as a bad year, not a turning point, and diversification stalls, as some already argue it has in Canada (Bloomberg, via Canadian Mortgage Trends, May 2026).

Early indicators: US-Canada talks resume; export curbs lapse; trust in the US recovers in polling.

Rationed by Washington

In 2030 partners still depend on the US, and Washington decides who gets fuel and on what terms, in step with its own elections. Supply security depends on the White House's calendar.

Early indicators: A formal US export ban is imposed and renewed; partners' alternatives stay on paper.

What We Are Not Planning For

Not planned for

A lasting US ban on all fuel exports

The debate is about diesel and a limited period, and the administration's own Energy Secretary opposes a formal ban (Reuters, Sep 2026). A permanent ban on all fuels would damage US refiners and prices at home.

Not planned for

A break-up of US alliances

↺ Reinstated if a partner formally leaves a security treaty with the US or ends a basing agreement.

Claim-fidelity self-disclosure

This appendix records where the evidence is thinner than a source count suggests, and what was done about it.

Plain language, same evidence. At the commissioning analyst's request this edition avoids technical language. Terms are explained where they first appear, and no claim was simplified beyond what its source says. The source's own wording is kept in the evidence record.

Reported, not announced. No US export ban has been announced. The 90-day ban is Politico's report as carried by Reuters (Reuters, Sep 2026), and the Energy Secretary disputes it. The fall in weekly US exports comes from Mexican financial press reading government data (El Financiero, in Spanish, Sep 2026) and may reflect voluntary cuts, logistics or both.

Different measures in one figure. The bar chart shows the share of diesel use met by US diesel for Chile and Peru (Bloomberg Línea, in Portuguese, Sep 2026), and the share of diesel imports from the US for European markets (Euronews, Sep 2026). The two are not the same measure and are labelled.

Non-English sources. Eleven sources are in Chinese, Korean, Spanish, Portuguese, Russian, Japanese, French and German. They are summarised in English and cited, not quoted. China's 5 March instruction to refiners was reported by the Swiss public broadcaster's Chinese service (swissinfo, in Chinese, Mar 2026); China's foreign ministry said it was not aware of any suspension.
