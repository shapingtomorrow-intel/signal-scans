Where Your Money Will Live in 2030
Most of us assume three things about our money:
  1. that it is safe in the bank,
  2. that a payment we did not make can be reversed, and
  3. that a savings fund will give it back when we ask.
All three promises were written for the bank account. This summer showed where they stop:
  1. In the United States, firms behind digital dollars are being licensed as banks that carry no deposit insurance.
  2. Nobody has yet decided who pays when an AI assistant spends your money on the wrong thing.
  3. Investors asked one private credit fund for 40.7% of their money back and received a fraction of that.
If you do not work in finance, the interesting part is not the technology. It is that the safety net under your pension, your savings or your company's cash may not be there, and nobody is obliged to tell you.

Decide now
Interest is out. Rewards are in.
Governments are drawing the same line for digital dollars: no interest for simply holding them, but rewards for using them. The US law that would have settled it failed in the Senate by 49 votes to 50 on 15 September; the UK and Korea have drawn the line anyway. Estimates of what it does to bank lending run from almost nothing to $60 billion to $126 billion for every $100 billion that leaves the bank for good. The contest for where people keep their money will be fought on rewards, which makes it a question for marketing and product teams as much as for treasurers.
If an app offered your customers a reward to move their balances, which of them would go?

Decide who pays when an AI agent pays
AI assistants are starting to make payments on people's behalf, not just recommend them. The Bank of England says its rules were not built for software that acts on its own, and a US banking group warns that the usual cap on what a customer can lose may not apply. The Financial Stability Board's final report on AI is due in October. Until the rules change, each firm's own terms decide who carries the loss, and that reaches every business that sells online, not only banks.
If a customer's AI assistant bought the wrong thing from you tomorrow, whose terms would decide the refund?

Find the promises of easy withdrawal
Private credit is lending by investment funds rather than banks, now $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion worldwide, and increasingly sold to ordinary savers. When withdrawal requests jumped this year, most managers capped them at 5%. Banks have lent around $220 billion to these funds, so the risk that left the banks has not gone far. The pressure point is simple: a promise to hand money back quickly, made on loans that cannot be sold quickly.
Where does your pension, your treasury or a product you sell promise money back on demand from assets nobody can sell in a hurry?
Built for people outside finance as much as in it: anyone holding company cash or a pension, running payments or customer accounts, putting AI to work where it can buy things, and boards asking where their organisation's money actually sits.
Where we might be wrong
Three parts of this reading could be overstated, and the briefing tests each. The drain from bank deposits may be small: the White House's economists find the effect of banning interest on bank lending close to nothing, and if stablecoin issuers keep their reserves in banks, the money simply changes owner. Banks' own digital tokens may not take off: earlier bank consortia failed, and a Bank of America payments executive has said clients are not beating down the door. And private credit may be less dangerous than it looks: the European Central Bank and Federal Reserve staff both judge the direct risk to large banks limited, which is why our concern is savers, not banks. We also grade ourselves. July's edition expected one US vote on stablecoin interest to set the pace of change. The vote failed and regulators set the pace anyway, so this cycle's Action Triggers marks that July belief as replaced.
