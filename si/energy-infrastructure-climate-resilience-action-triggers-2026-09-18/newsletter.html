Paying More Did Not Bring More Power
Nearly every plan has a line in it that assumes a high enough price will fetch the thing. This year that assumption was tested in public: a market paid the highest price its own rules allow and got 317 megawatts against a shortfall of 6,831. What is scarce now is not money, it is the equipment, the permission and, in two European river basins this summer, the water. If you are not in energy, the interesting part is not the electricity. It is what happens to an organisation when its price signal stops working and nobody updates the plan that depends on it.

Decide now
Settle who pays before somebody settles it for you
Twenty-three US states now have a rule deciding who funds the network a large new customer triggers, and seven more are drafting one. Oregon's approved version runs ten to thirty years, puts all of the local upgrade cost on the customer and charges a minimum whether or not the power is used. Spain has drafted one that can take a site's grid connection away entirely. These are being written now, in filings, mostly by people who are not in the room. The version you inherit by default is the version drafted without you.
In how many of the places you operate could you name the rule that decides who pays, and did anyone from your side comment on it?
Prepare
Check whether your suppliers' order book is orders
Four turbine manufacturers disclosed 246.6 gigawatts of backlog this summer. Around 89 of those gigawatts are reservations: someone has put a deposit down to hold a place in the factory queue, not signed for the machine. Eighteen of the twenty gigawatts one maker signed in the second quarter were reservations. The distinction is invisible in the headline number and it decides whether capacity you are counting on exists. It is worth asking of any long-lead supplier, in any industry, the moment their backlog becomes load-bearing in your own plan.
Is the capacity you have been promised contracted, or is it a place in a queue somebody can release?
Prepare
Discount any demand pipeline nobody has put money behind
Wherever a utility or a regulator has asked data-centre developers for a deposit or a security agreement, a third or more of the declared demand has disappeared. Consumption forecasts did not change. What changed is that somebody had to write a cheque to stay on the list. Any pipeline that costs nothing to join is a wish list with a spreadsheet around it, and that is as true of sales pipelines and customer commitments as it is of grid queues.
What would your own demand pipeline look like if joining it required a deposit?

Where we might be wrong
The whole reading rests on the shortage being a delivery problem rather than a forecasting one. If the demand being planned for never arrives, these auctions are pricing a phantom and patience beats procurement. That case is not weak: one Texan queue holds around 460,000 megawatts of interest on a system that has never exceeded 91,000, and this cycle's own evidence shows a third of declared demand vanishing the moment anyone asks for a deposit. We have also published a correction against ourselves. July's edition recorded a record-breaking July day as falling just short of the 2006 peak; the grid operator's own figure says it broke it, and this month's edition withdraws our reading rather than quietly restating it. On the weather, the European drought was wider than 2022 but less intense at its peak, which cuts against a simple worse-every-year story and we have said so.
