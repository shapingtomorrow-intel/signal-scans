Why This Matters Now

For decades many European states, especially smaller ones, could lean on the United States for the hardest parts of defence: the forces that arrive first in a crisis, the systems that sense and plan, and much of the long-term thinking about threats.

That is changing on several fronts at once. Washington is deciding how many troops to bring home. The planning tools armed forces are adopting increasingly run on American software and cloud. And governments are asking universities and research labs to help close the gap, before anyone has agreed how that work will be kept secure or open.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: list what your plans assume America, its software and its analysts will provide, and decide which of those you need to be able to do yourselves by 2030.

Executive Synthesis

Which of your plans assume that American forces, software and analysis will be there when you need them?

The evidence here says the US is weighing withdrawing 25,000 to 40,000 troops from Europe while European forces adopt AI planning tools that run largely on American systems.

Europe is being asked to defend itself with tools it does not control. The Pentagon is weighing withdrawing 25,000 troops or more from Europe, and as many as 40,000 (Anadolu, via The Express Tribune, Sep 2026), after cutting its brigade combat teams there from four to three (Washington Examiner, May 2026) and planning to cut the forces it earmarks for NATO crises by one-third to one-half (Stars and Stripes, May 2026). At the same time the tools that help forces see and decide are increasingly American: users of the US Maven AI system doubled to over 100,000 this year (DefenseScoop, Sep 2026), NATO runs Maven at its headquarters (Euronews, Oct 2026), and three non-EU providers control over 70% of Europe’s cloud market (Council of the EU, Jun 2026).

Some of this is settled: Germany plans €109.7 billion for defence in 2027 (German Bundestag, in German, Jul 2026), and a House bill would keep at least 76,000 US troops in Europe (Washington Examiner, May 2026). Three things are not. Which gaps will Europe fill first, and will states fill them alone or together? Will staff keep checking AI advice as it gets faster, when a human in the loop “does not guarantee meaningful control” (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, Oct 2026)? And will universities and research labs, now offered new defence money (Research Council of Norway, in Norwegian, Oct 2026), help close the gap on terms they have agreed?

Looking ahead to 2030, the Kiel Institute puts the cost of replacing critical US capabilities at €150 billion to €200 billion (Defense News, on the Kiel Institute, May 2026), the UK plans to invest at least £17bn in its digital backbone and targeting web between 2030 and 2035 (UK Ministry of Defence, Jun 2026), and the Netherlands has given its central government until about mid-2030 to bring its cloud use into line with sovereignty rules (Dutch government, in Dutch, Jul 2026). None of this is fixed, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the US drawdown will be large and fast. Congress may hold a floor of 76,000 troops (Washington Examiner, May 2026), NATO presents the review as consultation (NATO, Aug 2026), and AWS has just been cleared to handle NATO restricted information for every member (Defense One, Sep 2026). If the cuts stay small and access to American systems stays reliable, the cost of preparing would be duplication Europe did not need.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Cost the American capabilities your plans assume, and fund the first gap.
  2. Make challenging AI advice part of how staff are trained, before the tools spread further.
  3. Write exit plans for the cloud and AI services that critical functions depend on.

1. America is cutting its forces in Europe faster than Europe can replace what they do

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The US government decides; European defence ministries and NATO planners must respond · Room to act: Low on the decision, moderate on the response: Europe chooses what to replace first

For decades the United States has supplied the forces that arrive first in a European crisis and many of the specialist systems behind them: air-to-air refuelling, long-range strike, intelligence and command. Washington is now reviewing how much of that it keeps in Europe. The review matters less for the headline number of troops than for the enablers, which European states cannot buy quickly.

Sources: (Breaking Defense, May 2026); (Washington Examiner, May 2026); (Washington Examiner, May 2026); (Stars and Stripes, May 2026); (NATO, Aug 2026); (Anadolu, via The Express Tribune, Sep 2026); (Military Times, on RAND, Oct 2026); (Defense News, on the Kiel Institute, May 2026).

  • The cuts have started. The US ordered about 5,000 troops out of Germany, to be completed over the next six to twelve months (Breaking Defense, May 2026), cut its brigade combat teams in Europe from four to three (Washington Examiner, May 2026), and cancelled the move of about 4,000 armoured brigade soldiers to Poland in May (Defence24, in Polish, Jul 2026).
  • Bigger options are on the table. The Pentagon is considering withdrawing about 25,000 or more troops, possibly as many as 40,000 (Anadolu, via The Express Tribune, Sep 2026), and its review owes the defence secretary at least four sets of options by 6 November (Military Times, on RAND, Oct 2026).
  • The enablers go too. The administration aims to cut the forces earmarked to defend NATO by one-third to one-half, including strategic bombers, some refuelling tankers and warships available in the first 10, 30 and 180 days of a crisis (Stars and Stripes, May 2026).
  • Europe cannot replace them quickly. Hudson finds “not enough capabilities, particularly high-end enablers” to replace what the US withdraws (Hudson Institute, Sep 2026), and the US supplied 58% of the major weapons European NATO members imported from 2021 to 2025 (Military Times, on RAND, Oct 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Washington asks allies to talk less about Russia. Pentagon officials have asked several NATO countries, including the Baltic states, to tone down their rhetoric about the Russian threat, and the official behind the request is central to the force review (Defense News, on Reuters, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The Kiel Institute estimates that reaching defence autonomy would cost €150 billion to €200 billion by 2030 and €500 billion over the decade, around €50 billion a year (Defense News, on the Kiel Institute, May 2026); Germany plans to raise defence spending from 2.4% of GDP in 2025 to 3.5% by 2029 (Military Times, on RAND, Oct 2026); and a draft of the Commission’s 2027 work programme proposes a new European instrument for strategic enablers, while only a third of EU counter-drone programmes are expected to deliver by 2030 (Euronews, Nov 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 Europe has the money but not yet the enablers, and the states that agreed with neighbours which gaps to fill first are the ones that filled them.

What it means: Plan the first weeks of a crisis on the assumption that American enablers arrive late or not at all, and decide now which of them to replace first, alone or with neighbours.

Counter-argument

The cuts may stay small. A House bill would impose a 76,000 troop minimum in Europe (Washington Examiner, May 2026), NATO says allies welcomed US engagement on the review and points to a $139 billion rise in European and Canadian defence spending in 2025 (NATO, Aug 2026), Germany’s 2027 defence budget rises to €109.7 billion from €82.7 billion (German Bundestag, in German, Jul 2026), and Poland says the US rotation will be completed within weeks (Defence24, in Polish, Jul 2026). If Congress holds the floor, the drawdown is a trim, not a retreat.

On the System Map: loop R1 (each gap filled alone) is priming, as states buy separately what they once borrowed together; the US drawdown acts from outside the map, and leverage point 1 sits on shared enablers.

2. AI is moving into military planning faster than commanders are trained to question it

Near-Term

Weight: high · Who decides: Chiefs of staff, training commands and the ministries that buy planning tools · Room to act: High: each force decides how its staff are trained to use and question AI

Armed forces are adopting AI that reads sensor and intelligence data, proposes targets and drafts plans. The tools are fast and often good. The question is whether the people using them still check what the machine proposes, and whether they are trained and given the time to do so. Foresight and planning staff face the same risk: tools that speed up analysis without challenging its assumptions.

Sources: (War on the Rocks, Oct 2026); (US Government Accountability Office, Oct 2026). The first two bars come from one planning exercise; the third is the share of posts filled.

  • Adoption is fast. Pentagon officials say Maven users rose from about 50,000 in January to over 100,000 (DefenseScoop, Sep 2026); NATO’s Maven reached full technical operational capability in June (Euronews, Oct 2026); and Ukraine’s DELTA system is used by more than 200,000 members of its forces (NATO Allied Command Transformation, Jun 2026).
  • The machine is good. In a US Air Force planning sprint an AI produced options in under a minute rated 97% viable, against about 19 minutes and 48% for human planners, and the authors say officers should keep practising manual planning (War on the Rocks, Oct 2026).
  • Checking is the weak point. A human in the loop “does not guarantee meaningful control” when operators face hundreds of recommendations with too little time and commanders reward rapid acceptance (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, Oct 2026); the Pentagon office for responsible AI had filled only three of ten posts (US Government Accountability Office, Oct 2026).
  • Europe is writing its own rules. Germany’s new AI concept keeps humans in control of critical decisions and requires testing and training before use (German Federal Ministry of Defence, in German, Aug 2026); its public version adds that staff must be able to check AI outputs and must keep practising without AI, because skills can be lost (Augen geradeaus!, in German, Aug 2026). France is testing its own system after asking whether allies should “adopt Maven blindly” (Euronews, Jun 2026), and Estonia and Finland signed a joint plan to speed responsible AI in command and decision support (Estonian Defence Forces, in Estonian, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A rule to practise without the machine. Germany’s AI concept makes regular practice without AI mandatory, because constant or wrong use of AI can erode staff skills (Augen geradeaus!, in German, Aug 2026). None of the other allied rules in this cycle’s sources goes that far.

Outlook to 2030

The UK plans £80m for AI tools that help land commanders decide faster and at least £17bn on its digital backbone and targeting web between 2030 and 2035 (UK Ministry of Defence, Jun 2026), and the US 2027 request includes $2.3 billion for Maven and its fires network and $6.8 billion to integrate AI (Greenberg Traurig, May 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 AI drafts most staff work in allied forces, and the forces that trained people to question it, rather than only to use it, get the better decisions.

What it means: Treat the ability to challenge AI advice as a skill to train and practise, not a rule to write once, and keep planning without AI part of every exercise.

Counter-argument

Automation bias may be overstated. In an experiment on an AI targeting system, participants were more willing to approve strikes when information came from human intelligence officers, and the researchers “found no evidence of automation bias” (Carnegie Endowment, May 2026). France and Germany have opted out of NATO’s Maven (Euronews, Oct 2026), and both write human control into their rules. The bigger risk the same researchers flag is scale: an AI system “could generate fifty targets a day”.

On the System Map: loop B1 (speed crowds out checking) is priming: faster AI advice shortens the time to check it, but no error has yet forced a pause; leverage point 2 sits on training staff to challenge.

3. Europe’s armed forces depend on American cloud and AI, and few states have a plan to reduce that

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Defence ministries’ information chiefs, procurement agencies and the EU · Room to act: Moderate: states choose contracts and exit plans; they cannot conjure European capacity

Much of the software, cloud capacity and AI that European armed forces and governments use is American. That is efficient, and usually secure, but it gives another government’s laws and decisions a say over whether critical services keep running. Smaller states have the least capacity to build alternatives and the most to lose if access is withdrawn.

Sources: (Council of the EU, Jun 2026); (Bitkom, in German, Jun 2026). The market shares are the European Commission’s figures; the last bar is a survey of German firms.

  • Dependence is high. The EU’s own evidence says EU providers’ share of the European cloud market fell from 29% in 2017 to 15% in 2022, and three non-EU providers control over 70% (Council of the EU, Jun 2026); RUSI puts Europe’s reliance on foreign countries at over 80% of digital products, services and infrastructure (RUSI, Jun 2026).
  • A few states have rules. The Netherlands now advises its critical government bodies against providers under non-EU law for core tasks, requires an exit plan for important cloud services and gives four years to comply (Dutch government, in Dutch, Jul 2026); the French armed forces ministry reportedly cut its renewed Microsoft contract from five years to three (L’Essentiel de l’Éco, in French, Oct 2026).
  • Smaller states hedge. Denmark, which signed a 7-year deal with Palantir, said it will look for local replacements (Euronews, Jun 2026), but its new defence minister will not rule out non-European software with better technology (Altinget, in Danish, Jun 2026).
  • Companies are moving first. Airbus will move around 70 critical applications to the French provider Scaleway by the end of 2028, citing protection against a kill switch and extraterritorial laws (Reuters, via The Star, Jul 2026), and 85% of German firms say Germany is too dependent on US cloud providers (Bitkom, in German, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A sovereign battlefield system tested beside NATO’s. France connected its own AI command system, Arcadia, in Latvia from 19 October to test it against NATO’s Maven (L’Essentiel de l’Éco, in French, Oct 2026), after asking whether allies should “adopt Maven blindly” (Euronews, Jun 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The proposed EU Cloud and AI Development Act aims to triple EU data-centre capacity in five to seven years (Council of the EU, Jun 2026), an effort RUSI expects to need about €200 billion of mostly private investment (RUSI, Jun 2026); the Dutch rules apply to existing services by about mid-2030 (Dutch government, in Dutch, Jul 2026), and Airbus’s wider programme could cover up to 900 applications over five to six years (Reuters, via The Star, Jul 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 most European forces still run on American cloud for routine work, and the difference lies in which states can move their critical functions within weeks if they must.

What it means: Know which critical functions run on non-European cloud and AI, and have a tested way to move them, before a dispute forces the question.

Counter-argument

Defence officials push back. Officials and contractors warn that rules favouring EU-controlled providers “could leave armed forces using less capable services”, with opposition strongest on NATO’s north-eastern flank (Defence Matters, Sep 2026); AWS has become the first provider cleared to handle sensitive information for all NATO members (Defense One, Sep 2026); and 58% of German firms still reject paying more or losing features for a German cloud (Bitkom, in German, Jun 2026). For a small state, reliable American service may be the safer choice.

On the System Map: loop R2 (scale draws more users) is turning: the largest providers win more contracts and grow further ahead; leverage point 3 sits on exit plans.

4. Universities and research labs are being drawn into defence work before the rules are written

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: Research ministries, research councils, universities and research and technology organisations · Room to act: High: institutions can agree terms for security, openness and funding before the EU sets them

Many European states let their own defence research capacity shrink after the Cold War and relied on allies for long-term thinking about threats and technology. They are now asking universities and research and technology organisations to help rebuild it. The money is arriving before the terms on secrecy, openness, security and funding have been agreed, and the institutions involved are still deciding how far they will go.

  • New money in the Nordics. Norway funded a NOK 660 million, five-year defence, security and preparedness initiative and created a new Research Council portfolio for it (Research Council of Norway, in Norwegian, Oct 2026); its NOK 450 million centre call drew 112 outline bids from 30 organisations (Research Council of Norway, in Norwegian, Jun 2026). Sweden now has the highest Nordic share of state research money for defence, though much of the rise goes directly to the armed forces and its defence research agency rather than to universities (Statistics Sweden, in Swedish, Jun 2026).
  • Denmark is building links. The Technical University of Denmark asked the new defence minister to fund high-tech laboratories, citing a new collaboration with the Danish Defence Intelligence Service (Technical University of Denmark, in Danish, Sep 2026); the head of Denmark’s national defence technology centre says universities should not fear militarisation, while some researchers fear secrecy will damage open science (Altinget, in Danish, Jun 2026).
  • Security duties come with it. Germany’s rectors say many universities are not yet prepared for the security demands of such research and want funding they can apply for directly (German Rectors’ Conference, in German, May 2026); after a suspected espionage case in May, the government is starting a national platform for research security this autumn (German Bundestag, in German, Jun 2026); and Norway has tasked its research council, security authority and defence research institute with a shared method for assessing sensitive technologies (Norwegian Ministry of Defence, in Norwegian, Dec 2025).
  • The EU moves next. Its proposal for the next research programme gives €175 billion in total, of which €6.44 billion is for a new programme on defence research (Transnational Institute, Jun 2026), and the Council has added provisions on research security and dual-use activities (YERUN, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Start-ups get defence money before universities do. Before the next research programme’s rules are agreed, the EU’s innovation council opened a €100 million defence scale-up call to companies in the EU (ERA Portal Austria, Jun 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The European Defence Agency projects EU defence spending of up to €547 billion by 2029, but defence research and development rising only from €17 billion in 2025 to €20 billion (European Defence Agency, Jul 2026); Norway’s new centres start in 2027 and run up to five years (Research Council of Norway, in Norwegian, Jun 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 defence research is a normal part of European university life, and the institutions that agreed their terms early, and pooled their strengths with others, are the ones defence ministries turn to.

What it means: Agree how your institution will handle secrecy, openness, security vetting and funding for defence work now, while the national and EU rules are still being written.

Counter-argument

The pull may be weaker than it looks. The Guild of research universities insists dual-use research must remain “a carefully governed exception” in a civilian programme (The Guild, via ERA Portal Austria, Jul 2026); Norway’s 112 bids compete for a handful of centres (Research Council of Norway, in Norwegian, Jun 2026); and in Hesse only four of 14 public universities still have a civil clause, but Kassel postponed its vote on loosening its own to 4 November after a petition signed by 629 people (Forschung & Lehre, in German, Jul 2026).

On the System Map: loop B2 (secrecy deters researchers) is priming: stricter security can push researchers away from the work it is meant to protect; leverage point 4 sits on agreed terms.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Cost the American capabilities your plans assume, and fund the first gap with neighbours

The US plans to cut the forces earmarked for NATO crises by one-third to one-half (Stars and Stripes, May 2026), and Europe lacks the high-end enablers to replace them (Hudson Institute, Sep 2026). Defence planners should list the American enablers each plan depends on, cost a European replacement for the most critical, and agree with neighbouring states which gaps to fill together.

Action: Defence ministries and joint staffs to list the US enablers each operational plan depends on, and cost a shared replacement for the top three, by 31 March 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 1, shared enablers, where loop R1 (each gap filled alone) can be broken.

Implication 2: Make challenging AI advice a trained and practised skill before the tools spread further

Maven’s users doubled this year (DefenseScoop, Sep 2026), while a human in the loop does not guarantee control when time is short and speed is rewarded (Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, Oct 2026). Chiefs of staff should write a duty to check AI advice into staff procedures, train it, and keep planning without AI in every exercise, as Germany now requires (Augen geradeaus!, in German, Aug 2026).

Action: Chiefs of staff and training commands to add an AI-challenge step and a no-AI planning drill to staff training and exercises by 31 March 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 2. On the System Map: leverage point 2, training to challenge, where loop B1 (speed crowds out checking) turns.

Implication 3: Write and test exit plans for the cloud and AI services critical functions depend on

Three non-EU providers control over 70% of Europe’s cloud market (Council of the EU, Jun 2026), and the Netherlands now requires exit plans for important cloud services (Dutch government, in Dutch, Jul 2026). Information chiefs should identify which critical functions run on non-European services, write an exit plan for each, and test the most critical.

Action: Defence and government information chiefs to map critical functions on non-European cloud and AI and write an exit plan for each by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 3. On the System Map: leverage point 3, exit plans, where loop R2 (scale draws more users) can be slowed.

Implication 4: Agree the terms for defence research with universities and research organisations before the EU sets them

The EU’s next research programme proposes €6.44 billion for defence research (Transnational Institute, Jun 2026), and Germany’s universities say many are not prepared for the security demands (German Rectors’ Conference, in German, May 2026). Research ministries, universities and research organisations should agree common terms on security vetting, openness and funding, and pool capabilities across institutions rather than compete for each call.

Action: Research ministries, universities and research and technology organisations to agree shared terms for defence and dual-use work before the next EU research programme begins in 2028, by 31 December 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 4. On the System Map: leverage point 4, agreed terms, where loop B2 (secrecy deters researchers) can be eased.
