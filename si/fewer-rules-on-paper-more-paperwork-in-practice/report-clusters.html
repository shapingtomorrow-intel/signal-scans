Why This Matters Now

Governments on both sides of the Atlantic promised to cut red tape, and some big deadlines have moved: Europe's strictest rules for artificial intelligence now start in December 2027 rather than this summer.

The work has not gone away; it has moved. Since September, makers of connected products sold in Europe must report security flaws that attackers are already using within 24 hours. New liability rules that treat software as a product arrive in December, before most countries have written their own laws. Regulators in Brussels and London now treat AI chatbots as rivals to Google search. In the US, Washington is easing while the states pass their own AI laws, and China, India, Japan and Korea are each writing rules of their own.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: plan for the duties that already apply, from incident reporting to product liability, rather than for the deadlines that moved.

Executive Synthesis

Which plans on your desk this week assume a delayed rule means delayed work?

The evidence here says the deadlines moved but the duties did not: reporting, liability and disclosure dates still land this year.

Governments promised to simplify, and some headline dates have moved. The EU's AI simplification law pushes the AI Act's rules for high-risk systems back to 2 December 2027, and to 2 August 2028 for AI built into products (White & Case, Aug 2026). The duties that bind now have moved rather than gone away. Makers of connected products sold in the EU have had to report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents since 11 September 2026 (ENISA, Sep 2026). The EU's new product liability rules, which treat software as a product, must be in national law by 9 December 2026 (Dutch Senate, in Dutch, Sep 2026), yet only Hungary and Croatia had finished by September (Faegre Drinker, Sep 2026). And from 2 December, generative AI systems already on the market must mark what they produce (European Commission, Jul 2026).

Some of this is settled: the reporting duty is live, the EU's simplified sustainability standards apply to financial years from 2027 (German Chamber of Public Accountants, in German, Sep 2026), and the deforestation rules apply from 30 December with no further delay (Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, May 2026). Four things are not. Will the Commission designate ChatGPT a gatekeeper under the Digital Markets Act, now that it has told Brussels it crosses the thresholds (Eunews, Jul 2026)? Will California's first emissions reports fall due on 10 November, when the rule that sets the date awaits approval (Ropes & Gray, Sep 2026)? Will national parliaments pass their liability and platform-work laws in time? And will Washington's push against state AI laws prevail, when 36 state attorneys general oppose it (Holtzman Vogel, Jul 2026)?

Looking ahead to 2030, the Cyber Resilience Act's main security requirements apply from 11 December 2027 (ENISA, Sep 2026), California's emissions reporters move from limited assurance in 2027 to reasonable assurance by 2030 (Mayer Brown, Aug 2026), India's core data-protection duties start on 13 May 2027 (King Stubb & Kasiva, Aug 2026), and France's competition authority sees AI agents becoming a channel for close to 25% of consumers' access to services by 2030 (French competition authority, in French, Jul 2026). None of this is fixed, and each is a point where readers can still choose their position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the duties that apply now will be enforced now. The Cyber Resilience Act's fines take effect only on 11 December 2027, so the reporting duty runs for about 15 months with no threat of fines (it-daily.net, in German, Sep 2026), and private employers are generally not bound by an EU directive until national law exists (Morgan Lewis, Jun 2026). If regulators treat the next year as a grace period, the work has moved in law but not yet in risk.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Run Cyber Resilience Act reporting as a live duty for every connected product on sale in the EU. The 24-hour early warning already applies.
  2. Map the year-end EU duties against national laws, country by country. Liability, platform-work and AI-marking dates arrive before most national laws do.
  3. Plan one compliance programme with regional modules, not one global rulebook. The US, Europe and Asia are moving in different directions.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Europe delays the design rules but switches on the reporting rules

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The European Commission, ENISA and national market-surveillance authorities · Room to act: Moderate: firms choose how fast they build reporting and transparency processes, not the dates

Europe has delayed the rules on how AI systems must be designed, but switched on the rules on what companies must report. The high-risk AI obligations move back by more than a year, largely because the technical standards are not ready, while AI transparency enforcement and Cyber Resilience Act reporting are already live. The work has shifted from design files to reporting.

Sources: (White & Case, Aug 2026); (ENISA, Sep 2026); (European Commission, Jul 2026); (Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, May 2026).

  • The EU's AI simplification law, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, moves the high-risk dates to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone systems and 2 August 2028 for AI built into products, while most of the Article 50 transparency rules apply from 2 August 2026 (White & Case, Aug 2026), when enforcement began (European Commission, Jul 2026).
  • Since 11 September 2026, manufacturers must report actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents to ENISA's platform (ENISA, Sep 2026): an early warning within 24 hours and a full notification within 72 hours (European Commission, Sep 2026). Germany's federal cyber security office confirms the duty (German federal cyber security office, in German, Sep 2026).
  • The first harmonised European standard for the AI Act, on quality management, has been published (CEN-CENELEC, Jul 2026), but the standards bodies had to adopt exceptional measures to finish six of the most delayed drafts (CEN-CENELEC, Oct 2025).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Reporting may become Europe's evidence base on software security. Every report goes to ENISA's platform, including reports on products sold before the rules applied (ENISA, Sep 2026). The channel could become the EU's main record of which products were exposed, and when.

Outlook to 2030

From the end of 2027, products that miss the Cyber Resilience Act's requirements cannot be sold in the EU, and Germany is building testing capacity now (German federal cyber security office, in German, Sep 2026); the main requirements apply from 11 December 2027 (ENISA, Sep 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the reporting records built now become the evidence used to judge whether products were designed safely.

What it means: Treat incident reporting and AI transparency as live obligations now, and use the delay to finish design documentation against the standards as they are published.

Counter-argument

The duties are live but barely enforced. The Cyber Resilience Act's fines start only on 11 December 2027, so the reporting duty runs for about 15 months with no threat of fines, and only 29% of firms in a Bitkom survey knew what the Act meant for them (it-daily.net, in German, Sep 2026). The first AI standard also arrived early (CEN-CENELEC, Jul 2026), so the gap may close faster than the delay suggests.

2. Year-end deadlines arrive before national laws do

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: National parliaments and ministries, then national courts · Room to act: Moderate: firms can prepare on the EU date, but the national detail is not theirs to set

A cluster of EU deadlines lands in the last weeks of 2026, before most national laws are ready. The revised Product Liability Directive treats software and AI as products, and platform-work, AI-marking and deforestation dates fall in the same month. Companies face duties whose national detail is still unknown.

Sources: (ENISA, Sep 2026); (Ropes & Gray, Sep 2026); (King Stubb & Kasiva, Aug 2026); (Littler, Aug 2026); (Faegre Drinker, Sep 2026); (Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, May 2026).

  • Only Hungary and Croatia had fully transposed the Product Liability Directive by early September (Faegre Drinker, Sep 2026). Germany's Bundestag has since passed its law, its first full reform since 1989 (German Bundestag, in German, Oct 2026); France only envisages a dedicated bill (French National Assembly, in French, May 2026), and the Dutch bill is still before the lower house (Dutch Senate, in Dutch, Sep 2026).
  • The Platform Work Directive must be in national law by 2 December 2026, yet in several member states no draft bill has been published (Littler, Aug 2026); the Netherlands consulted on its draft only until 24 August (Dutch government, in Dutch, Jun 2026).
  • Generative AI systems on the market before 2 August 2026 must mark their output from 2 December 2026, the end of the AI Act's only grace period on transparency (European Commission, Jul 2026).
  • The deforestation rules will not be postponed again: large and medium companies must comply from 30 December 2026, and micro and small operators from 30 June 2027 (Hogan Lovells Cadwalader, May 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The new liability rules apply to products placed on the market on or after 9 December 2026 (Dutch Senate, in Dutch, Sep 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the first liability claims over software and AI will be heard in the countries that legislated first.

What it means: Prepare for each duty from the EU date, and track national laws country by country rather than waiting for all 27.

Counter-argument

A late national law may simply delay the duty. Private employers are generally not bound by the Pay Transparency Directive until it is in local law (Morgan Lewis, Jun 2026), and the same logic applies to liability and platform-work rules between private parties.

3. Competition regulators now treat AI assistants as search rivals

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The European Commission under the Digital Markets Act and Digital Services Act, and the UK's Competition and Markets Authority · Room to act: Moderate: firms choose how they reach customers through AI assistants; access rules are set by regulators

Regulators in Brussels and London now treat AI assistants as rivals to Google search, entitled to its data, Android features and places on its choice screens. The same move puts the largest assistants on the path to regulation themselves. How people find products is becoming a regulated channel.

  • The Commission issued binding measures requiring Google to give rival AI assistants equal access to Android features and to share search data, and AI chatbots that offer search are eligible to receive it (European Commission, Jul 2026). It also fined Google 890 million euros (Tech Policy Press, Jul 2026).
  • The UK's Competition and Markets Authority proposes putting AI assistants, which people increasingly use as their search service, on Google's choice screens, with a final decision expected by the end of the year (Competition and Markets Authority, Sep 2026).
  • The Commission designated ChatGPT a Very Large Online Search Engine under the Digital Services Act, with extra duties by January 2027 (European Commission, Aug 2026), and ChatGPT has told the Commission its user numbers exceed the Digital Markets Act's thresholds (Eunews, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The rules written to help AI rivals may soon bind them. ChatGPT gained access rights against Google in July (European Commission, Jul 2026) and was classed as a search engine in August (European Commission, Aug 2026). A gatekeeper designation would turn the same duties on it.

Outlook to 2030

France's competition authority finds that AI agents carry less than 5% of consumers' access to services in France today but could carry close to 25% by 2030 (French competition authority, in French, Jul 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the assistant that answers a customer's question is regulated much like the search engine it replaced.

What it means: Plan for AI assistants as a channel to customers with regulated access, and for your own assistant to carry gatekeeper-style duties if it grows.

Counter-argument

The market may move faster than the rules. The Google cases ran far beyond the Digital Markets Act's 12-month timeline (Tech Policy Press, Jul 2026), the Commission's first review of the Act added no AI category (Ashurst Perkins Coie, May 2026), and the International Center for Law & Economics argues that early rules could hold back innovation (International Center for Law & Economics, May 2026).

4. Washington loosens while the states tighten

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Federal agencies and the Justice Department on one side; state legislatures and attorneys general on the other · Room to act: Moderate: firms choose which state rules to build to first; the federal-state contest is not theirs to settle

In the United States, federal regulators are easing while the states hold or tighten the line. Bank supervisors have narrowed what examiners can criticise and the White House has set the Justice Department against state AI laws, but Congress has not overridden the states, and California keeps adding AI, privacy and climate disclosure rules.

  • The bank regulators' final rule says speculative concerns about future harm should not support a formal examiner finding (Federal Register, Sep 2026), and an executive order set up a Justice Department task force within 30 days whose sole job is to challenge state AI laws (Federal Register, Dec 2025).
  • Congress has not followed: 36 state attorneys general urged it not to block state AI laws (Holtzman Vogel, Jul 2026), and the bipartisan House authors introduced only a stand-alone bill on frontier AI, not their wider package (Washington Examiner, Jul 2026).
  • California's governor signed over two dozen privacy and AI bills, including a ban on employers relying solely on automated systems to discipline or dismiss staff (Wilson Sonsini, Oct 2026), and the state's air regulator has opened its platform for the first emissions reports (Jones Day, Sep 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal State attorneys general are becoming the AI enforcers. They can act on AI through consumer protection, privacy and civil rights law without any AI statute (Holtzman Vogel, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Beginning in 2027, California's emissions reporters need limited assurance over their direct and energy emissions, rising to reasonable assurance by 2030 (Mayer Brown, Aug 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the main US rules on AI and climate disclosure are written by states and enforced by state attorneys general, whatever happens to federal preemption.

What it means: Plan US compliance state by state, starting with California, and treat a federal override of state laws as a possibility, not a plan.

Counter-argument

The federal push may be winning without a court ruling. Colorado replaced its AI law with a narrower one that removes the duties at the core of the legal challenge (National Law Review, Jul 2026), and a court has blocked California's climate-risk law, SB 261, while the appeal is pending (Persefoni, Sep 2026).

5. Asia writes its own rulebooks

Longer-Range

Weight: low · Who decides: China's State Council, legislature and cyberspace regulator; Korea's science ministry; Japan's Cabinet; India's technology ministry · Room to act: Low before 2027 for most readers; high for firms deciding where to build, test and launch AI products

Asian governments are writing their own rules. China's government has committed to accelerate work on a comprehensive AI law while it enforces existing rules through campaigns; Korea's binding AI law is in force with fines deferred; Japan will review its light-touch law; and India's data rules phase in from November. Multinationals face several regimes, not one Asian model.

  • China's State Council commits to accelerate comprehensive legislation on the healthy development of AI (China's State Council, in Chinese, May 2026), but the national legislature lists it only for research and drafting, with sector rules first (Legal Daily, in Chinese, Jul 2026). The cyberspace regulator's campaign against AI misuse has removed more than 1,300 AI products from sale and 9 non-compliant open-source datasets (Cyberspace Administration of China, in Chinese, Jul 2026).
  • Korea's AI Basic Act and its decree took effect on 22 January 2026, with a grace period of at least one year on investigations and fines (US International Trade Administration, May 2026); its help desk logged 513 queries in two months from firms unsure whether the law applies to them (MTN, in Korean, May 2026).
  • Japan approved its second AI Basic Plan, and the Prime Minister said the government will actively review related rules, including the AI Promotion Act itself, so that it can evaluate high-performance AI (Japan's Prime Minister's Office, in Japanese, Jul 2026).
  • India's consent-manager rule takes effect on 13 November 2026 (King Stubb & Kasiva, Aug 2026), but its Data Protection Board had no chair and no members as of August (LiveLaw, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Japan is reconsidering light-touch AI rules. The government says it will review its AI Promotion Act so that it can evaluate high-performance AI (Japan's Prime Minister's Office, in Japanese, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

India's core data-protection duties start on 13 May 2027 (King Stubb & Kasiva, Aug 2026), and a University of California institute expects the most consequential decisions on AI governance over the next eighteen months to be made in Seoul and Tokyo (UC Institute on Global Conflict and Cooperation, Aug 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 Asia has several distinct AI regimes, none of them a copy of the EU's.

What it means: Build Asian compliance as separate national modules, and watch China's campaigns as closely as its legislation.

Counter-argument

Asia's rules may be slower and lighter than they look. China's legislature treats a unified AI law as preparatory work (Legal Daily, in Chinese, Jul 2026), Korea is deferring fines (MTN, in Korean, May 2026) and India's data protection board has no members (LiveLaw, Aug 2026).

Product and security teams should treat the reporting duty as live today, for products already on sale as well as new ones, rather than waiting for the fines that start in December 2027.

Action: Product security leads to name an owner and test the 24-hour early-warning process for every product with digital elements sold in the EU by the end of 2026.

Every region its own regulator

↗ Shift from todayKorea's grace period on fines ends (US International Trade Administration, May 2026) and Asian rules harden, while EU states still lack AI authorities and each regulator enforces its own rulebook.

In 2030 each region writes and enforces its own rules through its own regulators. Brussels, London, Washington's agencies, the US states, Beijing, Seoul, Tokyo and New Delhi each run separate inspections, registers and reporting portals. Compliance is organised by jurisdiction, and the cost of selling everywhere rises. Firms with strong local teams and good relations with regulators do best; smaller firms narrow the markets they serve.

Early indicators: Asian regulators start issuing fines; more EU states designate separate AI authorities; US state laws multiply.

Sue where it suits

↗ Shift from todayLiability claims over software begin in the first countries to legislate, such as Germany (German Bundestag, in German, Oct 2026), while US state attorneys general take on AI cases.

In 2030 the rules still differ by region, and the courts do much of the enforcing. Claimants bring cases where the law is most favourable, and state attorneys general use consumer protection law against AI products. Regulators are thin, but legal risk is high and uneven. Firms manage exposure through product design records, insurance and careful choices about where they launch.

Early indicators: The first software liability claims under the new rules; attorney general investigations of AI products; insurers price AI liability separately.

The managed baseline

↗ Shift from todayHarmonised standards follow the first published AI standard, on quality management (CEN-CENELEC, Jul 2026), and regulators in Asia and the US adopt them as a common baseline.

In 2030 a common technical baseline, set by international and European standards, is accepted in most major markets, and regulators supervise against it. Compliance is mostly a matter of meeting the standard once and proving it, with local additions. Costs fall for firms that sell widely, and the bodies that write standards hold much of the real power over the rules.

Early indicators: The EU publishes the remaining AI standards; Asian regulators reference them; mutual recognition talks begin.

Common rules, private policing

↗ Shift from todayLiability rules reach all member states, not just the few that have legislated so far (Faegre Drinker, Sep 2026), and contracts and insurers enforce shared standards.

In 2030 the rules have converged on shared standards, but regulators remain under-resourced, so enforcement runs through liability, insurance and customer contracts. Large customers and insurers demand proof of compliance before they buy or cover a product. Firms that can show clean records win business; those that cannot find it harder to sell, whatever the regulators do.

Early indicators: Insurers require standard certificates; procurement contracts cite the standards; few regulator fines but rising claims.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

AI assistants become regulated gatekeepers themselves

Window: 2030 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: The current rules treat AI assistants as challengers to be helped against Google, not as gatekeepers to be restrained.

The EU already classes ChatGPT as a search engine (European Commission, Aug 2026), and ChatGPT has told the Commission its users exceed the Digital Markets Act's thresholds (Eunews, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2027: the Commission designates an AI assistant as a gatekeeper under the Digital Markets Act.
  • By December 2028: an AI assistant provider is required to share data or interoperate with rival assistants.

What it touches today: Distribution, data and partnership deals with AI assistant providers signed on the assumption that the assistants stay lightly regulated.

Weak signal

Europe's vulnerability reports become the evidence in liability cases

Window: 2031 to 2037 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Firms treat security reports to regulators as an administrative duty, separate from their exposure in court.

Reports have gone to ENISA's platform since September (ENISA, Sep 2026), and the new liability rules treat software as a product from December (Dutch Senate, in Dutch, Sep 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: ENISA publishes its first figures on reports received through the Single Reporting Platform.
  • By December 2028: a product liability claim in an EU court relies on a Cyber Resilience Act report as evidence of a defect.

What it touches today: How vulnerability reports are worded, who signs them off, and whether legal teams see them before they are sent.

Weak signal

Asia's light-touch regimes end up controlling frontier AI more tightly than Europe

Window: 2031 to 2038 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: The common view is that Europe regulates AI hardest and Asia mainly promotes it.

Japan says it will review its AI Promotion Act so the government can evaluate high-performance AI (Japan's Prime Minister's Office, in Japanese, Jul 2026), and China's regulator enforces through mass campaigns (Cyberspace Administration of China, in Chinese, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: Japan amends its AI Promotion Act to give the government powers to evaluate high-performance AI.
  • By December 2028: China submits a draft comprehensive AI law to its national legislature.

What it touches today: Where frontier models are tested and first released, and which regulators see them before launch.

Options: Incident reporting (product and security leaders)

Option A · Least change

Report through the existing incident team

Handle Cyber Resilience Act reports through the current security incident process when a case arises.

Trade-off: Cheapest now, but the 24-hour clock is easy to miss when the process was built for a different purpose.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The managed baseline; Common rules, private policing

Option B · Middle course

A dedicated reporting desk

Set up a named team, templates and on-call cover for EU product reports.

Trade-off: Meets the clock reliably, at the cost of a standing team before fines apply.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Every region its own regulator; The managed baseline

Option C · Biggest shift

One global disclosure process

Run one vulnerability disclosure process for all markets, built to the strictest clock.

Trade-off: The most work now, but it also produces the records that defend liability claims anywhere.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Sue where it suits; Common rules, private policing

Options: Liability and contracts (general counsel)

Option A · Least change

Wait for national laws

Update contracts and product files country by country as each national law arrives.

Trade-off: Avoids work on rules that may change, but leaves products exposed where the law arrives first.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Every region its own regulator; The managed baseline

Option B · Middle course

Update contracts now

Rewrite supplier and customer contracts for software liability and evidence duties from the EU date.

Trade-off: Steady and defensible, though some clauses may need revising when national laws differ.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Sue where it suits; Common rules, private policing

Option C · Biggest shift

Design for evidence and insure

Keep design and test records as if for court, and buy cover for AI and software liability.

Trade-off: The highest cost, but the strongest position if claimants and insurers become the main enforcers.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Sue where it suits; Common rules, private policing

Options: Global compliance design (boards and compliance leaders)

Option A · Least change

Use EU law as the global baseline

Build to the EU's rules everywhere and add local requirements where needed.

Trade-off: Simple to run, but over-complies in light-touch markets and misses rules the EU does not have.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The managed baseline

Option B · Middle course

Regional modules on a common core

Keep a shared core, with separate modules for the US states, the EU, the UK and the main Asian markets.

Trade-off: Fits the rules as they are, at the price of more coordination.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Every region its own regulator; Sue where it suits

Option C · Biggest shift

Help shape the standards

Put experts into standards bodies and regulatory sandboxes to influence the baseline others will use.

Trade-off: Slow and costly, but gives a say in the rules that may matter most by 2030.

What We Are Not Planning For

Delay

A further postponement of the AI Act's high-risk rules beyond 2027 and 2028

The new dates are now in law (White & Case, Aug 2026), and the first harmonised standard has been published (CEN-CENELEC, Jul 2026).

↺ Reinstated if the Commission proposes another amendment to the dates, or the remaining standards miss their timetable again in 2027.

Override

A federal law overriding US state AI laws before 2028

Thirty-six state attorneys general oppose it (Holtzman Vogel, Jul 2026), and the bipartisan bill introduced covers only frontier AI (Washington Examiner, Jul 2026).

↺ Reinstated if Congress attaches an override of state AI laws to must-pass legislation.

Convergence

A single global AI rulebook, or mutual recognition between the EU, the US and Asia, by 2030

Korea's law is binding while Japan's is promotional (UC Institute on Global Conflict and Cooperation, Aug 2026), and China is taking its own path (Legal Daily, in Chinese, Jul 2026).

↺ Reinstated if the EU and a major Asian jurisdiction agree to recognise each other's AI conformity assessments.
