Why This Matters Now

Shoppers are spending at the two ends of the shelf. Premium products that can prove they work are growing, and so are cheap ones, including shops' own brands. The ordinary mid-priced brand in the middle, which most manufacturers are built around, is losing from both sides.

The cheap end looks different in each region. In the US, cuts to food support and higher prices are pushing more households to the cheapest products. In Europe, shoppers have decided shops' own brands are good enough. In India and other emerging markets, millions of people are buying branded goods for the first time, in small low-price packs.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: plan the range for both ends of the shelf, with a different plan for the cheap end in each region, rather than defending the middle by habit.

Executive Synthesis

Which of your brands this week are priced for a middle that has already emptied out?

In the US the top fifth of households now account for nearly 60% of consumer spending (CPG Matters, on Moody's Analytics, Mar 2026), and across Europe's six biggest markets half of grocery units are now shops' own brands (Circana, Apr 2026).

Demand for everyday branded goods has split. Premium and value are both growing while the mid-priced middle loses from both sides. In the US the top 20% of households account for nearly 60% of consumer spending (CPG Matters, on Moody's Analytics, Mar 2026), McKinsey finds premium spending pulling away from the mainstream even as shoppers scrutinise value (McKinsey, Jun 2026), and Bain sees the same split in China (Bain, Jun 2026). This is where the market now lives, not a passing reaction to inflation.

What is unsettled is the cheap end, which has three different causes. In the US, food support has fallen by more than 4 million people (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, Jun 2026) and tariffs weigh hardest on the poorest households (The Budget Lab at Yale, Apr 2026). In Europe, shops' own brands reached a record 50% of grocery units (Circana, Apr 2026) because shoppers now judge them good enough. In India, rural buyers are entering branded goods through small packs and now account for 42% of super-premium volume (Worldpanel by Kantar, via MediaBrief, Sep 2025). Premium, meanwhile, now grows on proof of results rather than heritage (Kearney, via Premium Beauty News, Feb 2026).

Looking ahead five to ten years, the analysis expects the two ends to keep pulling apart. US grocery prices are forecast to rise 2.8% in 2026 (USDA Economic Research Service, Jun 2026), and the IMF expects the slowdown to fall hardest on emerging economies (IMF, Apr 2026). Manufacturers are already reshaping ranges: Procter & Gamble runs brands at distinct price tiers (Procter & Gamble, via The Motley Fool, Apr 2026) and Unilever is cutting weaker brands (Unilever, Apr 2026). How far to move, and how differently in each region, is the choice in front of leadership teams: section 7 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The load-bearing assumption is that the empty middle is permanent. If real incomes recover strongly, some shoppers could return to mainstream brands. So far the opposite holds: US national brands grew sales 2.2% mainly by cutting prices, without winning back unit share from shops' own brands (BakeryandSnacks, on Circana, Jul 2026).

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Rebuild the range as a price-and-pack ladder with strong top and bottom rungs. The middle is where demand is thinnest and competition fiercest.
  2. Write a separate value-tier plan for each region. The US, Europe and emerging markets have different causes, so one global answer fits none of them.
  3. Base every premium claim on proof, and retire heritage-only lines. Premium that cannot show a benefit is the most exposed position on the shelf.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Premium grows on efficacy and format, not heritage

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Brand owners; shoppers and cheaper copies set the test · Room to act: High: claims and ranges are yours to change

Premium is the growing top end, but what shoppers pay for has changed. They now pay for results they can see and for better formats, not for a famous name alone. Every premium product that relies on heritage needs checking now, because the shopper who once bought the story is buying the proof. The most exposed brands this cycle are premium brands whose price rests on legacy rather than a benefit the shopper can feel.

  • L'Oréal Luxe grew 5.6% in the first quarter of 2026 and beat its market in every region, which the company puts down to its range and new products rather than heritage (L'Oréal, Apr 2026). Its French-language release gives the same figure (L'Oréal, in French, Apr 2026).
  • US prestige beauty sales rose 6% to $8.1 billion in the first quarter, led by higher-value formats and minis, while mass beauty rose 7% to $18.1 billion (Circana, May 2026).
  • In a survey of prestige buyers, 85% ranked quality and efficacy above price, reviews or heritage (Kearney, via Premium Beauty News, Feb 2026). NielsenIQ sees premium winning on efficacy and experience while cheaper brands compete on performance for the money (NielsenIQ, Apr 2026).

Outlook: the next 5 to 10 years

NielsenIQ expects premium to keep winning on efficacy and experience (NielsenIQ, Apr 2026), but the same active ingredients are moving down-market fast: in UK skin care, mass grew 21% against 7% for premium (Cosmetics Business, on Kantar, Mar 2026). Over five to ten years, on the analysis's reading, a premium price will hold only where the benefit is hard to copy.

What it means: A premium brand needs a benefit that is both real and hard to copy; one of the two is no longer enough.

Counter-argument

The same efficacy logic may erode the price gap premium relies on. Mass and prestige beauty grew at nearly the same rate for the first time in five years (BeautyMatter, on Circana, May 2026). This sharpens the theme rather than overturning it: efficacy that spreads turns yesterday's premium into today's mainstream, which is why heritage without proof is the exposed position.

2. The middle is being vacated from both ends

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Manufacturers' portfolio owners · Room to act: High, but every move costs a brand

The mid-priced mainstream brand, the asset most portfolios are built around, is being squeezed from above and below at once. Premium pulls spending up and shops' own brands pull volume down. This forces the hardest portfolio decision of the cycle: which mid-tier brands to move up, which to move down to fight for the value shopper, and which to retire.

  • The top 20% of US households now account for nearly 60% of consumer spending, and for consumer goods companies the middle has evaporated (CPG Matters, on Moody's Analytics, Mar 2026).
  • The pattern is global. McKinsey finds premium spending pulling away from the mainstream even as shoppers scrutinise value (McKinsey, Jun 2026), Bain finds the same split in China, where local and shop brands take much of the demand (Bain, Jun 2026), and a Canadian review describes a market that increasingly resembles a K (Retail Insider, Jul 2026).
  • Manufacturers are already reshaping their ranges. Procter & Gamble told investors it runs brands at distinct price tiers, giving shoppers a choice between premium innovation and a cheaper option (Procter & Gamble, via The Motley Fool, Apr 2026).

Outlook: the next 5 to 10 years

McKinsey's 2026 reading (McKinsey, Jun 2026) and the Canadian review (Retail Insider, Jul 2026) both describe the split as continuing rather than easing. The analysis expects mid-tier brands to keep losing share over five to ten years unless real incomes recover strongly, which no market has yet shown.

What it means: Holding the middle should be a deliberate choice in everyday habit categories, not the default.

Counter-argument

The middle may be quiet rather than empty. Plenty of mainstream demand persists in habitual categories where shoppers neither trade up nor down, and a manufacturer that abandons the middle too fast could hand a profitable base to a more patient rival. The risk this cycle is over-abandonment. But no major market has shown the mid-tier regaining share, so the safer error is to build both ends first and hold the middle deliberately.

Decision link: Strategic Implications 1 and 4.

3. The US value tier is expanding by policy, not just trading down

Immediate

Weight: medium · Who decides: The US federal government (food support and tariffs) · Room to act: Low on the cause; high on the response

The cheap end in the US is not only the usual trade-down that reverses when confidence returns. Government policy is adding households to it that were previously stable, so the value shopper is a growing group rather than a temporary visitor. That makes the US value tier a segment to design for deliberately, because people entering it in 2026 may not leave when the economy turns.

  • Between the 2025 budget law and March 2026, the number of people receiving SNAP food support fell by more than 4 million, about 10%, in every state (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, Jun 2026).
  • Tariffs act like a tax that hits the poorest hardest: their cost as a share of income is about three times higher for the poorest tenth of households than the richest, at 1.1% against 0.4% (The Budget Lab at Yale, Apr 2026).
  • Shops' own brands have settled at a record share of US grocery. They reached 23.8% of units in the first half of 2026, and national brands grew sales 2.2% mainly by cutting prices (BakeryandSnacks, on Circana, Jul 2026). Own-label is now a $330 billion business with a 24% unit share (Circana, Mar 2026).

Outlook: the next 5 to 10 years

The USDA forecasts grocery prices to rise 2.8% in 2026 (USDA Economic Research Service, Jun 2026), and a food economist warns they could reach 4% to 4.5% by the end of the year as tariffs pass through (FoodNavigator, May 2026). Both policies are set on multi-year paths, so the analysis expects the larger US value group to last well beyond one economic cycle.

What it means: Plan for the US value shopper as a lasting segment, not a visitor who will leave.

Counter-argument

US growth still looks healthy, with the IMF projecting 2.3% for 2026 (IMF, Apr 2026), so the value-tier growth is a story about who gets what inside a healthy total, and it could reverse if tariffs were cut or food support restored. That is a test of how long it lasts rather than a rebuttal: the value tier grows for as long as the policies hold.

Decision link: Strategic Implications 2 and 4.

4. Europe's own-label quality perception has reset for good

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Shoppers and European retailers · Room to act: Medium: compete on proof, not promotion

Europe's cheap end reflects a lasting change in how shoppers judge shops' own brands, not a reaction to tight budgets. Own-label now takes half of grocery units in the biggest markets and is holding as incomes recover, because shoppers who traded down between 2021 and 2023 decided it is good enough. For branded manufacturers this lowers the ceiling on how much of the basket a national brand can expect to hold.

Private label share of grocery by European market, 2025 to 2026

Sources: six-market unit share and Spain and Netherlands (Circana private label in Europe, April 2026); Germany (Lebensmittelpraxis, on YouGov, June 2026); France volume share (LSA Conso, on NielsenIQ, March 2026).

  • Shops' own brands reached a record 50% of grocery units across Europe's six biggest markets, led by Spain at 59% and the Netherlands at 56%, rising every year since 2021 (Circana, Apr 2026).
  • The shift is about perceived quality, not price, and it is holding as budgets ease. Own-label reached 40% of value across 11 EU markets, and its share and perceived quality drove retailers' market-share gains more than price (McKinsey, via ESM, Apr 2026). French household purchasing power steadied in the first quarter (INSEE, in French, May 2026), yet own-label kept gaining.
  • Shoppers now treat own-label as a legitimate choice rather than a compromise (DairyReporter, on Circana, Jun 2026). In Germany shops' own brands reached a record 47%, up from 41% in 2021 (Lebensmittelpraxis, on YouGov, in German, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Young French shoppers are drifting back to brands. NielsenIQ data show under-35s in France buying less own-label while their branded buying edged up (LSA Conso, on NielsenIQ, in French, Mar 2026). Two more quarters of this in more than one market would suggest the change belongs to one generation rather than being permanent.

Outlook: the next 5 to 10 years

Own-label share has risen every year since 2021 (Circana, Apr 2026), and retailers' own-label quality was the biggest driver of their market-share gains (McKinsey, via ESM, Apr 2026). Over five to ten years the analysis expects the brand ceiling to stay lower than before 2021, even if younger shoppers partly drift back.

What it means: European brands should compete on proof of benefit rather than try to out-promote retailers.

Counter-argument

The change may prove generational, softening as shoppers who never lived through the 2021 to 2023 squeeze reach spending age. The early drift among French under-35s is the signal to watch (LSA Conso, on NielsenIQ, in French, Mar 2026). Even so, a decade of retailer investment in own-label quality means a partial return would still leave the brand ceiling well below where it was.

5. Emerging-market entry is sachet-led, and it is not distress

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Manufacturers in each market, within the wider economy · Room to act: High for pack and distribution design

In emerging markets, led by India, the cheap end is the opposite of decline. Millions of rural and small-town shoppers are buying branded goods for the first time, mostly in small low-price packs and sachets. Reading them as shoppers in distress is the mistake that loses the fastest-growing volume of the decade. The task is to make small-pack entry pay and to win the second and third purchase.

  • Rural India is trading up through small packs. Rural buyers now account for 42% of super-premium volume, up from 30% in 2021, and premium brands make up 15% of FMCG volume (Worldpanel by Kantar, via MediaBrief, Sep 2025).
  • Rural demand is growing faster than urban (Dabur, via Business Standard, Jul 2026), and Hindustan Unilever posted its strongest quarter in three years, with 8% revenue growth on 6% volume growth (Hindustan Unilever, via ScanX, May 2026).
  • Nestlé India says growth will come from reaching more households and wider distribution, describing India as dozens of markets layered by income, place and aspiration (Nestlé India, via Business Standard, Jul 2026).

Outlook: the next 5 to 10 years

The IMF expects global growth to slow to 3.1% in 2026 before edging up in 2027, with emerging economies hit hardest (IMF, Apr 2026). Rural India's share of super-premium volume has risen steadily since 2021 (Worldpanel by Kantar, via MediaBrief, Sep 2025). Over five to ten years the analysis expects first-time buyers to become regular customers where manufacturers make small packs pay.

What it means: Whether shoppers buy again is the one number that decides if this is entry or distress.

Counter-argument

First purchases do not always become habits, and a slower economy could strand them. NielsenIQ still reads the rural-urban split partly as affordability stress (NielsenIQ, May 2026), and the IMF expects the slowdown to fall hardest on emerging economies (IMF, Apr 2026). The entry thesis holds only if first purchases turn into repeat purchases before conditions tighten.

6. The portfolio, pricing and pack architecture that wins each region

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: Leadership teams and regional presidents · Room to act: High, over several planning cycles

The answer to a split market is not one global plan but a range, pricing and pack design tuned to each region, because the cheap end has a different cause in each. The common move is to stop funding growth with price rises and promotions that no longer work, and to focus on fewer, stronger products. This is a multi-year build to revisit each cycle as the three regions move at different speeds.

One K-shape, three regional pack-and-price playbooks

The lower arm has three causes and three answers; the common move is to prune and repair base value (NielsenIQ Innovation under pressure, January 2026).

  • Price and promotion have stopped buying growth. One in four Western European units already sells on promotion (NielsenIQ, Feb 2026), sales of new products fell 5.8% in units as launches failed to justify a higher price (NielsenIQ, Jan 2026), and price rises are slowing, with Nestlé at 2.3% in the first quarter (Nestlé, Apr 2026).
  • Premium only pays when its extra cost is managed. US own-label revenue rose $64.8 billion, or 30%, between 2021 and 2025, and 70% of consumers now think own-label is as good as brands (Oliver Wyman, Jun 2026).
  • Growth is being won in emerging markets, which rewards local pack and range design. Mondelez grew revenue 12.1% in Latin America and 14.3% in Asia, the Middle East and Africa (FoodNavigator, Jul 2026), while Unilever focuses on its biggest brands and cuts unprofitable ones (Unilever, Apr 2026).

Outlook: the next 5 to 10 years

NielsenIQ's prescription for Western Europe is fewer, stronger products (NielsenIQ, Jan 2026), and growth is shifting to emerging markets where own-label competition is weaker (FoodNavigator, Jul 2026). Over five to ten years the analysis expects the winners to run one set of global brands and supply chains with different pack and price ladders in each region.

What it means: Keep brands and supply global, but let pack sizes and price points differ by region.

Counter-argument

One global operating model is cheaper to run than three regional ones, and splitting the range by region could give up the scale that makes large manufacturers competitive. But scale applied to the wrong shape is a cost: a range centred on the vanishing middle spreads investment where demand is thinnest.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Rebuild the price-and-pack ladder to span both ends

The Chief Growth Officer should treat the range as a ladder with strong top and bottom rungs, not a range centred on the mainstream, because demand and margin have moved to the two ends (CPG Matters, on Moody's Analytics, Mar 2026). Every mid-tier brand should get an explicit verdict this planning cycle: move up on a provable benefit, move down to compete for the value shopper, or retire and redirect its money. Holding the middle should be a deliberate choice in habit categories.

Action: By the next annual planning round, the Chief Growth Officer maps every mainstream brand to move up, move down or retire, with a two-year timetable and reinvestment plan.

Decide Draws on Themes 1, 2 and 6

Implication 2: Write a separate value-tier plan for each region

The Chief Growth Officer and regional leads should stop treating the value tier as one global segment. In the US it is enlarged by policy (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, Jun 2026) and calls for a clear position on own-label supply and a credible low-price brand. In Europe the own-label reset (Circana, Apr 2026) calls for competing on proof rather than promotion. In emerging markets it is first-time entry (Worldpanel by Kantar, via MediaBrief, Sep 2025) and calls for small packs that pay and a plan for the second purchase.

Action: Within two planning cycles, each region publishes its own value-tier plan, owned by the regional president.

Implication 3: Base every premium claim on proof and format, and retire heritage-only lines

The Chief Brand and Marketing Officer should check the premium range for lines whose price rests on heritage rather than a benefit the shopper can feel (Kearney, via Premium Beauty News, Feb 2026), because cheaper copies and a stronger own-label punish those first. Where a claim can rest on proven efficacy or a better format, it should, with the evidence visible on shelf and online. Where it cannot, the line should be repriced or retired.

Action: The Chief Brand and Marketing Officer completes a premium-claim audit within two quarters and reprices or retires the lines that fail it by the next range review.

Implication 4: Set up a regional early-warning dashboard

The Chief Financial Officer and strategy team should track the three regions on the measures that would confirm or overturn this reading: US food-support numbers and any tariff change (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, Jun 2026), European own-label share by age group (LSA Conso, on NielsenIQ, in French, Mar 2026), and repeat-purchase rates in emerging markets. A turn in any region should reach the portfolio decision before the next plan is set.

Action: The strategy team launches the dashboard within one quarter and reports to the leadership team monthly.

Two Brand Worlds

Five to ten years out, demand has split further, but manufacturers hold both ends with their own brands. Own-label has levelled off because brands invested early in proven premium products and credible low-price brands. The middle has been thinned on purpose, and the prize went to those who moved first on both ends.

Early indicators: Own-label share flattening; branded low-price lines gaining units; premium claims holding their price gaps.

Retailers Own the Floor

Five to ten years out, demand has split further and shops' own brands have taken the cheap end outright. Own-label is well past half of units in more markets, and brands that did not secure the value tier early find it closed. Manufacturers live on proven premium products and on supplying own-label on retailers' terms.

Early indicators: Own-label rising well past half of units in more markets; retailers' premium own-label ranges growing; national brands leaving value categories.

The Middle Refills

Five to ten years out, real incomes have recovered, traded-down shoppers have returned and own-label has levelled off, so the market drifts back towards a bell. Manufacturers that abandoned the middle too fast find a patient rival has held it. Promotions regain some power and the premium price gap narrows.

Early indicators: Mid-tier national brands gaining units for two or more quarters; real incomes growing faster; promotions adding volume again.

Quality-Value Convergence

Five to ten years out, incomes have recovered but the taste for good-value quality has not faded. The middle refills with premium own-label and good-value brands rather than the old mainstream, and everyone competes on quality for the price. Manufacturers win only where their benefit is clearly better or their costs clearly lower.

Early indicators: Premium own-label growing faster than standard own-label; price gaps between brands and own-label narrowing; efficacy claims becoming the norm.

What We Are Not Planning For

Not planned for

A near-term return of the mainstream middle as the main source of growth

The middle is being drained from both ends by causes that last years, from a policy-enlarged US value tier (Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, Jun 2026) to a decade-long European own-label reset (Circana, Apr 2026). No major market has shown the mid-tier regaining share, so planning for a recovery would put money where demand is thinnest.

↺ Reinstated if mid-tier national brands gain unit share for two consecutive quarters in both the US and Europe's six largest markets.

Not planned for

Own-label collapsing under a price war from national brands

US national brands have already cut prices without winning back unit share (BakeryandSnacks, on Circana, Jul 2026), and Europe's own-label growth rests on perceived quality rather than price alone (McKinsey, via ESM, Apr 2026). A price war would cut everyone's margin and is unlikely to win back shoppers who now see own-label as a legitimate choice.

↺ Reinstated if national brands recover unit share against own-label in Europe's six biggest markets for two consecutive quarters.

Not planned for

Small-pack demand in emerging markets vanishing as incomes rise

The evidence points to first-time buying and trading up through small packs, with rural buyers taking a rising share of super-premium volume (Worldpanel by Kantar, via MediaBrief, Sep 2025). The risk is under-investing in small-pack economics, not being over-exposed to a fragile group.

↺ Reinstated if rural repeat-purchase rates fall sharply even as emerging-market incomes and confidence improve.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If the middle is empty rather than quiet, which of our mid-tier brands do we move up, move down or retire, and what is the two-year sequence and cost?
  2. In the US, do we make or partner on own-label and keep a credible low-price brand, or leave the cheap end to retailers and put our money into premium?
  3. In Europe, do we accept a permanently lower ceiling for brands and compete on proof, or spend to win back shoppers we may not recover?
  4. In emerging markets, do our small packs lead to profitable repeat purchases, or are we paying for trial that will not convert if the economy tightens, and how would we know in time?
  5. If young French shoppers are the first to drift back to brands (LSA Conso, on NielsenIQ, in French, Mar 2026), are we planning for a change that may belong to one generation rather than last?
