Why This Matters Now

Air taxis are about to carry their first paying passengers in the Gulf. They will matter as a symbol and as a premium service, but each vertiport is built for the traffic of a small terminal, not a city.

The changes that could reshape how millions of people move over the next decade are arriving more quietly: cars that drive themselves, new rail lines, tunnels and automated pods, and freight that moves without a driver. All of them are real, all are small today, and all sit under targets that assume rapid growth. This year’s war also showed how quickly visitor demand and low-altitude flying can stop.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: judge the next flying taxi by the trips it will carry, not the launch it makes, and track which candidates are closing the gap to their targets each quarter.

Executive Synthesis

Which plans on your desk assume the next big change in Gulf mobility will arrive the way air taxis did, as a launch date?

The evidence in this briefing says it is arriving as fleets and corridors, and Dubai’s driverless taxis still complete around 50 trips a day.

Air taxis have become the Gulf’s symbol of what comes next. The UAE’s civil aviation regulator has certified a purpose-built commercial vertiport at Dubai International, which it describes as the world’s first (WAM, Jul 2026, Arabic), moved Archer’s aircraft onto a restricted certificate that allows limited commercial flights in Abu Dhabi (WAM, via Zawya, May 2026), and Joby still targets its first passengers in 2026 (Joby Aviation, Aug 2026). Yet the vertiport is designed for up to 170,000 passengers a year (The National, Apr 2026), while Dubai’s public transport, shared mobility and taxis carry about 1.9 million riders a day (Dubai Media Office, Aug 2026, Arabic). The developments with the scale to change the sector are driverless vehicles and new fixed corridors. Both are real, and both are small.

What is settled is that driverless taxis are now a commercial service: Dubai runs 144 of them with three Chinese partners, against a target of 25% of all trips by 2030 (Dubai Media Office, Aug 2026), and Saudi Arabia has amended its traffic regulations so that vehicles can move with no driver on board (Saudi Press Agency, Jun 2026, Arabic). What is not settled is how fast they scale, since the Dubai fleet completes around 50 trips a day (Dubai Media Office, Aug 2026); whether one software failure can halt a fleet, as one did in Wuhan (electrive, Apr 2026); and how fully visitor demand and low-altitude flying recover from the 2026 war, after drone strikes on Dubai and Abu Dhabi airports (The National, May 2026) and a fall in Dubai Taxi trips from 13.6 million to 10.3 million in the second quarter (Dubai Taxi Company, Jul 2026).

Looking ahead to 2035, the corridors carry the clearest dates: Dubai Loop’s first phase is expected to open in 2028 (The National, Sep 2026), the GCC railway targets full operation by December 2030 (Annahar, May 2026), and the Abu Dhabi to Dubai high-speed line is scheduled for completion in 2031 (International Railway Journal, Aug 2026). Saudi Arabia’s sovereign-backed AI company plans thousands of autonomous trucks on its logistics corridors by 2030 (Supply Chain Xchange, Aug 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

This reading assumes that targets set for 2030 still drive budgets. If the fall in visitors runs on through 2027, or a second fleet-wide failure leads a Gulf regulator to pause driverless permits, the next decade’s change will come mainly from metro and rail, which are already growing: Riyadh Metro carried 29.7 million passengers in the second quarter, a nearly 26% increase on a year earlier (Arab News, Aug 2026).

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Measure every candidate for the next flying taxi by the trips it carries, not by its launch. Dubai’s driverless taxis are commercial but still small.
  2. Write the rules for a fleet-wide failure before driverless fleets grow. A single system failure stranded robotaxis in Wuhan and led China to suspend new licences.
  3. Plan low-altitude services that can survive a security shutdown. Drone deliveries in Dubai restarted only in September, route by route.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal Hydrogen and autonomy in one truck. Saudi Arabia’s transport authority approved a pilot of an autonomous hydrogen fuel-cell heavy truck with Procter & Gamble (Hydrogen Fuel News, Jun 2026). It gains weight if a second shipper commits a corridor to driverless hydrogen freight within a year.
  • Weak signal A drone economy at national scale, next door to the Gulf’s suppliers. China recorded 3.3 million registered civil drones completing 45.3 million flight hours in 2025 (East Asia Forum, Oct 2026), and Gulf drone services already run on Chinese platforms. It gains weight if a Gulf authority adopts China’s rules for drone corridors rather than writing its own.

Outlook to 2035

The International Energy Agency projects that the global electric car fleet could reach as high as 510 million by 2035 (IEA, May 2026), and BloombergNEF expects 52% of passenger vehicles to be electric by then (BloombergNEF, Jun 2026). In the Gulf, the GCC railway targets full operation by December 2030 and expects passengers to exceed eight million by 2045 (Annahar, May 2026), Etihad Rail’s passenger numbers are expected to reach 36 million by 2030 (The National, Sep 2026), and the GCC population grew at an average annual rate of 3.5% to about 62.8 million in 2025 (Qatar News Agency, Jul 2026).

What it means: By 2035 the Gulf’s busiest new modes are likely to run on rails and roads, electric and increasingly driverless, with air taxis as a fast premium layer above them.

Counter-argument

The strongest challenge is that driverless mobility may stay a showcase for most of the decade. The leading platforms lose more than they earn: WeRide lost RMB400.7 million in the second quarter on revenue of RMB231.7 million (WeRide, Aug 2026), and Pony.ai’s net loss was US$45.4 million on revenue of US$36.2 million (Automotive World, Aug 2026); Waymo recalled 3,791 vehicles after one drove into a flooded road (Transport Topics, May 2026); and corridor projects have a long record of delay, with the Saudi Landbridge first announced in 2004 and put on hold in 2010 (MEED, Aug 2026), and no hyperloop yet carrying passengers anywhere (Electrek, Sep 2026). On that reading the decade’s change comes from metro and conventional rail, and the frontier modes stay small. This briefing’s reading is that both happen: corridors carry the volume, and driverless vehicles decide how people reach them. The test is whether Dubai’s driverless trips reach the thousands a day by the end of 2027.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Judge the next flying taxi by trips carried, and fund two candidates to scale

The strategy and investment board should choose, by the end of the first quarter of 2027, which two candidates get money to scale, and set each a daily-trips test. The evidence favours the modes with dated capacity: Dubai Loop’s first phase is expected to carry more than 13,000 people a day from 2028 (The National, Sep 2026), and Etihad Rail had sold 70,000 tickets by August (International Railway Journal, Aug 2026), while driverless taxis are commercial but complete around 50 trips a day (Dubai Media Office, Aug 2026). Spreading money across every pilot keeps options open but lets none reach the scale at which its costs fall. This is Decide rather than Prepare because the 2030 targets leave about three budget cycles to show progress.

Action: by 31 March 2027, the strategy office presents a scorecard of the seven candidates in Theme 1, with daily-trips targets for 2028 and 2030 for the two it recommends funding.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 1, driverless trips carried, the measure that tells whether R1 is turning.

Implication 2: Write the rules for a fleet-wide failure before driverless fleets grow

The regulator and its operators should agree what happens when one supplier’s software fails across a fleet. In Wuhan a system failure stranded robotaxis and China suspended new licences (electrive, Apr 2026), and Waymo recalled 3,791 vehicles after one drove into a flooded road (Transport Topics, May 2026). Dubai’s three robotaxi partners are all Chinese platforms (Dubai Media Office, Aug 2026), and UAE law already places strict civil liability on operators and protects their records from alteration (Bracewell, Aug 2026). It is Prepare because fleets are still small enough to set multi-supplier and fallback rules before contracts lock them in.

Action: before the next driverless permit round, the regulator publishes a fleet-failure protocol: a cap on the share of trips on any one software supplier, a fallback service plan, and shared access to incident data.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 2, the fleet share on one supplier’s software, which decides how hard B1 can brake.

Implication 3: Plan low-altitude services that can survive a security shutdown

Air-taxi and drone planners should assume that airspace can close at short notice. Drone strikes targeted both main UAE airports after Iran began its attacks on 28 February (The National, May 2026); Dubai’s drone deliveries resumed in September under route-specific clearances (Unmanned Airspace, Sep 2026); and Sharjah now requires prior permission and registration for every drone (WAM, Jun 2026, Arabic). The first air-taxi vertiport sits at Dubai International, one of the airports that was targeted. It is Prepare because the first passenger services will launch on rules written before the war.

Action: before air-taxi passenger services begin, the operator and the aviation authorities agree a shutdown and restart plan with a ground alternative for every vertiport route, and test it once a year.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 3, permits and route clearances, where the security switch sets B2 Permit ceiling.

Implication 4: Track the corridor dates and the gap to the 2030 targets each quarter

The planning team should keep a short watch list of the dated commitments that decide which future arrives: Dubai Loop in 2028 (The National, Sep 2026), the GCC railway in December 2030 (Annahar, May 2026), the high-speed line in 2031 (International Railway Journal, Aug 2026), and the targets of 25% of trips driverless by 2030 in Dubai (Dubai Media Office, Aug 2026) and smart or autonomous by 2040 in Abu Dhabi (Abu Dhabi Media Office, Jun 2026). It is Monitor because none is yet slipping in a way that changes today’s decisions; the value lies in seeing the first slip early.

Action: from January 2027, the planning team reports each quarter on the seven candidates and these five commitments, and marks which scenario the readings point towards.

Monitor Draws on Theme 1. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 4, corridor capacity, which sets how fast R2 turns.

The Networked Gulf

↗ Shift from todayFrom a GCC railway whose progress has passed 50% (Annahar, May 2026) to one rail and visa market across six states, with authority-run driverless feeders at every main station.

By 2035 cross-border rail and a single tourist visa make the Gulf one travel market. Transport authorities run driverless fleets as part of public transport, feeding metro and rail stations, and own the trip data. Air taxis are a premium link between airports and districts. Growth is steady rather than spectacular, paced by public budgets.

Early indicators: The GCC railway opens on time; an authority tenders a driverless feeder network rather than licensing a platform; the unified tourist visa launches.

Platform Gulf

↗ Shift from todayFrom about 400 WeRide robotaxis in the Middle East (WeRide, Aug 2026) to global platforms running most driverless and air-taxi trips in the Gulf’s main cities.

By 2035 global platforms run the driverless taxis, the air taxis and the delivery drones, and compete across the Gulf. Visitors have returned and trips grow fast, but the data and much of the margin sit with the platforms, and authorities regulate rather than operate.

Early indicators: Platform fleets in the Gulf double within a year; a platform wins a multi-emirate licence; air-taxi fares fall towards taxi prices.

Corridor States

↗ Shift from todayFrom Riyadh Metro’s 29.7 million passengers a quarter (Arab News, Aug 2026) to fixed corridors carrying most new trips, with low-altitude flying a minor mode.

By 2035 security comes first. Low-altitude flying is tightly permitted and often paused, so growth goes to rail, metro, tunnels and automated pods that the state controls. Driverless vehicles run mainly in closed corridors, depots and freight routes.

Early indicators: A second airspace closure within two years; new drone laws in more emirates; metro and rail ridership keeps rising by double digits.

Gated Premium

↗ Shift from todayFrom air-taxi fares floated at Dh300 to Dh350 for inner-city trips (The National, Apr 2026) to premium services that reach few people and pause with each security alert.

By 2035 platforms serve affluent residents and visitors with premium driverless and air services under rules that differ emirate by emirate, while public transport grows slowly. Disruptions are frequent, and the next mode is a luxury rather than a network.

Early indicators: Air-taxi services launch on airport routes only; driverless permits stay emirate-specific; visitor numbers stay below their 2025 level.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

Driverless freight, not passenger taxis, becomes the Gulf’s first autonomy at scale

Window: 2029 to 2034 · Probability band: Medium (40 to 60%)

Why it would surprise: Autonomy targets count passenger trips, and freight corridors are planned around conventional trucks.

Saudi Arabia’s sovereign-backed AI company plans thousands of autonomous trucks on its logistics corridors by 2030 (Supply Chain Xchange, Aug 2026), and the country’s transport authority has approved a pilot of an autonomous hydrogen truck (Hydrogen Fuel News, Jun 2026).

Signposts to watch:
