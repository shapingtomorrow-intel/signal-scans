Which Moves Hold in Every Future for Gulf Mobility to 2035

19 October 2026 · Built on the Decision Intelligence briefing Small Fleets, Big Targets and its Causal Loop Diagram · Horizons: 2035 and 2040 Uses the briefing’s own scenarios, options and evidence; no new sources. Grades are drafted by analysis, to be challenged and judged by people.
Scanned and filtered by AI. Stress-tested and judged by humans.

Scenarios are not forecasts. Their job is to show how differently the next decade could turn out, so that a plan can be built to survive any of them, and then to be watched, so that the plan changes when the evidence does.

This page does both. It takes the nine options in the briefing and runs each one through four plausible futures to 2035, plus a wild card to 2040. A move that works in every future is robust and can be committed to now. A move that works well in some futures and poorly in others is a bet, held ready with a clear trigger. A move that makes things worse in any future is fragile and should not be relied on. It then scores the leading indicators for each future, to show which one the evidence is moving towards this quarter.

1. Four futures to 2035, and a wild card to 2040

The two axes are the system’s two biggest uncertainties, the same two the Causal Loop Diagram identifies: who runs the driverless flywheel, transport authorities or global platforms, and whether security keeps Gulf travel and airspace guarded or lets them open up.

Open · authorities

The Networked Gulf

Cross-border rail and a single tourist visa make the Gulf one travel market; authorities run driverless feeders as part of public transport and own the trip data.

By 2040: Public networks absorb the new modes. The open question is whether public budgets can keep pace with demand.

Open · platforms

Platform Gulf

Global platforms run the driverless taxis, air taxis and delivery drones across the Gulf; trips grow fast, but the data and much of the margin sit with them.

By 2040: A few platforms carry most new trips. Authorities regulate an industry they no longer operate.

Guarded · authorities

Corridor States

Security comes first: low-altitude flying is tightly permitted and often paused, and growth goes to rail, metro, tunnels and pods that the state controls.

By 2040: Fixed corridors carry the decade’s growth; driverless vehicles run in closed corridors, depots and freight routes.

Guarded · platforms

Gated Premium

Platforms serve affluent residents and visitors with premium driverless and air services under rules that differ emirate by emirate; public transport grows slowly.

By 2040: The next mode is a luxury rather than a network, and it pauses with every security alert.

Wild card to 2040

Robots move the goods first

Driverless trucks on Saudi corridors and robots and drones for summer deliveries reach scale before passenger autonomy does.

Built from two of the briefing’s Potential Surprises to 2040: driverless freight becomes the first autonomy at scale (2029 to 2034, Medium) and heat makes robots and drones the default for summer deliveries (2030 to 2036, Low-Medium).

What the stress test shows
  1. There is a robust core, and it matches the briefing’s first three Implications. Funding two candidates against trip targets, relying on several suppliers with shared safety data, and flying fixed routes with a ground fallback hold up in all four futures.
  2. The fastest routes are the fragile ones. One partner per service and launching on current rules both backfire in Corridor States, one of the two guarded futures this quarter’s evidence leans towards: one stops the service when a supplier fails, the other when airspace closes.
  3. The public-ownership moves are bets on one axis. Building a public driverless network and owning the fleet are strong where authorities lead and weak if platforms win. Hold them ready, and decide when the horizontal axis moves: the signpost is a platform winning a multi-emirate licence, or an authority tendering a feeder network.

3. What it means for the plan

Commit now

Robust: works in every future.

  • Fund two candidates for the next flying taxi against daily-trips targets (Implication 1).
  • Cap any one software supplier’s share of driverless trips and require shared incident data (Implication 2).
  • Launch air taxis on fixed routes with a ground alternative for each (Implication 3).

Prepare, and act on a trigger

Bets: strong in some futures, weak in others.

  • A public driverless feeder network: release it if an authority-led future gains momentum.
  • Owning the fleet and buying the driving: hold ready until the platform question is settled.
  • A Gulf-wide low-altitude rulebook: pursue it if the unified visa and the GCC railway arrive on time.
  • Letting pilots compete: acceptable only while platforms carry the cost.

Do not rely on

Fragile: backfires in at least one future.

  • A single software partner for any driverless service.
  • Launching low-altitude services on rules written before the 2026 war.

7. Running it with your own plan

The options above are the briefing’s. The real value comes from replacing them with the organisation’s own investment priorities and asking the same question of each: does it hold in every future?
