Twenty Years of Promises: Where GCC Transport Really Stands

The record a new programme starts from: what the Gulf states promised for transport and mobility since 2006, what actually happened, the shocks that changed course, and the questions that will shape the next decade.

Built from 150 verified sources, 2006 to 2026 (sources before 2011 only where they record when a promise was first made): 46 from governments, regulators, statistics offices and research bodies (Tier 1 and 2), 27 in Arabic. Every source was opened and its date read off the page. Download as PDF

The short version. Of 48 big transport promises whose deadline has passed, 8 arrived on time. 19 arrived late, by a median of 2.6 years. 13 are still not delivered, and 8 were scaled back or abandoned.

The pattern is steady. Airports, ports and road links usually arrive: 12 of 16 past their date have been delivered, typically about 2 years late, though Abu Dhabi's Midfield Terminal took 13. Rail and metro has the weakest record: of 23 promises past their date, 13 have been delivered and only 3 on time. The rest, mostly cross-border links and metros outside Dubai, Doha and Riyadh, are still waiting or were dropped. The few on-time cases mostly had a date that could not move, such as a Hajj season, a royal order or Dubai's 9 September 2009 opening. Targets for new technology, from driverless trips to air taxis and electric cars, have so far been met only in pilot form. Two shocks did the most damage to timetables: the oil price collapse of 2014 to 2016 and the pandemic of 2020.

The programme uses this record to judge new announcements. When a fresh deadline is set, the first question is how similar promises have fared.

2. The shocks that changed course

Events that stopped, delayed or redirected transport plans. They are the first place to look when thinking about wild cards: each one was a surprise at the time.

  • April 2013Civil Defence directive cancels Hamad International Airport opening. The opening of Doha's new airport was cancelled hours before it was due and slipped by more than a year. The Peninsula, 2013; Gulf Times, 2014
  • September 2014Oil price collapse of 2014-16 and the Gulf budget cuts that followed. Rail and metro projects across the GCC were slowed, phased or suspended, including the GCC Railway, Oman Rail, Etihad Rail stage 2 and the Jeddah Metro. Al Jarida (Kuwait), 2016; Oxford Business Group, 2019; Oxford Business Group, 2016
  • August 2015UAE retail fuel price deregulation. Linked UAE pump prices to global markets with monthly resets, framed as a way to curb fuel consumption. UAE Cabinet, 2015
  • June 2017Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain and Egypt cut ties and transport links with Qatar. Qatar Airways lost access to 18 cities and rerouted over Iran, Turkey and Oman; Qatar rerouted trade the same way and leaned on the new Hamad Port and direct shipping lines, opening the port formally three months into the crisis. The already stalled Qatar-Bahrain causeway was frozen. Links reopened in January 2021 after the AlUla Declaration. Al Jazeera Centre for Studies, 2017; Oxford Business Group, 2019; Aviation International News (AIN), 2017; Al Jazeera, 2017
  • July 2017Etihad's equity alliance strategy collapses into large write-downs. Etihad posted a $1.87bn 2016 loss, mostly aircraft impairments and exposure to Alitalia and AirBerlin, and began restructuring. MEED, 2017
  • January 2018Saudi January 2018 gasoline price rise. Raised Saudi gasoline to 1.37 and 2.04 riyals per litre, a large step up in the cost of private car use. Saudi Press Agency (SPA), 2017
  • 2019DXB southern runway closure, Jet Airways bankruptcy and Boeing 737 Max grounding. DXB passengers fell 3.1% to 86.4 million and cargo fell 4.8% in 2019. Dubai Airports, 2020
  • March 2019Uber agrees to buy Careem for $3.1 billion. Consolidated GCC ride-hailing under one global platform, which later became the channel for robotaxi launches in Abu Dhabi and Dubai. Wamda, 2019; Uber Technologies, 2024
  • September 2019Fire at the Haramain Jeddah station. Gutted a flagship station one year into commercial operations and injured at least 10 people. MEED, 2019
  • March 2020COVID-19 pandemic and the 2020 oil price crash. Passenger transport was suspended or collapsed: air travel and transit ridership fell sharply, and governments cut capital budgets. Saudi Press Agency (SPA), 2020; Emarat Al Youm, 2021; World Bank Blogs (PPPs), 2023; World Bank, 2020
  • July 20212021 global fuel price spike and GCC price caps. Saudi Arabia and Oman froze pump prices, pausing market-linked pricing and weakening the price signal for demand restraint. Saudi Press Agency (SPA), 2021
  • January 2023Kuwait scraps its $20bn metro project. Removed Kuwait's main urban rail plan on financial grounds, leaving national rail at consultancy stage. Oxford Business Group, 2024
  • October 2023Gaza war after 7 October 2023 and the 2026 war with Iran. IMEC was left effectively on hold with no firm funding or construction timelines, and the Strait of Hormuz route was disrupted in 2026. Middle East Institute, 2026
  • December 2023Houthi attacks on Red Sea shipping. Suez Canal trade fell 42% in two months and container rates more than doubled; Saudi Red Sea ports lost traffic (King Abdullah Port down over 80% in 2024) while Jebel Ali reached its highest volume since 2015. UNCTAD, 2024
  • December 2023Hyperloop One shuts down. Ended the company behind the Dubai to Abu Dhabi hyperloop promise, with DP World keeping the intellectual property. AGBI (Arabian Gulf Business Insight), 2023
  • April 2024Saudi Arabia scales back The Line's 2030 scope. Cut the showcase car-free, high-speed linear transit concept to 2.4 km by 2030, shrinking the largest planned GCC test bed for new mobility. AGBI (Arabian Gulf Business Insight), 2024
  • December 2024GM ends Cruise robotaxi development. Removed the partner behind Dubai's plan for 4,000 Cruise robotaxis by 2030; Dubai moved to Chinese operators Baidu Apollo Go and WeRide. Al Etihad, 2021; Khaleej Times (Reuters), 2024

4. Open questions to 2035

The uncertainties that matter most, drawn from the record. They are the starting list for the programme's scenarios and System Map.

  • Oil and capital spending. Will a third oil price shock repeat the 2016 and 2020 cuts to transport capital spending?
  • GCC Railway. Does the December 2030 date hold when the Kuwait, Bahrain and Oman links are still at an early stage?
  • Saudi Landbridge. How will it be financed and delivered now that the partnership model has stalled?
  • Hub airports. Can Dubai World Central, King Salman International, Riyadh Air, Doha and Abu Dhabi all grow at once without overcapacity?
  • Airspace and sea lanes. Will airspace closures or Red Sea disruption recur, and do the routing shifts between Red Sea and Gulf ports last?
  • Trade corridors. Does IMEC move beyond a non-binding memorandum, and do Duqm and the Red Sea ports turn pledges into throughput?
  • Robotaxis. Can fleets scale from about 100 vehicles to thousands, and how far will the region rely on Chinese operators as US and China technology rivalry sharpens?
  • Air taxis. When do certified, paying passenger services start in Dubai and Abu Dhabi, and is there demand beyond showcase routes?
  • Electric vehicles. How fast does uptake rise from about 1% of Saudi car sales, and do Ceer, Lucid and the charging networks keep pace with the targets?
  • Public transport and the car. Do Riyadh Metro and Etihad Rail ridership shift trips away from the car, towards the 15% Saudi and 20% Riyadh public transport targets?
  • Giga-projects. How far are NEOM and The Line cut back under fiscal pressure, and what happens to the mobility systems promised for them?
  • Fuel prices. Do the pump price caps of 2021 stay, or does market-linked pricing resume and change how people travel?
  • Population. How far does expatriate population growth continue, given census results below earlier estimates?
  • Kuwait and Bahrain. Do long-standing rail and metro plans, the Bahrain Metro and the King Hamad Causeway to Saudi Arabia, turn into signed contracts?

5. How the programme uses this

  • Signal Scans check each new development against this record, so a repeat of an old pattern is called a repeat, and something genuinely new can be called the first of its kind since a given year.
  • Change Trackers use the base rates to judge how likely a target is to be met, rather than taking the announced date at face value.
  • Decision Intelligence briefings draw their scenario axes and wild cards from the open questions and the shocks above, and grade new promises against how similar ones fared.
  • The System Map starts from the cause and effect this history shows, for example oil prices driving capital spending, which drives project timetables.
  • The track record adds each new promise to this ledger, so the record keeps growing with the programme.
