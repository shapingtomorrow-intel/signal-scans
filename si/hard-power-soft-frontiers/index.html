Defence has stopped being a sector. Six domains, one operating environment.

Executive Synthesis

Defence has stopped being a sector. The boundary between hard military capability, civil resilience, critical-infrastructure protection, supply-chain security and information integrity has dissolved into a single, multi-domain operating environment, with one cross-cutting commercial consequence. The 1.5% "security-related" band of NATO's Hague 5% commitment (June 2025) explicitly funds critical-infrastructure protection, network defence, civil preparedness, innovation and the defence industrial base. Which side of the operating model each of those sits on is now a commercial-allocation question, not a definitional one.

Resilience used to be a soft frontier. The 1.5% security-related band of the NATO 5% commitment just made it the procurement category.

This cycle's six clusters trace the shift end to end: the headline European spending step-change and the resilience-procurement category it creates; the upstream repricing of critical minerals; the subsea and cyber domains where infrastructure is now under live attack; the autonomous and counter-drone operational layer; the neoprime reshape of the defence-industrial base; and the information-operations contest. The materially new development since the format's last defence-and-resilience read is that two regulatory clocks are now visibly running against each other: China's critical-minerals export-control suspension expires in November 2026 just as the EU's company-level reporting obligations begin, and the same November-2026-to-2029 window decides whether the NATO commitment hardens into a binding procurement environment or softens into an accounting exercise.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The thesis depends on the NATO 5% commitment being a binding ten-year target rather than a political headline that softens under fiscal pressure. If two or more European Allies miss interim milestones at the 2029 review without consequence, the commitment becomes a directional aspiration, the 1.5% security-related band becomes a creative-accounting bucket, and the demand-pull on civilian operators, dual-use industry and resilience procurement loosens materially. Watch the 2029 review and the first SAFE disbursement reports, expected from March 2026, for the leading indicators.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Map your exposure to the NATO 1.5% security-related band. It is where civilian operators, dual-use industry and resilience procurement become first-class line items, and an unmapped exposure is an unmanaged one.
  2. Commission the critical-minerals dependency audit before the November 2026 window closes. The operators who walk into the 2027 procurement conversations with a documented dependency map will price differently from those without one.
  3. Treat critical-infrastructure protection as procurement leverage, not a compliance cost. Subsea, cyber and counter-drone threats are converging into a single buyer expectation, and being early is pricing power.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Signal Clusters

The cycle's signals are organised into six clusters, ranked by impact on readers' near-term decisions. Immediate: changes H1 or H2 2026 budgets, contracts or procurement decisions. Near-Term: changes competitive position over the next twelve months. Longer-Range: a multi-year structural shift to track and revisit each cycle.

1. The spending step-change and the resilience procurement category

Immediate

The NATO Hague Summit locked a 5% of GDP defence and security commitment by 2035, split into 3.5% core defence plus a 1.5% security-related band, with a 2029 review point, and the EU's SAFE loan facility converts the political signal into a multi-decade procurement environment. The structural redefinition lives inside the 1.5% band, because that is where critical-infrastructure protection, civil preparedness, innovation and the defence industrial base become first-class procurement line items. Civil resilience is the clearest case: the EU and UK both moved in 2025 to formalise citizen-facing preparedness as a permanent capability category, which means resilience products, services and disclosure are now demand-pulled by the same envelope that funds hard capability.

The 5% commitment and what the 1.5% band funds

NATO Hague Summit Declaration: 5% of GDP defence and security commitment by 2035, split 3.5% core defence plus 1.5% security-related, with a 2029 review point.

  • NATO Allies committed to 5% of GDP defence and security spending by 2035, split 3.5% core defence plus 1.5% security-related, with a 2029 review point. NATO Hague Summit Declaration (June 2025).
  • The EU adopted the SAFE regulation, providing 150 billion euros in long-maturity loans for joint defence procurement, and approved a first wave of plans for eight member states with first payments expected in March 2026. EU Council (May 2025); European Commission (January 2026).
  • The EU Preparedness Union Strategy set out 30 actions including a 72-hour household supplies baseline, converting civil preparedness from policy aspiration into a baseline procurement expectation. European Commission (March 2025).
  • The UK Resilience Action Plan committed a Resilience Academy training 4,000 people a year, 4.2 billion pounds in flood defence and an annual National Exercising Programme, the same capability category in UK form. UK Cabinet Office (July 2025).

Counter-argument

Two or more Allies could miss interim milestones at the 2029 review without consequence, and the 1.5% band could be filled with reclassified line items rather than new spending. SIPRI commentary already flags the fiscal-sustainability questions. Resilience expectations may also overshoot citizen tolerance: the 72-hour baseline could be politicised as government-led prepper-ism, and member-state implementation may stall on funding-architecture and division-of-responsibility questions. If that happens, the structural-procurement thesis softens to a directional aspiration.

2. Critical minerals and supply-chain securitisation

Immediate

Two regulatory windows close inside a six-month band: China's twelve-month suspension of export controls on gallium, germanium and antimony expires in November 2026, and the EU Critical Raw Materials Act's company-level reporting and stockholding obligations enter their first compliance year in 2026. The deeper structural shift is China's licensing architecture itself, where an upstream market position has been converted into a formal end-user and end-use screening mechanism that survives any temporary suspension. Operators with documented dependency maps will price differently in the 2027 procurement conversations from operators without them.

  • The EU Critical Raw Materials Act is in force with 2030 targets (extraction at least 10%, processing at least 40%, recycling at least 25%, no single third-country dependency above 65%), and member-state reporting begins in 2026. European Commission (in force May 2024).
  • The EU Council adopted an amended CRMA position transferring identification of large critical-raw-material users to the Commission and adding transparency and stockholding obligations on large users. Council of the European Union (March 2026).
  • China's MOFCOM temporarily suspended export controls on gallium, germanium, antimony, tungsten and graphite to US end-users for twelve months, but left the military end-user prohibition in place and the licensing framework intact. Pillsbury Law (November 2025).
  • China's licensing-based export-control architecture is the structural shift; the headline suspension is tactical, and when it expires MOFCOM retains discretion on every shipment. Andersen Institute (2026).

Counter-argument

Substitution and recycling could change the dependency curve faster than the regulatory windows close. Rare-earth-free magnet technology, gallium recovery at scale and synthetic-substitute development are all progressing, and a genuine breakthrough at industrial scale on the top five inputs would reduce the urgency of the dependency audit. Alternative European processing capacity is also coming online, though none has yet reached commercial scale.

3. Critical infrastructure under live attack

Immediate

The threat to critical infrastructure is no longer prospective; it is documented and persistent, across both the subsea and the cyber domains. Baltic Sea cable-cutting has converted undersea-infrastructure protection from an industry compliance topic into a state-level deterrence question, and the China-linked "Typhoon" cyber operations have moved from individual incidents to a sustained pattern of pre-positioning inside operational-technology networks. For critical-infrastructure boards the threat model shifts from intrusion detection to compromise assumption: assume the adversary is already inside, and design for graceful degradation under disruption.

  • Estlink 2 and four telecoms cables were damaged in December 2024, with the suspected vessel part of Russia's shadow fleet, and the Baltic pattern of shadow-fleet anchor-dragging has exposed legal and deterrence gaps. EU EEAS (December 2024); Carnegie Endowment (June 2025).
  • Salt Typhoon penetrated Verizon, AT&T and Charter, gaining visibility into call records, text metadata and geolocation for an estimated one million US individuals, and infiltrated more than 200 targets across 80-plus countries. US Congressional Research Service (2025).
  • Volt Typhoon is pre-positioned for disruption inside US critical-infrastructure operational-technology networks, distinct from Salt Typhoon's espionage but with the same state sponsor, and the 2026 ODNI Annual Threat Assessment names these as a primary national-security concern. CISA (2025-26); ODNI (2026).
  • EU and NATO moved together at unusual pace, with the Baltic Sentry mission and the EU Action Plan on Cable Security setting out prevention, detection, response and deterrence focus areas. European Commission (February 2025).

Counter-argument

Both halves of this cluster may be over-claimed. Some Baltic cable damage may be accidental: the sea is a busy commercial waterway and anchor-drag incidents pre-date the hybrid-threat era. And the Typhoon attributions serve a deterrence-signalling purpose for the US intelligence community; Salt Typhoon may be primarily intelligence collection rather than sabotage preparation, and Volt Typhoon's actual disruption capability is undemonstrated. If post-incident forensics attribute a meaningful share of cable cuts to accidents, and the Typhoon disruption threat proves more latent than active, the procurement urgency softens.

4. Autonomous systems and the counter-drone problem

Near-Term

The US Replicator initiative has been absorbed into the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group, with an FY27 funding request rising from $225.9 million to as much as $54.6 billion. The dollar figure is striking, but the doctrine being procured is the more consequential signal: a shift from attritable single platforms to coordinated multi-asset swarms. The dual-use read-across is direct, because civilian critical-infrastructure operators (data centres, ports, power substations) face the same physical-layer threat surface as deployed forces, and defending an asset against twelve coordinated drones is a different problem from defending it against one.

The DAWG funding jump: FY26 to FY27 request

US Defense Autonomous Warfare Group funding: FY26 enacted versus FY27 request. Source: DefenseScoop, April 2026.

  • Replicator launched in August 2023 targeting thousands of attritable autonomous systems by August 2025, but only hundreds materialised by the target date. US Congressional Research Service (2025).
  • Replicator has been absorbed into the Defense Autonomous Warfare Group, with a broader scope including larger uncrewed-aircraft classes. Breaking Defense (December 2025).
  • The DAWG FY27 funding request rises to as much as $54.6 billion, from $225.9 million in FY26, the Pentagon's largest-ever investment in drones and counter-drone systems. DefenseScoop (April 2026).
  • The operational shift is toward coordinated multi-asset autonomy, the swarm rather than the single platform as the unit of capability. Defense One (January 2026).

Counter-argument

The FY27 $54.6 billion request is a Defense Department aspiration, not a Congressional appropriation, and real spend may land at a fraction of the request. The Replicator delivery shortfall, hundreds rather than thousands of systems by the August 2025 target, points to genuine execution risk on autonomous-systems procurement at scale, which would slow the civilian counter-drone read-across with it.

5. The neoprime reshape of the defence-industrial base

Near-Term

The Pentagon has begun consolidating procurement through a small set of "neoprime" software and autonomy vendors, and the consolidation absorbs the supplier base, not just the headline contracts. Anduril's $20 billion US Army enterprise contract absorbs 120 to 130 prior orders; Palantir's $10 billion ten-year enterprise service agreement absorbs around 75. For mid-tier suppliers and traditional primes the implication is close to binary over the next twelve to eighteen months: enter the neoprime supply chain, most likely via acquisition, or risk being routed around. The valuation reset is happening live.

  • The US Army awarded Anduril a five-to-ten-year enterprise contract worth up to $20 billion, consolidating 120 to 130 prior orders into a single enterprise relationship. TechCrunch (March 2026).
  • The US Army awarded Palantir a ten-year enterprise service agreement worth up to $10 billion, consolidating approximately 75 existing software and data contracts. Motley Fool (March 2026).
  • The shift from pilot contracts to consolidated multi-billion enterprise deals concentrates buyer power in a few neoprime winners and reshapes the supplier base beneath them. Fortune (March 2026).
  • Anduril's revenue is projected at $4.3 billion in 2026, up from $2.2 billion in 2025, with a March 2026 funding round implying a $60 billion valuation. Fortune (May 2026).

Counter-argument

Neoprime contracts may not deliver at the advertised scale. Legacy primes (Lockheed, Northrop, RTX, General Dynamics) retain platform-integration and certification advantages that software-first vendors have not yet proven across major programmes. If the neoprimes underdeliver, the consolidation reverses and the established prime ecosystem regains procurement weight, and the binary positioning choice facing mid-tier suppliers loosens into a more familiar competitive landscape.

6. Information operations as a sustained pattern

Longer-Range

The 2024-25 European election cycle established a documented pattern of AI-augmented Russian and Chinese influence operations against democratic processes, and the structural shift is the operational tempo: campaigns now run continuously rather than spiking around elections. EEAS findings put 27% of 2025 disinformation incidents as using AI-generated text, synthetic audio or manipulated video, which lets state actors scale content production with fewer resources. The defender response, principally the EU Digital Services Act, is still mostly framework-stage, and the enforcement and platform-transparency gaps are the immediate constraint.

  • 27% of analysed disinformation incidents in 2025 involved AI-generated text, synthetic audio or manipulated video, and Russian and Chinese actors are scaling content production with fewer resources. EU DisinfoLab, relaying EEAS findings (2025-26).
  • The Russia-linked Storm-1516 operation created over 100 fake websites pushing deepfakes during German parliamentary elections, and the Doppelganger campaign targeted Polish presidential elections with 279 posts in March and April 2025. European Digital Media Observatory (2025).
  • A Chinese "Spamouflage" operation targeted Spain by exploiting a Valencia floods narrative, signalling state-level information operations shifting toward the European theatre. EU DisinfoLab (September 2025).
  • AI-driven information manipulation is now a standing concern for EU institutions, with the Digital Services Act the primary instrument and its enforcement gaps the binding constraint. European Parliament Research Service (2025).

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal A European information-protection apparatus formalising. Would gain weight if any major EU member state stands up a dedicated information-protection agency in 2026.
  • Weak signal AI-detection tools achieving consistent attribution accuracy. Would gain weight if a third-party benchmark confirms above 90% detection accuracy on current-generation AI-generated content.

Counter-argument

The 27% AI-augmented figure may overstate operational impact. Traditional human-driven influence and bot-network amplification may still account for the larger share of actual reach and persuasion, and AI-generated content may be detected and removed faster than non-AI content. The EU Digital Services Act enforcement gap may also close faster than the threat-actor adaptation curve, in which case the contest stabilises rather than escalates.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's signals into the plan. Each follows the four-question discipline (who, by when, what, why), names a concrete action and carries a decision posture. Opportunities are expressed here, as Prepare-posture moves, rather than in a separate section.

SI 1: Map exposure to the NATO 1.5% security-related band

By end-Q3 2026, the executive committee or National Security Council sub-committee should identify the three line items in the three-year plan most affected by the 1.5% security-related band (critical-infrastructure protection, network defence, civil preparedness, innovation, defence industrial base), because that band is where civilian operators and dual-use industry become first-class procurement participants, and an exposure that is not mapped is one that drifts. A senior owner should be assigned and the governing body briefed by year-end.

Action: by year-end 2026, three named line items, a senior owner, and a 2029-review trigger schedule, briefed to the board.

Decide Draws on Cluster 1.

SI 2: Commission a critical-minerals dependency audit

By end-Q2 2026, the executive committee should commission a critical-minerals dependency audit covering the top twenty inputs to operations, because two regulatory windows close inside a six-month band (China's suspension expires in November 2026 just as the EU CRMA's company-level reporting begins) and operators that walk into the 2027 procurement conversations with a documented dependency map and pre-positioned alternatives will see preferential pricing and terms. The audit should be commissioned at the next board meeting.

Action: commission the audit at the next board meeting; deliver findings by end-Q3 2026 with a pre-positioning plan for the top five inputs.

Decide Draws on Cluster 2.

SI 3: Publish a Critical Infrastructure Protection Plan

Critical-infrastructure operators (undersea cable, power, terrestrial telecoms, water, transport, data centres) should publish an internal Critical Infrastructure Protection Plan by end-Q3 2026, because the NATO 5% commitment, the EU Action Plan on Cable Security, the CISA Typhoon advisories and the counter-drone threat surface are converging into a single procurement standard under which operators that can demonstrate detection, response, redundancy and recovery see preferential terms. The plan should cover the physical, cyber and aerial threat surfaces together, with crisis-communications readiness alongside.

Action: decide the scope at the next Audit Committee; ship version one by end-Q3 2026, covering subsea, cyber and counter-drone exposure.

Decide Draws on Clusters 3 and 4.

SI 4: Build a defence-and-resilience allocation and positioning thesis

By the next investment committee, asset managers and corporate-strategy teams should establish a defence-and-resilience allocation and positioning thesis tied to the NATO 5% timeline, the SAFE disbursement schedule and US FY27 appropriations, because the cycle's upside is structural ten-year compounding rather than a single-year move, and the same thesis frames the neoprime-versus-prime positioning question for industry players. The two or three signals that would unwind it should be pre-committed: missed European interim milestones at the 2029 review, a slipping SAFE disbursement rate, a US procurement-policy shift, and an escalation in information-operations tempo are the watch-list.

Action: set the allocation thesis and the unwind thresholds at the next investment committee, so the position does not drift without a decision.

Prepare Draws on Clusters 1, 5 and 6.

Scenario Matrix

Two uncertainties will shape the next three years more than any other. First, the NATO 5% commitment trajectory: does it harden through the 2029 review or soften under fiscal pressure? Second, hybrid-threat intensity: do critical-infrastructure attacks, supply-chain coercion and information operations escalate or stabilise? The four scenarios below are planning aids, not forecasts.

Resilience Decade

Commitment hardens, threats stabilise. NATO 5% becomes a binding ten-year compounding cycle and the 1.5% security-related band creates a sustained civilian-procurement category. Hybrid threats persist but stabilise at current intensity. Critical-infrastructure protection becomes a standard procurement clause, civil resilience becomes a permanent capability category, and investors price defence and resilience as structural rather than cyclical.

Early indicators: SAFE disbursements on schedule through 2027; the 2029 review confirms interim milestones met by a majority of Allies; the insurance market formalises critical-infrastructure-protection premiums.

Escalation Spiral

Commitment hardens, threats escalate. Defence and resilience spending compound while subsea attacks intensify, critical-infrastructure cyber pre-positioning becomes active disruption, and information operations move from elections to ongoing societal coercion. Civilian operators face mandatory disclosure and protection regimes, the dual-use sector accelerates further, and the boundary between military and civilian operating models effectively dissolves.

Early indicators: first public disclosure of operational-technology sabotage attributed to a named state actor; a subsea cable incident causing material commercial loss; an AI-augmented information operation directly affecting a national election outcome.

Fiscal Softening

Commitment softens, threats stabilise. Two or more European Allies miss interim milestones at the 2029 review with limited consequence, the 1.5% band becomes a creative-accounting bucket, and SAFE disbursements slip. The civilian-procurement category contracts back to compliance-driven minima, and the defence-and-resilience investment thesis becomes selective rather than structural.

Early indicators: SAFE first-payment delays beyond Q2 2026; a major-Ally domestic political shift making 5% deliverability publicly questioned; a NATO communique softening on interim milestones.

Asymmetric Stress

Commitment softens, threats escalate. The most demanding scenario: the spending commitment loosens while hybrid threats intensify. Critical-infrastructure operators face an escalating threat surface without the procurement support of a hardening NATO envelope, civilian operators carry the cost of resilience without the capital-markets premium of a structural defence cycle, and information-operation incidents accumulate without enforcement teeth catching up.

Early indicators: a combination of indicators from scenarios 2 and 3; the insurance market repricing risk faster than regulatory frameworks can compensate; a first major civilian operator publicly stating it cannot absorb resilience cost without policy support.

What We Are Not Planning For

A major-power direct kinetic confrontation

The base case is escalating hybrid and proxy conflict, not direct kinetic engagement between NATO and Russia or the US and China. A direct confrontation would re-anchor every cluster in this report and shift the timeframe of every Strategic Implication from years to weeks. The probability is hard to bound, but the consequence is high enough to track rather than plan around.

Reinstate if: a major-power exchange of fire occurs at sea, in the air, or in a third-country theatre with attribution to a named state actor.

A successful catastrophic critical-infrastructure cyber attack

The base case is sustained pre-positioning and intelligence collection with episodic high-profile incidents, not a coordinated disruption. A successful attack affecting a major Western power grid, water system or financial-settlement infrastructure would compress every regulatory and procurement timeline by twelve to twenty-four months.

Reinstate if: there is public attribution of a state-led successful disruption of critical-infrastructure operations.

A coordinated US withdrawal from NATO commitments

The base case is that US participation in the NATO 5% commitment is sustained, with European Allies taking on the bulk of the marginal increase. A formal or de facto US withdrawal would shift the entire structural argument of this report, not just one cluster of it.

Reinstate if: the US Congress passes legislation materially altering NATO commitments, or the Administration formally announces conditional or reduced participation.

Discussion Points for the Leadership Team

  1. Of the NATO 1.5% security-related band, which line items in our operating plan are direct or adjacent recipients? If the answer is "we do not know yet", the band is drifting into a creative-accounting bucket, and the discipline is to name the three line items by Q3.
  2. Do we have a documented dependency map for our top twenty critical-minerals inputs? If not, the November 2026 China suspension expiry and the 2026 EU CRMA enforcement window are both passing without us positioning.
  3. Have we published a Critical Infrastructure Protection Plan for internal board purposes? If we operate critical infrastructure, that plan is becoming a procurement document, not a compliance one, and it now has to cover the subsea, cyber and counter-drone threat surfaces together.
  4. What is our positioning toward the neoprimes? If we are a mid-tier supplier or a traditional prime, the partner-with, compete-against or be-acquired-by question has an eighteen-month answering window.
  5. Which line items in our plan get materially better under Resilience Decade, worse under Asymmetric Stress, and what is our one-line answer to "what changes if NATO Allies miss the 2029 interim milestones"? If we do not have that answer, the plan has not been stress-tested.
