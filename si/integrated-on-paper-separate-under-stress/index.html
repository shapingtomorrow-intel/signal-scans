Why This Matters Now

Australia and New Zealand have spent 2026 drawing closer on paper. Ministers launched a programme to carry out the Forum's own proposals, joint standards are rising and New Zealand has adopted Australian settings on fuel without being asked. By the measures the relationship usually uses, integration is working.

The year's shocks tested something else. When fuel, rare earths and tariffs came under pressure, each country reached for its own reserve, its own deal with a third partner and its own crisis plan. New Zealand's first binding promise of supply in a crisis was signed with Singapore, not Australia. The pattern repeats in cyber protection, minerals and defence trade.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: the Single Economic Market measures what the two countries share in normal times, and nothing yet commits either one to the other when times are not normal.

Executive Synthesis

If fuel, medicines or rare earths ran short across the Tasman next year, which document obliges Australia to keep supplying New Zealand, or New Zealand Australia?

None does. New Zealand's first binding promise of that kind is with Singapore, in force since 17 July, and the leaders' June statement commits the two governments to coordinate their respective supply measures, not to share them.

Start with what went right. On 4 September the CER trade ministers announced they were “launching a new programme of Trans-Tasman business-government collaboration to implement the Australia New Zealand Leadership Forum's proposals to reduce the cost of doing business between New Zealand and Australia” (the CER Trade Ministers, Sep 2026), alongside a 30% increase in joint trans-Tasman standards and coordinated engagement across CPTPP, RCEP, PACER Plus and AANZFTA. In June the two prime ministers reaffirmed the Single Economic Market objective of no significant barriers arising from differences in policy, standards and regulation (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026). Under pressure from the Hormuz disruption New Zealand went further on its own: MBIE records that it has “aligned fuel specifications with Australia's standards to improve supply flexibility, and we are assessing whether further specification changes could be helpful” (MBIE, undated), and it is evaluating Australian WaterMark plumbing certification for recognition (MBIE Building Performance, undated). Measured by rules, integration is ahead of schedule.

Measured by what each country can count on in a crisis, it is not. The same leaders' statement is careful about ownership: “Prime Ministers committed to deepen trans-Tasman collaboration on our respective supply measures, demand management and crisis planning.” (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026). There is no joint reserve, no sharing mechanism and no commitment not to restrict exports to each other. New Zealand found that commitment elsewhere. Singapore and New Zealand “commit not to impose unnecessary export restrictions on agreed essential supplies, including food, fuel, healthcare, chemical and construction products” (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated), under an agreement in force since 17 July in which Singapore covers fuel, medicines and chemicals and New Zealand covers food (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026). Australia, meanwhile, is building a 1 billion litre fuel reserve inside a A$14.8 billion package (Budget 2026-27, May 2026) and New Zealand a 90 million litre diesel reserve at Marsden Point (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026). Two buffers, sized and owned nationally, with no claim of either on the other.

The pattern holds well beyond fuel. On critical minerals Australia signed an agreement with the United States under which each side put at least US$1 billion into projects within six months (the Department of Industry, Mar 2026), and its new Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve names Canada, Europe, Japan, Korea, the UK and the US as partners, not New Zealand (the Department of Industry, undated). New Zealand's Cabinet agreed to pursue its own bilateral deal with Washington (MFAT's Cabinet papers, May 2026). China's Ministry of Commerce has said, in Chinese, “中方2025年10月9日公布的相关出口管制等措施，暂停实施至2026年11月10日”, which reads in English as “the relevant export control and other measures China announced on 9 October 2025 are suspended until 10 November 2026” (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026), which puts a date on the lever both countries are separately hedging against. On cyber, New Zealand's own officials record that the country stands out among advanced economies for having no dedicated law protecting critical infrastructure from cyber harm, while Australia regulates under the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act (DPMC, Feb 2026). In defence industry, New Zealand firms count as local content in Australian procurement (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025), yet sit outside the licence-free trade environment Australia built for its AUKUS partners (the Department of Defence, undated).

The Single Economic Market measures what the two countries share in normal times. Nothing yet commits either one to the other when times are not normal.

None of this is a failure of goodwill. Cooperation is visible wherever it runs through defence channels or through coordinated national action. Both countries joined the International Energy Agency's largest ever collective fuel release in March (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026), the two defence forces now train on a common cyber platform (the Department of Defence, Mar 2026), and the Anzac 2035 statement commits them to an increasingly integrated combat force (the Anzac 2035 statement, Mar 2026). The gap is narrower and more specific. The instruments that decide what happens under stress, whether reserves, export promises, mineral offtake, critical infrastructure law or export control, are being written now, in 2026, and they are being written nationally or with third countries.

That matters for the Forum because the stress is not hypothetical and it is not easing. The IEA's August report is blunt: “Diesel exports from Russia, the Middle East and Asia were 1.3 mb/d lower y-o-y, equivalent to about 20% of global seaborne trade.” (the IEA, Aug 2026). New Zealand held 25.5 days of diesel in the country on 13 September, 48.3 counting cargoes at sea (MBIE stock updates, Sep 2026). Inflation rose to 4.1% in the June quarter because of fuel, against 2.9% excluding vehicle fuels, and the Reserve Bank raised the cash rate to 2.75% (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, Sep 2026). The US imposed an extra 12.5% tariff on most New Zealand exports from 24 July, with Australia in the same band (MFAT, Jul 2026). China's rare earth suspension lapses on 10 November (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026). New Zealand votes in November, and the next government inherits the frigate choice that decides whether the two navies share sustainment (ASPI, Sep 2026).

The implementation programme launched on 4 September is the Forum's own instrument, and its scope already names supply chains, climate adaptation, defence industry and space (the CER Trade Ministers, Sep 2026). The question for this cycle is whether it is used to reduce the cost of doing business in normal times, which it will do well, or also to write the first trans-Tasman commitments that hold when times are not normal. The four Strategic Implications below set out where that is achievable before 2030 and where it is not.

Where this analysis could be wrong

Buying assurance from the right supplier is not a failure of integration. A refining and trading hub can promise fuel in a way a fellow importer cannot, and importers across Asia made the same turn from just-in-time to precautionary supply this year (Fortune, Aug 2026). On fuel, the Singapore choice may simply be correct. The claim here is narrower: the Singapore text also covers food, healthcare, chemical and construction products (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated), and no trans-Tasman instrument covers any of them.

Coordination may be enough, and it has worked. Joint participation in the IEA release, an insurance working group and aligned vehicle-to-grid standards (the 2+2 Dialogue, Dec 2025), and a joint insurance industry initiative (the Insurance Council of Australia, Jun 2026) are real. The distinction drawn in this briefing is between coordination, which depends on both governments choosing to act together at the time, and commitment, which does not. It is possible that the relationship is close enough that the distinction never bites. It has not yet been tested by a shortage in which both countries want the same cargo.

The rules story is not uniformly convergent either. New Zealand has raised its climate reporting threshold for listed issuers to $1 billion and eased director liability (Buddle Findlay, Feb 2026), a move away from Australia's mandatory regime rather than towards it. And on cyber the direction is towards alignment: New Zealand's discussion document proposes Australian-style mandatory reporting (DPMC, Feb 2026). The thesis should be read as describing where binding crisis commitments sit, not as a claim that every rule is converging.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Put a crisis supply clause on the implementation programme's agenda. The programme is being scoped now, and a commitment not to restrict essential exports to each other is a short, legible addition to an agenda that already names supply chains.
  2. Make critical minerals a trans-Tasman offer before the reserve starts buying. Australia's reserve becomes operational in the second half of 2026 without New Zealand as a named partner, and China's suspension of its rare earth controls lapses on 10 November.
  3. Engage in New Zealand's cyber law while it is still a discussion document. A regime drafted to be recognised in Australia from the start costs less than one aligned afterwards.
  4. Name the defence trade gap in the Forum's defence industry proposals. Local content status in procurement does not reach the AUKUS licence-free environment, and security clearance recognition is still unfinished.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Audience Snapshots

Four lenses on the same evidence base. Each card surfaces the one question this cycle puts to that reader.

Australian Members

Australia is building national buffers that a New Zealand shortage would draw on anyway

The shift: Australia's fuel reserve (Budget 2026-27, May 2026) and minerals reserve (the Department of Industry, undated) are sized and partnered without New Zealand, while New Zealand firms supplying Australian defence are counted as local (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025) and New Zealand has adopted Australian fuel settings (MBIE, undated).

The question to brief: If a New Zealand shortage became an Australian customer's problem, what would our contracts and our government's instruments actually oblige us to do?

New Zealand Members

New Zealand's first binding crisis supply promise is with Singapore, and its cyber law is still unwritten

The shift: The Singapore agreement has been in force since 17 July (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026), diesel cover in the country stood at 25.5 days on 13 September (MBIE stock updates, Sep 2026), and officials propose mandatory incident reporting within 24 and 72 hours (DPMC, Feb 2026).

The question to brief: Should New Zealand seek a trans-Tasman equivalent of the Singapore commitment, and which goods would we ask Australia to cover?

Infrastructure and Energy Firms

Fuel, power and insurance resilience are being funded separately on each side of the Tasman

The shift: Two national fuel reserves of very different size (Budget 2026-27, May 2026) (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026), forecast data centre demand rising to about 13% of Australia's grid by 2035-36 (AEMO, Aug 2026), and around 60,000 uninsured New Zealand homes (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, May 2026).

The question to brief: Which of our assets would be treated as critical infrastructure in one country and not the other, and what does that mean for our resilience spend?

Technology and Defence-Industrial Firms

Firms that count as local in procurement still face two export regimes and two clearance systems

The shift: The licence-free environment covers only AUKUS partners (the Department of Defence, undated), clearance recognition is unfinished (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025), and Japan has named New Zealand as interested in the frigate Australia is buying (Japan's Ministry of Defense, Apr 2026).

The question to brief: Where in our supply chain would a licence requirement or a clearance mismatch stop a trans-Tasman programme, and has anyone mapped it?

Themes

The cycle's signals are organised into four themes, ranked by impact on near-term decisions. Immediate: changes the near-term plan, the structure or the proposition. Near-Term: changes position over the next twelve months. Longer-Range: a multi-year factor to track and revisit each cycle.

1. Standards are converging while crisis supply and minerals deals are struck separately

Immediate

Trans-Tasman Economic & Supply Chain Resilience

The Single Economic Market is moving faster in 2026 than in any recent year. Ministers have launched a programme to implement the Forum's proposals and lifted joint standards by 30% (the CER Trade Ministers, Sep 2026), and New Zealand is adopting Australian settings unilaterally where it helps (MBIE Building Performance, undated). At the same moment the instruments that decide who supplies whom under stress are being written with third countries: an essential supplies agreement with Singapore, separate critical minerals deals with Washington, and an Australian minerals reserve with a partner list that does not include New Zealand. Both sets of decisions are sensible. They are not the same project.

  • The New Zealand-Singapore agreement entered into force on 17 July, with Singapore covering fuel, medicines and chemicals and New Zealand food (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026). Singapore describes it as the world's first legally binding bilateral supply chain resilience agreement (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated).
  • Australia and the United States each mobilised at least US$1 billion for critical minerals projects within six months of their October 2025 agreement, $1.4 billion from Australia and $2.2 billion from the United States (the Department of Industry, Mar 2026), against a project pipeline ASPI put at $8.5 billion (ASPI, Oct 2025).
  • Australia's Critical Minerals Strategic Reserve draws $1 billion from the expanded $5 billion Critical Minerals Facility, is due to operate from the second half of 2026 and lists Canada, Europe, Japan, Korea, the UK and the US as partners (the Department of Industry, undated).
  • New Zealand's Cabinet agreed that officials should re-engage with the US on New Zealand's own bilateral arrangement, noting the US had signed 21 of them with other countries, and the Minerals Strategy aims to double mineral exports to $3 billion by 2035 (MFAT's Cabinet papers, May 2026).
  • The US imposed an additional 12.5% tariff on most New Zealand exports from 24 July, one of 60 economies covering 99% of US goods imports, with Australia in the same band (MFAT, Jul 2026). The shock is shared, which makes the separate responses to it more striking, not less.
  • Australia's container ports are a bottleneck of price rather than capacity: real stevedoring revenue reached a record $423.11 per container lift in 2024-25, and the ACCC concludes a government policy or regulatory response is likely required (the ACCC, Dec 2025).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A dated lever on the minerals both countries are hedging. China's Ministry of Commerce stated, in Chinese, “中方2025年10月9日公布的相关出口管制等措施，暂停实施至2026年11月10日”, which reads in English as “the relevant export control and other measures China announced on 9 October 2025 are suspended until 10 November 2026” (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026). Australia's reserve and both countries' US deals are built around that lever, separately.
  • Weak signal The trade architecture is widening faster than it is deepening. CPTPP ministers recalled the conclusion of Costa Rica's accession talks and agreed to begin preparatory discussions with the UAE, the Philippines and Indonesia (CPTPP ministers, Jun 2026), while New Zealand's overseas investment reform now aims to decide most applications within five working days (Land Information New Zealand, undated). Both widen access. Neither adds a commitment that holds under stress.

Counter-argument

The objection is that this is how a mature relationship should work: harmonise rules jointly, buy insurance from whoever sells it best. Singapore refines and trades fuel; Australia imports it. The leaders' statement shows close coordination on fuel specifications and crisis planning (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026), and the US tariff treated both countries identically (MFAT, Jul 2026). The thesis concedes all of that on fuel. It rests on the goods where Australia is the natural supplier, food processing inputs, construction products and, above all, critical minerals, and on the fact that no trans-Tasman text yet covers any of them. It weakens the moment the implementation programme adds a continuity workstream.

2. AI rules are aligning lightly while critical digital systems are protected under two regimes

Near-Term

Digital, AI & Cyber Resilience

On artificial intelligence the two governments have converged, and they have converged on doing little by law. On the systems that would carry a digital crisis, critical infrastructure, subsea cables and incident response, they run two different regimes, and one of them is still a discussion document. The asymmetry is the opportunity: New Zealand is about to legislate, and a regime designed to be recognised across the Tasman from the outset costs less than one aligned later.

  • The two prime ministers committed to a close relationship on AI built on targeted and proportionate security and safety measures, in a joint statement that says nothing about cyber security or subsea cables (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026).
  • Australia's National AI Plan stepped back from the proposed mandatory guardrails for high-risk AI in favour of existing, largely technology-neutral law, with a $30 million AI Safety Institute from 2026 (ABC News, Dec 2025). Adoption has plateaued: 43% of Australian SMEs reported some AI use in the December to February quarter, down from 45% (the National AI Centre, May 2026).
  • New Zealand's officials write that “New Zealand stands out from other advanced economies in not using dedicated legislative mechanisms to protect critical infrastructure from cyber harm (see Figure 2 for a comparison of global practice).” (DPMC, Feb 2026). The discussion document proposes a 24 hour early warning and a 72 hour full report for about 200 significant entities, and notes that about 35% of SCADA assets are at or near end of life.
  • The threat is rising on both sides. “In Q1 the NCSC responded to three C2 category or 'highly significant' incidents, the first type of these incidents since the 2021/22 financial year.” (the NCSC, Jun 2026) Australia's signals directorate responded to more than 1,200 incidents in 2024-25, up 11%, and reports state actors targeting critical infrastructure (ASD, Oct 2025).
  • Australia depends on about 16 privately operated submarine cables for around 99% of its international communications and data, and cable security barely features in its strategic documents (the ANU National Security College, Aug 2026).
  • Data centre electricity use in Australia's main grid is forecast to rise from about 5 TWh to 34 TWh by 2035-36, from around 3% to about 13% of grid supply (AEMO, Aug 2026), which makes compute a question of energy resilience as much as digital policy.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Cable security is now a contested diplomatic frame. China's Foreign Ministry, responding to questions about trans-Pacific cables, said in Chinese, “将海缆议题政治化、泛安全化，只会破坏正常的国际市场规则，威胁全球数据互联互通和网络安全，不符合任何一方利益。”, which reads in English as “Politicising and pan-securitising the submarine cable issue will only undermine normal international market rules, threaten global data connectivity and cyber security, and serves no party's interests.” (China's Foreign Ministry, Jul 2026). Any trans-Tasman cable protection regime will be read in Beijing through that lens.
  • Weak signal Where integration is real, it runs through defence. The ADF and NZDF signed an agreement giving New Zealand dedicated access to Australia's cyber training environment, a common platform for both forces (the Department of Defence, Mar 2026). The civil equivalent does not exist.

Counter-argument

The strongest counter is that New Zealand is already moving towards Australia: the discussion document benchmarks explicitly against the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act (DPMC, Feb 2026), and the defence cyber agreement shows joint response works where it is organised (the Department of Defence, Mar 2026). On that reading the two regimes are converging with a lag, not diverging. The thesis holds for the next two to three years, which is the window before New Zealand's law is drafted and while its reporting thresholds, definitions and assistance powers are still open. It weakens if the draft bill provides for mutual recognition of Australian obligations.

3. Each country is rebuilding its own fuel buffer and neither holds a claim on the other

Immediate

Energy, Climate & Infrastructure Resilience

The Hormuz disruption turned fuel from a background risk into the year's defining test, and both governments responded quickly and separately. Australia is building a government-controlled reserve and lengthening stockholding obligations; New Zealand has opened its first strategic diesel reserve and aligned its fuel specifications with Australia's to widen its supply pool. The alignment makes New Zealand more dependent on the Australian and regional market at exactly the moment each country's buffer is being sized for itself alone. Climate and insurance risk follow the same pattern: coordinated conversation, national balance sheets.

  • Australia's package commits A$14.8 billion, including a $3.2 billion reserve of about 1 billion litres and 10 more days of minimum stockholding. “Together through these efforts, Australia will increase its diesel and jet fuel reserves to 50 days.” (Budget 2026-27, May 2026)
  • New Zealand's first strategic diesel reserve, 90 million litres at Marsden Point managed by Z Energy, opened in July (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026). On 13 September the country held 25.5 days of diesel, 48.3 including cargoes at sea (MBIE stock updates, Sep 2026).
  • “Diesel exports from Russia, the Middle East and Asia were 1.3 mb/d lower y-o-y, equivalent to about 20% of global seaborne trade.” (the IEA, Aug 2026) Korea, a major refined fuel supplier to the region, reported August petroleum product exports up 65.3% in value but down 2.2% in volume (Korea's trade ministry, Sep 2026), and China, South Korea and India have banned, capped or taxed kerosene exports (ORF Middle East, Jun 2026).
  • The cost is already in the economy. New Zealand inflation rose to 4.1% in the June quarter because of fuel, against 2.9% excluding vehicle fuels, and the cash rate rose to 2.75% (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, Sep 2026). The OECD names the electricity system as the part of New Zealand's economy to strengthen against foreign energy shocks (the OECD, May 2026).
  • Insurance is the slower version of the same stress. Around 60,000 of New Zealand's roughly 2 million homes are uninsured and the average premium is about $2,900 (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, May 2026), while the two industries have formed a joint initiative citing among the world's highest per capita disaster losses (the Insurance Council of Australia, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Climate disclosure is moving apart, not together. New Zealand has raised its climate reporting threshold for listed issuers to $1 billion and eased director liability (Buddle Findlay, Feb 2026), the opposite direction from the aligned taxonomy and standards agenda of the 2+2 Dialogue (the 2+2 Dialogue, Dec 2025).
  • Weak signal Low-carbon fuels are being planned as a shared market but funded as a national one. The 2+2 Dialogue welcomed Australia's $1.1 billion Cleaner Fuels Program and an industry-led trans-Tasman sustainable aviation fuel strategy (the 2+2 Dialogue, Dec 2025). There is no equivalent New Zealand funding line in this evidence base.

Counter-argument

Both countries took part in the IEA's largest ever collective fuel release in March (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026), and importers across Asia have made the same turn to precautionary stock (Fortune, Aug 2026), so national buffers may simply be the correct response. A joint reserve would be expensive and politically awkward. The claim here is not that a common stockpile is needed. It is that New Zealand's supply flexibility now runs through the same regional market Australia draws on, and that in a shortage both buyers compete for the same cargoes with no agreed rule for what happens next (MBIE, undated).

4. New Zealand firms count as Australian in procurement but sit outside AUKUS trade rules

Near-Term

Strategic Industry, Defence & Sovereign Capability

Defence is the most integrated part of the relationship. Anzac 2035 sets out an increasingly integrated combat force, New Zealand firms are treated as Australian for procurement, and Australia is New Zealand's first choice for sustainment. The integration stops at a line drawn elsewhere: the export control environment Australia built with the UK and US, the security clearance systems that have not been mutually recognised, and a frigate decision New Zealand cannot yet legally make the way Australia made it.

  • “The Australian and New Zealand Defence Forces are operationalising our Alliance with a vision of being able to operate seamlessly as an increasingly integrated, combat capable Anzac force by 2035, while remaining respectful of our status as two sovereign countries.” (the Anzac 2035 statement, Mar 2026) Defence industry integration is one of the statement's lines of effort, with co-production to be explored when it makes sense to do so.
  • New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy states: “Under the Closer Economic Relations Trade Agreement and Closer Defence Relations, New Zealand companies are treated as local content in the Australian Defence market.” (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025) It puts the industrial base at around 800 suppliers and records that the current focus is mutual recognition of industry security clearances, which is not yet achieved.
  • Australia's licence-free defence trade environment under the Defence Trade Controls Amendment Act covers authorised users in Australia, the UK and the US (the Department of Defence, undated), and the first Pillar II signature project names only the three AUKUS partners (the Department of Defence, Jun 2026). New Zealand has taken no decision on Pillar II and set aside no funding for it (New Zealand's Ministry of Defence, Sep 2025).
  • Australia has allocated $425 billion over the decade and will reach 3% of GDP by 2033 (the Minister for Defence, Apr 2026). New Zealand plans $12 billion over four years and a move to more than 2% of GDP within the decade (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025).
  • At the signing of Australia's frigate contract, Japan's defence minister said, in Japanese, “「もがみ」型護衛艦に関心を有しているニュージーランドをはじめ、高い技術力を有する我が国装備品に対して既に様々な国からニーズや期待が寄せられています。”, which reads in English as “Starting with New Zealand, which is interested in the Mogami-class frigate, various countries have already expressed needs and expectations for our country's equipment, with its high technological capability.” (Japan's Ministry of Defense, Apr 2026). New Zealand has no equipment transfer agreement with Japan, which Australia has held since 2014, and a recommendation is expected to reach Cabinet before the end of 2027 (East Asia Forum, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The frigate choice is the integration decision in disguise. If New Zealand selects the same Japanese frigate as Australia, ASPI argues, it could unlock economies of scale, joint sustainment and interoperability (ASPI, Sep 2026). The decision falls to whoever governs after November.
  • Weak signal The leaders already welcomed the Forum's defence and space proposals. The June statement welcomed the Forum's proposals on defence industry collaboration and space, but limited space cooperation to exchanging information on regulation (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026).

Counter-argument

Integration in defence industry is already substantial and working: local content status is real market access that most of New Zealand's roughly 800 defence suppliers can use now (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025). The export control boundary matters only to the part of the industrial base working on controlled technology, and New Zealand may prefer to stay outside AUKUS for reasons of foreign policy that have nothing to do with industry. The thesis is therefore about the frontier of the relationship, not its core: the next generation of co-developed capability is exactly the work that sits behind the licence line.

Strategic Implications

Four implications, each tied to a theme and each carrying a posture. Decide means the decision is live now and deferring it is itself a choice. Prepare means the work has to start now for a decision that lands later. Monitor means the evidence does not yet support acting, and says what would change that.

SI 1: Propose a trans-Tasman essential supplies commitment inside the implementation programme

The programme launched on 4 September implements the Forum's own proposals and already names supply chains (the CER Trade Ministers, Sep 2026). The leaders committed only to coordinate their respective supply measures (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026), while New Zealand's binding supply commitment runs to Singapore (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated). A commitment not to impose unnecessary export restrictions on a named list of goods to each other is short, legible and has a working precedent. The programme's scope is being set now, and a proposal made after its first outputs are tabled will be an amendment rather than a design choice.

Action: Table a draft clause with the Forum's next submission: a named goods list covering what each country actually supplies the other, a trigger defined by reference to declared emergencies, and a review mechanism. Ask both governments whether they would accept it, and record the answer.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 3

SI 2: Make critical minerals a trans-Tasman offer before the reserve starts buying

Australia's reserve begins operating in the second half of 2026 with six named partners, none of them New Zealand (the Department of Industry, undated). New Zealand is negotiating its own minerals deal with the United States and wants to double mineral exports by 2035 (MFAT's Cabinet papers, May 2026). China's suspension of its rare earth controls lapses on 10 November (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026). Two small, separate offers to Washington are weaker than one that combines Australian processing with New Zealand deposits.

Action: Commission a short joint assessment of which New Zealand resources could feed Australian processing and the reserve, and what legal step would make New Zealand an eligible partner. Have it ready before the reserve's first offtake decisions.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1

SI 3: Shape New Zealand's critical infrastructure cyber law so Australia can recognise it

New Zealand is consulting on its first dedicated critical infrastructure cyber regime, with 24 and 72 hour reporting for about 200 entities, benchmarked against Australia's Act (DPMC, Feb 2026). Serious incidents are rising on both sides (the NCSC, Jun 2026) (ASD, Oct 2025). Firms operating in both countries will otherwise carry two registers, two reporting clocks and two sets of government assistance powers.

Action: Submit on the design with one ask: that definitions, thresholds and reporting formats be drafted to allow mutual recognition with Australia, and that a joint incident learning channel be provided for. Add subsea cable protection to the Forum's digital agenda, since neither leaders' nor ministers' statements cover it (the ANU National Security College, Aug 2026).

Prepare Draws on Theme 2

SI 4: Track whether New Zealand firms are brought inside the defence trade line

Local content status is real (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025), but the licence-free environment covers only AUKUS authorised users (the Department of Defence, undated), clearance recognition is unfinished, and the frigate choice needs a legal instrument with Japan that New Zealand does not have (East Asia Forum, Jul 2026). None of these is the Forum's to decide, and each moves on a government timetable after the November election.

Action: Watch three triggers: a decision on Pillar II participation (New Zealand's Ministry of Defence, Sep 2025), completion of mutual recognition of industry security clearances, and signature of a New Zealand equipment transfer agreement with Japan. Any one of them moves this implication to Prepare.

Scenario Matrix

Two uncertainties shape the relationship to 2030. The first is whether the external shocks of 2026 prove episodic or become a standing condition of trade in fuel, minerals and technology. The second is whether the two countries answer them with coordinated national measures or with binding commitments to each other. The first is not in trans-Tasman hands. The second is.

Compounding shocks, binding commitments

Good neighbours

Hormuz reopens, rare earth controls stay suspended and the implementation programme delivers cheaper compliance and more joint standards (the CER Trade Ministers, Sep 2026). National buffers are never tested against each other. This is the comfortable path and, on current evidence, the default.

Early indicators: Programme outputs limited to standards and cost of doing business; fuel stock cover recovers; no new supply commitments.

Competing for the same cargo

A second disruption arrives before buffers are built. Both countries buy from the same shrinking regional pool (the IEA, Aug 2026), New Zealand leans on Singapore (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated), and trans-Tasman supply decisions are made case by case under political pressure. Integration on paper meets separation in practice.

Early indicators: Diesel cover falls below current levels; further export restrictions by regional refiners (ORF Middle East, Jun 2026); public friction over allocation.

Insured in advance

The two governments use a calm period to write an essential supplies clause and a minerals arrangement into the Single Economic Market. Nothing is triggered, and the value is invisible until needed. This is the outcome this briefing argues is available before 2030.

Early indicators: A supply continuity workstream in the programme; New Zealand named as a reserve partner; mutual recognition in New Zealand's cyber law.

One market under stress

Commitments written in advance are tested and hold. Allocation follows agreed rules, minerals move to Australian processing under a joint offer, and defence industry works behind a shared licence line. Expensive to build and demanding to operate, and the only quadrant in which the Single Economic Market behaves as one market when it matters.

Early indicators: Joint crisis allocation rules; New Zealand inside the licence-free environment; shared frigate sustainment (ASPI, Sep 2026).

What We Are Not Planning For

Out of scope this cycle

A shared trans-Tasman fuel stockpile

The briefing argues for a commitment not to restrict supply, not for a pooled reserve. Both governments have just funded national reserves of very different size (Budget 2026-27, May 2026) (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026), and nothing in the evidence suggests either would fund a joint one within the horizon.

↺ Reinstated if either government proposes pooled stockholding or reciprocal ticket arrangements in a formal statement.

Out of scope this cycle

A trans-Tasman electricity interconnector

The two grids are separate and will remain so to 2030. Electricity resilience is treated here as a national question with shared consequences, through data centre demand (AEMO, Aug 2026) and New Zealand's firming gap (the OECD, May 2026), not as a joint infrastructure project.

↺ Reinstated if a government or credible developer publishes a feasibility study for a cable link.

Deliberately not modelled

New Zealand joining AUKUS as a full partner

New Zealand has taken no decision on Pillar II and set aside no funding (New Zealand's Ministry of Defence, Sep 2025), and full membership is not on any public agenda. The briefing treats the licence line as a fact to work around rather than a decision to anticipate.

↺ Reinstated if a New Zealand government announces Pillar II participation or a funded project.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If fuel, medicines or construction products ran short across the Tasman next year, what would oblige either government to keep supplying the other, beyond coordinating their respective measures (the Leaders' Meeting statement, Jun 2026)?
  2. New Zealand's first binding supply commitment is with Singapore (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated). Which goods would the Forum ask Australia to cover, and which would New Zealand offer in return?
  3. Australia's minerals reserve starts buying this half-year without New Zealand as a partner (the Department of Industry, undated). Is a combined offer to Washington stronger than two separate ones, and who would make it?
  4. New Zealand's cyber law is still a discussion document (DPMC, Feb 2026). What would it take for a firm registered under Australia's regime to meet New Zealand's with the same filing?
  5. Local content status stops at the AUKUS licence line (the Department of Defence, undated). Which trans-Tasman programmes in our members' pipelines will need to cross it before 2030?
