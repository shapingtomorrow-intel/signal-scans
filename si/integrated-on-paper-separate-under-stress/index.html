Why This Matters Now

Australia and New Zealand drew closer in 2026 where it counts in normal times. Ministers launched a programme to carry out the Forum's proposals, the two standards bodies signed a new agreement, and New Zealand adopted Australia's fuel specifications to widen its pool of suppliers.

The year's fuel shock tested something else, and the answer was realistic. Two countries that both import their fuel cannot promise each other supply in a shortage, and their trade treaty has always let each protect its own market when essential goods run critically short. Both turned to Singapore, the region's refining hub, under agreements that leave the same room.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: resilience across the Tasman will come from keeping commercial supply moving and from agreeing, in advance, how each government treats the other's market before it restricts exports. The implementation programme is where that can be proposed, and its scope is being set now.

Executive Synthesis

Which of the Forum's proposals would still matter in the week a crisis began?

The ones that keep commercial supply moving. Neither government will export essential reserves before meeting its own needs, and every agreement in force is written to allow for that.

Australia and New Zealand are joining up where it costs nothing in a crisis and staying national where it would. Ministers launched a programme to implement the Forum's proposals and lifted joint standards by 30% (the CER trade ministers, Sep 2026), and New Zealand aligned its fuel specifications with Australia's to widen its pool of suppliers (New Zealand's Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment, undated). But two net importers of fuel cannot promise each other supply. The CER agreement has barred new export restrictions between them since 1983, while letting either act “to prevent or relieve critical shortages of foodstuffs or other essential goods” (the CER agreement, 1983). Both countries' new agreements with Singapore leave the same room (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated) (Singapore's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Apr 2026).

Four questions stay open. Will the implementation programme set any rules for how each government treats the other's market before it restricts exports? Will New Zealand be offered a place in Australia's critical minerals reserve before it starts buying, with China's suspension of its rare earth export controls due to lapse on 10 November (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026)? Will New Zealand's first critical infrastructure cyber law be written so that Australia can recognise it (New Zealand's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, Feb 2026)? And will New Zealand firms move inside the defence trade line Australia drew with the UK and the US (Australia's Department of Defence, undated)?

Looking ahead to 2030, Australia plans to hold 50 days of diesel and jet fuel (Australia's 2026-27 Budget, May 2026). The International Energy Agency projects that the leading country's share of rare earth refining falls only to 70% by 2035, even if planned projects arrive on time (the International Energy Agency, Jul 2026). And Australia's defence spending rises to 3% of GDP by 2033 (Australia's Minister for Defence, Apr 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 7 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The analysis assumes that commercial supply keeps crossing the Tasman short of rationing. In a severe shortage both governments may ration at once, and importers across Asia have already turned to precautionary stock (Fortune, Aug 2026). If that happens before any rules of conduct are agreed, Implication 1 moves from proposing rules to managing a dispute.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Put supply continuity rules on the implementation programme's agenda. The achievable ask is not promised volumes but agreed conduct: notice and consultation before either government restricts essential exports to the other, and restrictions no wider or longer than necessary.
  2. Make critical minerals a trans-Tasman offer before Australia's reserve starts buying. The reserve operates from the second half of 2026 without New Zealand as a named partner.
  3. Engage in New Zealand's cyber law while it is still a discussion document. A law drafted to be recognised in Australia from the start costs less than one aligned afterwards.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Standards and suppliers are shared while crisis supply and minerals deals are made nationally

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Both governments' trade and resources ministers, through the implementation programme · Room to act: High: the programme implements the Forum's own proposals

For members, the gain of 2026 is shared rules and shared suppliers; the limit is that crisis supply and minerals are handled nationally. The Single Economic Market moved faster this year than in any recent one. At the same time, the instruments that decide what happens under stress were written with third partners: both countries have essential supplies agreements with Singapore, both are pursuing their own critical minerals deals with Washington, and Australia's minerals reserve lists partners that do not include New Zealand. For fuel, no promise between two importers could do what a hub can. For minerals, where each country has something the other lacks, separate deals leave value on the table.

Analyst construction from the sources cited in the Themes. Joint decisions set rules and coordinate; the supply agreements are made with Singapore.

  • Rules are converging by agreement and by borrowing. Ministers launched a programme to implement the Forum's proposals and lifted joint standards by 30% (the CER trade ministers, Sep 2026). The standards bodies' new agreement supports “more than 2,000 existing joint standards” (Standards Australia, Feb 2026), after about 500 had been dropped since 2016 because New Zealand could not afford to take part (RNZ, Feb 2026). New Zealand is also evaluating Australian plumbing certification for recognition (the ministry's building performance unit, undated).
  • The treaty keeps exports open in normal times and national in a shortage. The CER agreement bars either country from imposing new limits on the quantity of goods it exports to the other, but allows measures to relieve critical shortages of essential goods (the CER agreement, 1983). The leaders' June statement commits the two only to coordinate their respective supply measures and crisis planning (the leaders' meeting statement, Jun 2026).
  • Both countries turned to Singapore. New Zealand's agreement, in force since 17 July, has Singapore covering fuel, medicines and chemicals and New Zealand covering food (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026). Australia signed its own protocol on 27 July and set up a standing dialogue to run it (Australia's Foreign Minister, Jul 2026).
  • Minerals are being bargained separately. Australia and the United States each put at least US$1 billion into projects within six months of their October 2025 agreement (Australia's Department of Industry, Mar 2026). Australia's reserve draws A$1 billion from its critical minerals facility and names Canada, Europe, Japan, Korea, the UK and the US as partners (Australia's Department of Industry, undated), while New Zealand's Cabinet agreed to pursue its own deal with Washington (New Zealand Cabinet papers, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A dated lever on the minerals both countries are hedging. China's Ministry of Commerce said the export controls it announced on 9 October 2025, including its expanded rare earth controls, are suspended only until 10 November 2026 (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026). Australia's reserve and both countries' US deals are built around that lever, separately.

Outlook to 2030

Rare earth supply stays concentrated to the horizon and beyond. The International Energy Agency says the leading country's share of rare earth refining fell from over 90% in 2023 to 85% in 2025 and, “if planned projects come online as scheduled, it is projected to fall to 70% by 2035” (the International Energy Agency, Jul 2026). New Zealand aims to double its mineral exports to $3 billion by 2035 (New Zealand Cabinet papers, May 2026).

What it means: A joint offer that pairs Australian processing with New Zealand resources is worth more before Australia's reserve makes its first purchases than after.

Counter-argument

The objection is that integration is advancing exactly where it can and nothing is missing. Common standards and specifications widen each country's pool of suppliers at no cost to taxpayers, the CER agreement already keeps exports open in normal times (the CER agreement, 1983), and the US tariff treated both countries identically (New Zealand's Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Trade, Jul 2026). This briefing accepts that on fuel. It still holds for the goods where Australia is New Zealand's natural supplier, and for minerals, where nothing yet says how a joint offer would be made. An academic review describes a trans-Tasman habit of “cooperation modalities that assume trust and confidence in each other, while recognising the need for safeguards for those times when sovereign states differ” (Policy Quarterly, Aug 2026). The safeguards are the point: the shortage exception is one.

2. AI rules are aligning lightly while critical digital systems are protected under two regimes

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: New Zealand's government and Parliament, drafting its first critical infrastructure cyber law · Room to act: Moderate: submissions while the law is a discussion document

Firms that operate in both countries face two cyber regimes for their most critical systems, and one of them is still being written. On artificial intelligence the two governments have converged on doing little by law. On critical infrastructure, subsea cables and incident response they run different regimes. The opening is timing: New Zealand is about to legislate, and a law designed to be recognised across the Tasman costs less than one aligned later.

  • New Zealand's officials write that “New Zealand stands out from other advanced economies in not using dedicated legislative mechanisms to protect critical infrastructure from cyber harm (see Figure 2 for a comparison of global practice).” (New Zealand's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, Feb 2026). The discussion document proposes an early warning within 24 hours and a full report within 72 hours for about 200 significant entities.
  • The threat is rising on both sides. New Zealand's cyber agency handled three incidents in its most serious category in the first quarter of 2026, the first since 2021/22 (New Zealand's National Cyber Security Centre, Jun 2026). Australia's signals directorate responded to more than 1,200 incidents in 2024-25, up 11% (the Australian Signals Directorate, Oct 2025).
  • Australia relies on about 16 privately operated submarine cables for around 99% of its international communications, and cable security barely features in its strategic documents (the Australian National University's National Security College, Aug 2026). The leaders' June statement covers AI but not cyber security or subsea cables (the leaders' meeting statement, Jun 2026).
  • Australia stepped back from mandatory rules for high-risk AI in favour of existing law, with a A$30 million safety institute (ABC News, Dec 2025), and 43% of Australian small and medium-sized firms reported some AI use in the December to February quarter, down from 45% (Australia's National AI Centre, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Where integration is real, it runs through defence. The two defence forces signed an agreement giving New Zealand dedicated access to Australia's cyber training environment (Australia's Department of Defence, Mar 2026). The civil equivalent does not exist.

Outlook to 2030

Demand on the systems the regimes protect keeps rising. The Australian Energy Market Operator forecasts data-centre electricity use in the main grid rising from about 5 TWh to 34 TWh by 2035-36, from around 3% to about 13% of grid supply (the Australian Energy Market Operator, Aug 2026). New Zealand's law will be drafted within the horizon, and its reporting thresholds, definitions and assistance powers are still open (New Zealand's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, Feb 2026).

What it means: Submit now for definitions and reporting formats that Australia can recognise; a later fix means two filings for every firm operating in both countries.

Counter-argument

The strongest counter is that New Zealand is already moving towards Australia: the discussion document benchmarks explicitly against Australia's Security of Critical Infrastructure Act (New Zealand's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, Feb 2026), and the defence cyber agreement shows joint response works where it is organised (Australia's Department of Defence, Mar 2026). The thesis holds for the two to three years before New Zealand's law is drafted, and weakens if the draft provides for mutual recognition.

3. Each country is rebuilding its own fuel buffer while shared specifications and suppliers keep the market joined

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Energy ministers, fuel importers and Singapore · Room to act: Moderate on conduct rules and specifications; low on reserves

For members in fuel, freight and energy, supply across the Tasman now rests on commercial alignment, not on either government's reserves. Both governments answered the fuel shock with national buffers, which is the right answer for two net importers. New Zealand aligned its fuel specifications with Australia's, which opens it to the larger pool of suppliers already serving the Australian market without a separate compliance regime. Ampol's Singapore trading arm sources product for its Australian network and for “Z Energy in New Zealand, which is 100% owned by Ampol” (Ampol, undated). Climate and insurance risk follow the same pattern: coordinated conversation, national balance sheets.

Two national fuel buffers, in days of diesel cover

Bars to scale from zero. Australia's figure is the 2026-27 Budget target for diesel and jet fuel, not current stock (May 2026); New Zealand's are diesel on 13 September 2026 from its Ministry of Business, Innovation and Employment. Behind the bars sit a 1 billion litre Australian reserve and a 90 million litre New Zealand one, each held for its own market.

  • Australia's package commits A$14.8 billion, including a reserve of about 1 billion litres and 10 more days of minimum stockholding: “Together through these efforts, Australia will increase its diesel and jet fuel reserves to 50 days.” (Australia's 2026-27 Budget, May 2026)
  • New Zealand's first strategic diesel reserve, 90 million litres at Marsden Point managed by Z Energy, opened in July (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026). On 13 September the country held 25.5 days of diesel, 48.3 counting cargoes at sea (the ministry's fuel stock updates, Sep 2026).
  • The regional pool is shrinking. Diesel exports from Russia, the Middle East and Asia were 1.3 million barrels a day lower than a year earlier, about 20% of global seaborne trade (the International Energy Agency, Aug 2026), and China, South Korea and India banned, capped or taxed kerosene exports (the Observer Research Foundation Middle East, Jun 2026).
  • The cost is already in the economy. New Zealand inflation rose to 4.1% in the June quarter because of fuel, against 2.9% excluding vehicle fuels, and the official cash rate rose to 2.75% (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, Sep 2026). Around 60,000 of New Zealand's roughly 2 million homes are uninsured (the Reserve Bank of New Zealand, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Low-carbon fuels are being planned as a shared market but funded as national ones. The two countries' climate and finance ministers welcomed Australia's A$1.1 billion Cleaner Fuels Program and an industry-led trans-Tasman sustainable aviation fuel strategy (the two countries' climate and finance ministers, Dec 2025), and the treasurers returned to that supply chain in April (Australia's Treasurer, Apr 2026). There is no equivalent New Zealand funding line in this evidence base.

Outlook to 2030

Australia's reserves are to reach 50 days of diesel and jet fuel (Australia's 2026-27 Budget, May 2026), and its protocol with Singapore enters into force once both sides complete their domestic processes (Singapore's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Apr 2026). Both countries will go into the horizon holding national buffers and leaning on the same hub.

What it means: Plan on reserves staying national, and treat aligned specifications and agreed conduct in a shortage as the trans-Tasman levers.

Counter-argument

The strongest counter is that the market may not behave as this briefing expects in a severe shortage. Importers across Asia turned to precautionary stock this year (Fortune, Aug 2026), and when both countries buy from the same hub at the same time, shared specifications mean they bid for the same cargoes. National buffers answer that risk, and both countries joined the International Energy Agency's largest collective fuel release in March (the leaders' meeting statement, Jun 2026). The claim is narrower: short of rationing, what keeps supply moving is commercial.

4. New Zealand firms count as Australian in procurement but sit outside AUKUS trade rules

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Defence ministries, and after November New Zealand's next government · Room to act: Low: government to government, though members can map their exposure

Defence is the most integrated part of the relationship, and the integration stops at a line drawn elsewhere. New Zealand firms are treated as Australian for procurement, and Australia is New Zealand's first choice for sustainment. But the licence-free trade environment Australia built with the UK and the US, the unfinished recognition of security clearances and a frigate decision New Zealand cannot yet legally make the way Australia did all sit outside that integration.

  • “The Australian and New Zealand Defence Forces are operationalising our Alliance with a vision of being able to operate seamlessly as an increasingly integrated, combat capable Anzac force by 2035, while remaining respectful of our status as two sovereign countries.” (the Anzac 2035 statement, Mar 2026)
  • “Under the Closer Economic Relations Trade Agreement and Closer Defence Relations, New Zealand companies are treated as local content in the Australian Defence market.” (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025) The strategy puts the industrial base at around 800 suppliers and records that mutual recognition of security clearances is not yet achieved.
  • Australia's licence-free defence trade environment covers authorised users in Australia, the UK and the US (Australia's Department of Defence, undated), and the first joint advanced-technology project under AUKUS names only those three (Australia's Department of Defence, Jun 2026). New Zealand has taken no decision on joining that work (New Zealand's Ministry of Defence, Sep 2025).
  • At the signing of Australia's frigate contract, Japan's defence minister named New Zealand first among the countries interested in the Mogami-class frigate Australia is buying (Japan's Ministry of Defense, Apr 2026). New Zealand has no equipment transfer agreement with Japan, which Australia has held since 2014 (East Asia Forum, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The frigate choice is the integration decision in disguise. If New Zealand selects the same Japanese frigate as Australia, it could unlock economies of scale, joint sustainment and interoperability (the Australian Strategic Policy Institute, Sep 2026). The decision falls to whoever governs after November.

Outlook to 2030

Australia has allocated A$425 billion over the decade and its defence spending rises to 3% of GDP by 2033 (Australia's Minister for Defence, Apr 2026). New Zealand plans NZ$12 billion over four years and more than 2% of GDP within the decade (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025). A frigate recommendation is expected to reach New Zealand's Cabinet before the end of 2027 (East Asia Forum, Jul 2026).

What it means: Map now which members' programmes would need to cross the licence line before 2030, so the Forum's defence proposals rest on named cases.

Counter-argument

Integration in defence industry is already substantial and working: local content status is real market access that most of New Zealand's roughly 800 defence suppliers can use now (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025). The export control boundary matters only to firms working on controlled technology, and New Zealand may prefer to stay outside AUKUS for reasons of foreign policy. The thesis is about the frontier of the relationship, not its core.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Agree how the CER shortage exception would be used, inside the implementation programme

The Forum should propose agreed conduct around the exception, not promised supply. The programme launched on 4 September implements the Forum's own proposals and already names supply chains (the CER trade ministers, Sep 2026), and supply chain resilience was the subject of the Forum's April discussion with both finance ministers (Inside Government NZ, Apr 2026). The CER agreement keeps exports open in normal times and lets either government act to relieve critical shortages (the CER agreement, 1983), and both Singapore agreements leave the same room (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated) (Singapore's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Apr 2026). No government will trade that room away. What is missing is whether the other government hears before a restriction lands, how narrow and how long it may be, and how the two work their shared Singapore relationship.

Action: Table a short supply continuity proposal with the Forum's next submission: notice and consultation before either government restricts essential exports to the other, restrictions no wider or longer than necessary, specifications and product standards kept aligned, and a joint approach to Singapore. Exclude guaranteed volumes explicitly. Record each government's answer before the programme's first outputs are tabled.

Implication 2: Make critical minerals a trans-Tasman offer before Australia's reserve starts buying

Australia's reserve begins operating in the second half of 2026 with six named partners, none of them New Zealand (Australia's Department of Industry, undated). New Zealand is pursuing its own deal with Washington and wants to double its mineral exports by 2035 (New Zealand Cabinet papers, May 2026). China's suspension of its rare earth controls lapses on 10 November (China's Ministry of Commerce, Apr 2026). Two small, separate offers to Washington are weaker than one that combines Australian processing with New Zealand resources.

Action: Commission a short joint assessment of which New Zealand resources could feed Australian processing and the reserve, and what legal step would make New Zealand an eligible partner. Have it ready before the reserve's first purchasing decisions.

Implication 3: Shape New Zealand's critical infrastructure cyber law so that Australia can recognise it

New Zealand is consulting on its first dedicated cyber regime for critical infrastructure, with 24 and 72 hour reporting for about 200 entities, benchmarked against Australia's law (New Zealand's Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, Feb 2026). Serious incidents are rising on both sides (New Zealand's National Cyber Security Centre, Jun 2026) (the Australian Signals Directorate, Oct 2025). Otherwise firms operating in both countries will carry two registers, two reporting clocks and two sets of government assistance powers.

Action: Submit on the design with one ask: definitions, thresholds and reporting formats drafted to allow mutual recognition with Australia, plus a joint channel for learning from incidents. Add subsea cable protection to the Forum's digital agenda.

Prepare Draws on Theme 2

Implication 4: Track whether New Zealand firms are brought inside the defence trade line

Local content status is real (New Zealand's Defence Industry Strategy, Oct 2025), but the licence-free environment covers only the three AUKUS partners (Australia's Department of Defence, undated), clearance recognition is unfinished, and the frigate choice needs a legal agreement with Japan that New Zealand does not have (East Asia Forum, Jul 2026). None of these is the Forum's to decide, and each moves on a government timetable after the November election.

Action: Watch three triggers: a decision on joining AUKUS advanced-technology work (New Zealand's Ministry of Defence, Sep 2025), completion of mutual recognition of security clearances, and a New Zealand equipment transfer agreement with Japan. Any one of them moves this implication to Prepare.

Joined-Up Supply

In 2030 firms carry most trans-Tasman supply through a shortage. Common fuel specifications and joint standards let cargoes and products go where they are needed, and the two governments have agreed to give notice and consult before either restricts exports. Critical minerals are offered to partners jointly, and New Zealand's cyber law is recognised in Australia. Reserves stay national and are rarely drawn.

Early indicators: A supply continuity workstream in the implementation programme; New Zealand named as a reserve partner; a mutual recognition clause in New Zealand's cyber bill.

Managed Together

In 2030 governments hold the levers in a shortage: national reserves, demand management and export restrictions. They use them on agreed terms, notifying each other first, coordinating demand management and dealing with Singapore together under the dialogue Australia set up (Australia's Foreign Minister, Jul 2026). Defence industry works behind a shared licence line.

Early indicators: Agreed notice and consultation rules; joint demand-management plans; New Zealand inside the licence-free environment.

Two Markets, One Supply Chain

In 2030 integrated firms still move fuel and goods across the Tasman, but the governments have agreed nothing about conduct in a shortage. Each firm follows two sets of rules when governments act, and plans its contingencies as two supply zones. Minerals deals with Washington stay separate, and firms file cyber incidents twice.

Early indicators: Specifications stay aligned; no conduct rules in the programme's outputs; two separate US minerals frameworks.

Each Behind Its Own Reserve

In 2030 a further disruption has made governments the main allocators. Each rations and restricts exports case by case, leaning on its own Singapore agreement, and New Zealand buyers bid against Australian ones for the same hub cargoes as regional producers keep restricting exports (the Observer Research Foundation Middle East, Jun 2026). The Single Economic Market works in normal times and pauses in a crisis.

Early indicators: Diesel cover falls below current levels; further export restrictions by regional refiners; public friction over allocation.

What We Are Not Planning For

Out of scope this cycle

Guaranteed fuel volumes, or a shared stockpile, between the two countries

Both countries are net importers of fuel, and neither government would bind itself to export essential reserves before meeting its own needs. The treaty already says as much: the CER agreement's bar on new export restrictions gives way to measures to relieve critical shortages of essential goods (the CER agreement, 1983), and both Singapore agreements leave the same room (Singapore's Ministry of Trade and Industry, undated) (Singapore's Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Apr 2026). Both governments have also just funded national reserves of very different size (Australia's 2026-27 Budget, May 2026) (the New Zealand Government, Jul 2026).

↺ Reinstated if either government proposes pooled stockholding, reciprocal stockholding tickets or guaranteed supply volumes in a formal statement.

Out of scope this cycle

A trans-Tasman electricity interconnector

The two grids are separate and will remain so to 2030. Electricity resilience is treated here as a national question with shared consequences, through data-centre demand (the Australian Energy Market Operator, Aug 2026) and New Zealand's need to strengthen its electricity system (the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, May 2026), not as a joint infrastructure project.

↺ Reinstated if a government or credible developer publishes a feasibility study for a cable link.

Deliberately not modelled

New Zealand joining AUKUS as a full partner

New Zealand has taken no decision on joining AUKUS advanced-technology work and set aside no funding (New Zealand's Ministry of Defence, Sep 2025), and full membership is not on any public agenda. The briefing treats the licence line as a fact to work around rather than a decision to anticipate.

↺ Reinstated if a New Zealand government announces participation in AUKUS advanced-technology work or a funded project.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. The CER agreement lets either government act to relieve a critical shortage. If either used that exception next year, what notice, consultation and limits would the Forum want agreed first?
  2. Both countries now rely on Singapore for fuel assurance. What could the two governments and the fuel companies that serve both markets do together with Singapore that neither can do alone?
