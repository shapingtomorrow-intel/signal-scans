Key risks

Why This Matters Now

Laboratories that run experiments with little human help are moving from showcase projects to national infrastructure in the US, China, Japan and Europe, and the largest drug and life-science companies are building their own.

These labs, and the AI that directs them, make ideas for new medicines cheap. What they have not yet made cheap is proof. No medicine discovered by AI is yet fully approved, AI-made results need careful checking, and many routine experiments fail when another lab repeats them. At the same time, regulators are starting to accept human-relevant laboratory data in place of animal tests, which makes trusted proof more valuable.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: a charity’s money may do most for medical research by paying for shared, trusted proof, meaning open data, replication and validation capacity, rather than for more robots.

Executive Synthesis

Which grants on your desk this year pay to generate new ideas, and which pay to prove them?

The evidence here says AI and autonomous labs make hypotheses cheap, while validation and replication remain slow, scarce and underfunded.

Autonomous laboratories are being built at national scale. The US has committed more than $5 billion to its Genesis Mission, under which four federal agencies will build autonomous labs (The White House, Jul 2026), and the National Science Foundation is funding 20 shared cloud-lab teams (US National Science Foundation, Jul 2026). Japan’s science ministry has asked for 378.9 billion yen for AI-driven science next year (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, Sep 2026), and Beijing’s plan makes autonomous labs a flagship goal by 2028 (Beijing municipal government, in Chinese, Jul 2026). Companies are moving too: Danaher expects its autonomous lab to run at scale in early 2027 (Reuters, Oct 2026). Yet the gain so far is in ideas, not proof. Google DeepMind calls AI agents “conjecture machines” that make hypotheses cheap (Google DeepMind, Jul 2026), while AI-enabled drug programmes do no better than others at Phase II (IQVIA Institute, May 2026) and no AI-discovered drug is yet fully approved (Clinical Trial Vanguard, Aug 2026).

Some of this is settled: regulators are moving away from animal data, with the US Food and Drug Administration rewriting its rules around ‘nonclinical’ tests (US Food and Drug Administration, Sep 2026). Four things are not. Can AI-run results be trusted, when AI scientist systems showed integrity problems in 34.2% of test runs (SciIntegrity-Bench preprint, May 2026) and routine experiments replicate only 20% to 44% of the time (Brazilian Reproducibility Initiative, eLife, Jul 2026)? Will faster discovery raise success at Phase II, where most medicines fail? Who will own the data, when partners in a virtual-cell coalition get a year’s exclusive access (eWeek, Oct 2026)? And will safety rules keep pace, when screening of AI-designed DNA has not been updated (Congressional Research Service, Sep 2026)?

Looking ahead to 2030, the UK aims to develop trial-ready drugs within 100 days (UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, Nov 2025), Japan plans to expand shared AI-for-science computing more than tenfold (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, May 2026), and Roche aims to bring up to 20 new molecular entities to market (Cailian Press, in Chinese, Sep 2026). Market analysts expect lab automation to grow steadily, to $9.01 billion by 2030 (MarketsandMarkets, Aug 2026), rather than in a leap. None of this is certain, and each is a place where a medical research funder can still choose its position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that proof, rather than speed, is now the limit. In Google’s aptamer work an AI-run loop raised confirmed hit rates from 0% to 42.1% in a few rounds of 7 to 10 days each (Google Research, Oct 2026). If closed loops like this work as well in disease biology, speed itself becomes the prize, private capital will build its own validation, and a charity’s money might do more on the biology questions than on shared infrastructure.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Fund shared proof, not private robots. Co-fund validation and replication capacity that every grantee can use.
  2. Make open, standard data a condition of AI-enabled grants. The rules on data in the new networks are being set now.
  3. Attach safety and integrity conditions to autonomous biology. Public rules lag the capability.

Each is developed in the Strategic Implications, with alternatives in the Options.

1. Autonomous laboratories are becoming public infrastructure, but proof, not speed, now limits medical discovery

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Governments and the largest companies on capacity; research funders, including charities, on what gets proved and shared · Room to act: Wide: the networks, data rules and standards are being set now, and funders can still shape them

Autonomous laboratories combine AI that proposes experiments with robots that run them, often in shared ‘cloud labs’ that researchers program remotely. In 2026 they stopped being a curiosity: governments are building them as national infrastructure, and the largest companies are building their own. They make hypotheses fast and cheap. The question for medical research is what they do to the slow, expensive part, which is proving that a result is real, reproducible and relevant to people, and who will own the data and rules of the new networks.

Sources: (The White House, Jul 2026); (US National Science Foundation, Jul 2026); (US Food and Drug Administration, Sep 2026); (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, Sep 2026); (US Department of Energy, Oct 2026); (Reuters, Oct 2026); (Beijing municipal government, in Chinese, Jul 2026); (European Commission, Jun 2026); (UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, Nov 2025).

  • Public build-out. The US Genesis Mission brings more than $5 billion of federal commitments, with the Department of Energy, the health department, the National Science Foundation and the national standards institute to build autonomous labs (The White House, Jul 2026); the National Science Foundation alone is funding 20 shared cloud-lab teams with $380 million over four years (US National Science Foundation, Jul 2026). Japan’s science ministry has asked for 378.9 billion yen for AI-driven science in FY2027, against 56.3 billion yen this year (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, Sep 2026), and Beijing will part-fund autonomous-lab projects only where companies put in at least twice the public money (Beijing Science and Technology Commission, in Chinese, Aug 2026). Germany’s Helmholtz research centres already run 21 self-driving labs, including work on heart valves for infants (Computerwoche, in German, Sep 2026).
  • Companies are scaling. Danaher says its autonomous lab will make discovering molecules eight times faster from early 2027 (Reuters, Oct 2026), Roche has begun building AI automated labs and aims to bring up to 20 new molecular entities to market by 2030 (Cailian Press, in Chinese, Sep 2026), and USTC’s robotic chemist in China coordinates 110 robots and 196 workstations (China National Radio, in Chinese, Sep 2026). The business model is unproven: Ginkgo Bioworks says its automated testing service is ten times cheaper than outsourcing, yet its quarterly revenue fell 48% (Ginkgo Bioworks, Aug 2026).
  • Early loops are much faster. In Google’s agent-run aptamer work each design, test and analyse round took 7 to 10 days, and confirmed hit rates rose from 0% to 42.1% (Google Research, Oct 2026); FutureHouse’s Robin went from concept to a submitted Nature paper in 2.5 months (FutureHouse, May 2026); and Insilico’s rentosertib went from target to preclinical candidate in about 18 months and is now in a Phase III trial (News-Medical, Sep 2026).
  • Proof has not kept pace. AI-enabled programmes reach 75% Phase I success but only match other programmes at Phase II (IQVIA Institute, May 2026), and as of August 2026 no AI-discovered drug held full approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (Clinical Trial Vanguard, Aug 2026). AI scientist systems showed integrity problems in 34.2% of test runs (SciIntegrity-Bench preprint, May 2026), and across 56 laboratories only 20% to 44% of routine biomedical experiments replicated (Brazilian Reproducibility Initiative, eLife, Jul 2026).
  • Regulators are changing what counts as evidence. The US Food and Drug Administration has replaced ‘animal tests’ with ‘nonclinical tests’ throughout its drug and biologics rules (US Food and Drug Administration, Sep 2026), the National Cancer Institute asks applicants to validate animal findings in human models (US National Cancer Institute, Aug 2026), and the European Commission’s roadmap away from animal testing says AI will play an important role, with a stock-take by 2029 (European Commission, Jun 2026).
  • Philanthropy is buying in, mostly on data. Biohub has committed $500 million to open biological data (Biohub, Apr 2026), the Department of Energy is matching it (US Department of Energy, Oct 2026), and the Novo Nordisk Foundation intends to open its AI supercomputer to European researchers through the European Commission’s pledging scheme (European Commission, Oct 2026). But companies funding the virtual-cell coalition get a year’s exclusive access to the data (eWeek, Oct 2026), and the field names proprietary formats, high set-up costs and missing standards as its main barriers (Matter, May 2026).

Sources: (Google Research, Oct 2026); (FutureHouse, May 2026); (News-Medical, Sep 2026); (Clinical Trial Vanguard, Aug 2026). Company and non-profit accounts of their own projects, read as directional.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal China is writing the first standards for autonomous labs. Beijing’s plan includes construction standards and graded evaluation for autonomous labs (Beijing municipal government, in Chinese, Jul 2026), and preparation has begun for a national standards subcommittee on them (Science and Technology Daily, in Chinese, Oct 2026). Whoever sets these standards shapes which instruments, data formats and safety checks become the default.
  • Weak signal Instruments built for human hands are the hidden bottleneck. Researchers argue that most lab instruments cannot be driven by software, after nearly $2 billion went into autonomous labs in 2025, and Renaissance Philanthropy is scoping a fund for open, automation-ready instruments (Renaissance Philanthropy, Feb 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The UK aims to develop trial-ready drugs within 100 days by 2030 and says there is strong evidence that autonomous labs will substantially accelerate research (UK Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, Nov 2025). Japan plans to expand shared AI-for-science computing more than tenfold by FY2030 (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, May 2026), and Japan’s cabinet wants automated and cloud labs that continuously generate high-quality data (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, Aug 2026). Roche aims to bring up to 20 new molecular entities to market by 2030 (Cailian Press, in Chinese, Sep 2026), while market analysts expect lab automation to reach $9.01 billion by 2030, a steady 7.2% a year (MarketsandMarkets, Aug 2026). On this briefing’s reading, by 2030 shared autonomous labs are common in well-funded systems, and the gap between those that can prove and share their results and those that cannot is wider than the gap in speed.

What it means: treat proof, meaning validation, replication and open, standard data, as the scarce asset worth a charity’s money, and treat speed as something governments and companies will buy anyway.

Counter-argument

Speed may be the prize after all. Danaher expects eight times faster discovery (Reuters, Oct 2026), and Google’s agent-run loop raised hit rates from 0% to 42.1% within a few rounds (Google Research, Oct 2026); if such loops work in disease biology, companies will fund their own validation. The Institute for Progress also warns that AI will not accelerate fields that lack data or instruments (Institute for Progress, Jul 2026), so shared infrastructure for medicine may arrive slowly whatever a charity funds, and its money may do more on specific biological questions than on platforms.

On the System Map: loop R2 (proven results attract investment) is priming and loop B1 (checking can’t keep up) is turning; leverage points 1 to 3 sit on replication, open data and verification.

The theme is not a list of separate trends. Four loops connect its parts. More data, better models (R1): experiments produce data that improves the models that propose the next experiments. Checking can’t keep up (B1): more AI-made hypotheses add checking work and erode trust. Proven results attract investment (R2): replicated results build the confidence that brings more lab capacity. Data gets locked up (R3): exclusive data deals give a model lead that wins the next deal.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Fund shared validation and replication capacity, not private robots

Google DeepMind argues that experimental validation, not ideas, is now the scarce resource (Google DeepMind, Jul 2026), and the Institute for Progress says public and non-profit money should go where private start-ups will not (Institute for Progress, Jul 2026). The National Science Foundation’s cloud-lab network shows the model: a philanthropy’s more than $20 million sits alongside $380 million of public money (US National Science Foundation, Jul 2026). The trust should look for a shared validation or cloud-lab partnership in its disease areas that any grantee can use.

Action: Trustees to decide by 31 March 2027 whether to commit to a co-funded validation or cloud-lab partnership, with a named public or charity partner.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 1, verified and replicated results, where loop R2 (proven results attract investment) can be pushed.

Implication 2: Make open, standard data and code a condition of every AI-enabled grant

The self-driving lab community names proprietary formats and missing standards as its main barriers (Matter, May 2026), Biohub has committed to make its data open (Biohub, Apr 2026), and partners in the virtual-cell coalition get a year’s exclusive access before data becomes public (eWeek, Oct 2026). The trust’s grant conditions are one of the few levers it controls outright.

Action: The research team to publish data, metadata, code and replication conditions for AI-enabled grants by 30 June 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 2, open, standard data, where loop R1 (more data, better models) turns and loop R3 (data gets locked up) is held back.

Implication 3: Pay for verification and replication of AI-made results as a service

AI scientist systems showed integrity problems in 34.2% of test runs (SciIntegrity-Bench preprint, May 2026), scientists already spend a large share of the time AI saves on checking its output (Campus Technology, Sep 2026), and routine biomedical experiments replicate only 20% to 44% of the time (Brazilian Reproducibility Initiative, eLife, Jul 2026). A funded verification service would turn that hidden cost into a shared asset.

Action: Fund a pilot by the end of 2027 to replicate a set of high-value preclinical results on automated platforms, and publish the replication rate.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 3, verification load, where loop B1 (checking can’t keep up) can be eased.

Implication 4: Attach biosecurity and integrity conditions to autonomous biology the trust funds

The Congressional Research Service notes that cloud labs run experiments with minimal human participation and that the US framework for screening DNA orders has not been updated (Congressional Research Service, Sep 2026), and one AI model has already designed and run 36,000 biological experiments through a robotic cloud lab (The Conversation, via Singularity Hub, Jun 2026). China is already preparing standards for autonomous labs (Science and Technology Daily, in Chinese, Oct 2026).

Action: Adopt funding conditions by the end of 2027 on DNA-synthesis screening, human sign-off for wet-lab runs and disclosure of AI involvement in results.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 4, biosecurity and screening rules, the outside switch on what autonomous labs may run.

The Open Proving Ground

↗ Shift from todayFrom the National Science Foundation’s new network of shared cloud-lab teams (US National Science Foundation, Jul 2026) to shared networks that test and replicate results for any funded researcher.

In 2030 public and philanthropic money runs shared autonomous labs that any funded team can use, with open data standards and routine replication. Medicines move from idea to trial faster because proof is shared and trusted, and smaller funders and universities compete with the largest companies on ideas rather than on equipment.

Early indicators: Shared cloud labs open to outside users; a major funder requires replication of key preclinical results; regulators accept nonclinical data packages.

Private Engines

↗ Shift from todayFrom Roche beginning to build AI automated laboratories (Cailian Press, in Chinese, Sep 2026) to a few companies running most autonomous discovery behind closed doors.

In 2030 the largest drug and tools companies run autonomous discovery in-house and prove results quickly inside closed systems. Medicines advance faster, but data and know-how stay private, and academic and charity researchers rent access on companies’ terms. Public networks exist but are thin.

Early indicators: Big companies report autonomous-lab output in results; exclusive data deals spread; public cloud-lab budgets are cut.

Data Without Verdicts

↗ Shift from todayFrom Biohub’s commitment to open biological data (Biohub, Apr 2026) to vast shared datasets whose results few have the time or money to check.

In 2030 open data and shared labs have grown, but validation and replication lag. The field produces far more hypotheses than it can test, checking AI output eats researchers’ time, and trust in AI-made results is low. Funders that paid for capacity without proof find much of it unused or disputed.

Early indicators: Verification time keeps rising in surveys; integrity failures in AI-made papers make news; replication rates stay low.

The Hype Discount

↗ Shift from todayFrom $8.9 billion raised by AI drug-discovery companies in a single year (Clinical Trial Vanguard, Aug 2026) to closed labs producing many candidates and few approvals.

In 2030 private labs generate candidates quickly, but success at Phase II has not improved and investors discount claims about AI. Companies consolidate, public money is tighter, and charities are asked to rescue promising but unproven programmes.

Early indicators: AI-enabled Phase II success stays level with other programmes; funding rounds for AI discovery fall; consolidation among AI drug companies.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes. In October no future leads: Data Without Verdicts led in July, and the reading has since moved back to the centre on lab capacity and data while staying on the side where proof lags.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

Regulators accept an automated, animal-free data package for a first trial in people

Window: 2030 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Animal studies remain the default evidence that a new medicine is safe enough to test in people.

The US Food and Drug Administration now writes ‘nonclinical tests’ in place of ‘animal tests’ (US Food and Drug Administration, Sep 2026), and the European Commission’s roadmap says AI will play an important role, with a stock-take by 2029 (European Commission, Jun 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: the FDA’s database of non-animal methods includes a case built mainly on automated in vitro data.
  • By December 2029: the EU’s stock-take reports a pharmaceutical area where animal tests are no longer the default.

What it touches today: Which preclinical work the trust funds, and whether grants budget for human-relevant validation.

Weak signal

Replication becomes a routine, automated service that funders require

Window: 2031 to 2038 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: Replication is rarely funded, rarely published and rarely rewarded.

Only 20% to 44% of routine biomedical experiments replicated across 56 laboratories (Brazilian Reproducibility Initiative, eLife, Jul 2026), and the self-driving lab community calls replication studies a key step (Matter, May 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2028: a major biomedical funder requires independent replication of key preclinical results on shared platforms.
  • By December 2029: a leading journal accepts automated cloud-lab replication as part of peer review.

What it touches today: How the trust judges the strength of the results it funds, and whether replication appears in its budgets.

China’s standards for autonomous labs become the reference others adopt

  • By December 2027: China publishes a national standard for autonomous laboratories.
  • By December 2028: an international standards body opens work on autonomous laboratories that draws on China’s standard.

What it touches today: Which data, equipment and safety standards the trust asks its grantees to follow.

Each option is graded against all four futures and a wild card in the Scenario Stress Test: three hold in every future (co-fund a shared validation or cloud-lab node; require open data, metadata and code in AI-enabled grants; fund verification, with safety conditions), five are bets, strong in some futures and weak in others, and one backfires in at least one future (rely on existing review).

Options: Where the money goes (trustees and chief executive)

Option A · Least change

Fund projects that use AI as a tool

Keep funding investigator-led projects, letting grantees buy AI and automation as they choose.

Trade-off: Least disruption, but leaves the trust paying for speed others will fund, and buys little shared proof.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Private Engines; The Hype Discount

Option B · Middle course

Co-fund a shared validation or cloud-lab node

Join a public or charity partner to fund shared validation capacity in the trust’s disease areas.

Trade-off: A larger, longer commitment with partners’ terms to negotiate, but it buys proof every grantee can use in any future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Anchor a dedicated autonomous lab for one disease area

Fund a focused research organisation with its own autonomous lab and an open-data mandate.

Trade-off: The biggest bet: high cost and execution risk, but it could set the standard for a whole disease area.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Open Proving Ground; Data Without Verdicts

Options: Data and standards (the research and grants team)

Option A · Least change

Encourage open data

Ask grantees to share data where they can, as now.

Trade-off: No new cost, but leaves the trust’s data in formats others cannot use, and invites exclusive deals.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Private Engines

Option B · Middle course

Require open data, metadata and code in AI-enabled grants

Make open, standard data and code a condition of every grant that uses AI or automation.

Trade-off: Some grantees and partners will object, but it keeps the trust’s money building a shared asset.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Fund an open dataset and benchmark for a disease area

Pay for a field-wide, open dataset and benchmark that AI-driven labs can test against.

Trade-off: Costly and slow to show results, but it shapes what every lab in the field measures.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Open Proving Ground; Data Without Verdicts

Options: Trust and safety (governance and policy leads)

Option A · Least change

Rely on existing review

Leave integrity and safety to grantees’ institutions and current rules.

Trade-off: No new burden, but exposes the trust if an AI-made result fails or autonomous biology causes harm.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Private Engines; The Hype Discount

Option B · Middle course

Fund verification, with safety conditions

Pay for verification and replication of high-value results, and attach screening and human sign-off conditions to autonomous biology.

Trade-off: A modest cost that protects the trust’s reputation and the value of what it funds in every future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Lead a funders’ coalition on standards

Convene other medical research funders on shared rules for integrity, screening and reporting of AI-made results.

Trade-off: Demands time and influence, but turns the trust’s position into the field’s default.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Open Proving Ground; Data Without Verdicts

What We Are Not Planning For

Autonomy

AI systems replacing human scientists in medical discovery before 2030

A position paper argues current AI scientists are not built for autonomous discovery (IIT Delhi and Jena preprint, May 2026), and in Google’s own autonomous wet-lab work humans still authorise designs and supervise robot runs (Google Research, Oct 2026).

↺ Reinstated if an autonomous system completes a biomedical discovery loop without human wet-lab work, and an independent lab replicates the result.

Funding

A collapse in public money for AI-driven science before 2030

The US has committed more than $5 billion to the Genesis Mission (The White House, Jul 2026) and Japan has asked for about 6.7 times this year’s budget (Japan's science ministry, in Japanese, Sep 2026).

↺ Reinstated if the Genesis Mission lines are zeroed in final appropriations, or Japan’s FY2027 budget for AI for science falls below this year’s.

Approvals

AI raising the overall approval rate of new medicines before 2030

Industry-wide success rates are unchanged and AI-enabled programmes only match others at Phase II (IQVIA Institute, May 2026), and a review finds clinically relevant impact disappointingly limited (Timmerman Report, Aug 2026).

↺ Reinstated if AI-enabled programmes show higher Phase II success than other programmes in two successive industry reviews.
