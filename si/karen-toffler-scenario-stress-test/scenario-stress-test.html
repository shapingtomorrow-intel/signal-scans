Which Funding Moves Hold in Every Future for Autonomous Labs to 2030

26 October 2026 · Built on the Decision Intelligence briefing Where Charity Money Can Make Autonomous Labs Deliver for Medicine and its System Map · Horizons: 2030 and 2040 Uses the briefing’s scenarios and options, with evidence from the briefing; no other sources. Grades and indicator readings are drafted by analysis, to be challenged and judged by people.

Scenarios are not forecasts. Their job is to show how differently the next few years could turn out, so that a funding strategy can be built to survive any of them, and then to be watched, so that the strategy changes when the evidence does.

This page does both. It takes the nine options in the briefing and runs each one through four plausible futures to 2030, plus a wild card to 2040. A move that works in every future is robust and can be committed to now. A move that works well in some futures and poorly in others is a bet, held ready with a clear trigger. A move that makes things worse in any future is fragile and should not be relied on. It then scores leading indicators on the matrix’s two axes, shows how that reading has moved since May, and turns the reading into funding priorities.

1. Four futures to 2030, and a wild card to 2040

The two axes are the system’s two biggest uncertainties, the same two the System Map identifies: whether lab capacity and data end up shared and open or private, which is whether the data flywheel beats the enclosure loop, and whether proof keeps pace with discovery, which is whether the proof flywheel overcomes the verification brake.

Proof keeps pace · shared

The Open Proving Ground

Public and philanthropic money runs shared autonomous labs that any funded team can use, with open data standards and routine replication. Medicines move from idea to trial faster because proof is shared and trusted.

By 2040: Shared proving grounds are part of how medical research is done, and replication is a normal line in a grant budget.

Proof keeps pace · private

Private Engines

The largest drug and tools companies run autonomous discovery in-house and prove results quickly inside closed systems. Academic and charity researchers rent access on companies’ terms.

By 2040: A few companies hold most of the data and the know-how, and public networks fill the gaps they leave.

Proof lags · shared

Data Without Verdicts

Open data and shared labs have grown, but validation and replication lag. The field produces far more hypotheses than it can test, and trust in AI-made results is low.

By 2040: Funders that paid for capacity without proof are paying again to check what it produced.

Proof lags · private

The Hype Discount

Private labs generate candidates quickly, but success at Phase II has not improved and investors discount claims about AI. Charities are asked to rescue promising but unproven programmes.

By 2040: The survivors are the companies that invested in proof, and the field rebuilds trust slowly.

Wild card to 2040

Proof goes automatic

Regulators and funders accept automated, replicated laboratory evidence as standard, so a first trial in people can rest on an animal-free package and replication is a service funders require.

Built from two of the briefing’s Potential Surprises to 2040: regulators accept an automated, animal-free data package (2030 to 2036, Low-Medium) and replication becomes a routine, automated service (2031 to 2038, Low).

What the stress test shows
  1. There is a robust core, and it matches the briefing’s four Implications. Co-funding shared validation, requiring open data and code, and paying for verification with safety conditions hold up in all four futures.
  2. Relying on existing review is the one fragile move. Where open data grows faster than anyone can check it, integrity failures in AI-made results land on the funders that paid for them. The reading in section 4 has sat on the side where proof lags at every date so far.
  3. Each bet turns on one axis. Anchoring a dedicated lab, funding a benchmark and leading a coalition on standards are strong where capacity and data stay shared and weak where companies hold them. Exclusive data deals and the standards race are the signposts that settle them.

3. What it means for the plan

Commit now

Robust: works in every future.

  • Co-fund a shared validation or cloud-lab node in the trust’s disease areas (Implication 1).
  • Make open data, metadata and code a condition of every AI-enabled grant (Implication 2).
  • Fund verification and replication of high-value results, with biosecurity and integrity conditions (Implications 3 and 4).

Prepare, and act on a trigger

Bets: strong in some futures, weak in others.

  • A dedicated autonomous lab for one disease area: release it if capacity and data settle on the shared side.
  • An open dataset and benchmark: hold ready on the same trigger.
  • A funders’ coalition on standards: start it if China publishes its standard first.
  • Funding projects that use AI as a tool, and encouraging open data: acceptable only until the core is funded.

Do not rely on

Fragile: backfires in at least one future.

  • Relying on existing review for AI-made results and autonomous biology.

4. Which future is gaining momentum, and how it has moved since May

Each future is a corner of the matrix, so the question “which future is gaining momentum” becomes two questions the evidence can answer: are lab capacity and data ending up shared or private, and is proof keeping pace with discovery? Twelve leading indicators answer them, six on each axis. Scored at each reading, they give a reading on each axis and a point on the matrix; the trail of points shows where the evidence is heading.

The pale band marks readings within 15 points of an axis, where no future can lead. Hover over a point for its reading.

How the reading is scored

  1. Each indicator is marked Moving (+1), Pending (+½), Not yet (0) or Against (−1), on the evidence published by the date of the reading.
  2. Each carries a weight: 3 if it would settle its axis on its own, 2 if it is strong evidence, 1 if it supports.
  3. An axis reading is the weighted total for that axis, scaled from −100 (shared and open, or proof lagging) to +100 (private, or proof keeping pace).
  4. A future leads when the reading sits in its corner, at least 15 points from both axes, for two readings running.
  5. A shift is a move of 10 points or more on either axis between readings. It is flagged and reviewed in the next quarterly briefing.
  6. A signpost reached (section 6) overrides the score and triggers its decision directly.

7. How the monitoring runs over time

Every weekSignal Scans attach new evidence to each indicator and signpost, including weak signals that do not yet fit any future.
Every monthThe Change Tracker rescores each indicator, the two axis readings are recalculated, and the trail on the matrix gains a point.
Every quarterThe Decision Intelligence briefing reviews any flagged shift, and redraws the scenarios, the System Map and this stress test.
When a signpost is reachedAction Triggers fire, releasing the bet prepared for it and naming the decision it changes.

8. Running it with your own plan

The options above are the briefing’s. The real value comes from replacing them with the trust’s own funding priorities and asking the same question of each: does it hold in every future?

BeforeThe trust shares its research strategy and funding priorities for the next few years; we grade each against the scenarios and the evidence base.
Session, 2 hours20 minutes in the four futures; 40 minutes in which each group lives in one future and answers “it is 2030: what do we wish we had funded in 2026?”; 40 minutes testing the strategy against the matrix; 20 minutes agreeing the robust core and the trigger for each bet.
AfterThe agreed matrix, indicators and signposts join the programme, and the monitoring cycle above keeps them current.
