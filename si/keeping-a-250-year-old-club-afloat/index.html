Why This Matters Now

The club will mark its 250th year in 2087, on the same stretch of the Hamble it has occupied since the 1930s. By then the river will be higher, its storms harder and its water warmer, and the plan that decides which parts of Hamble village are defended is being rewritten now.

That plan holds the line along the Quay and Rope Walk, where the clubhouse stands, but it does not promise public money to do it. Insurers are starting to ask for storm plans before they will cover marinas. Warmer seasons bring more sailing days, but also more days when the water is not fit for young sailors.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: decide who will speak for the club’s waterfront while the Hamble’s shoreline plan is being refreshed, and what the club will ask for.

Executive Synthesis

Which decision on the clubhouse or pontoons this year assumes the Hamble will stay at today’s level until 2087?

The evidence in this briefing says planning allowances already assume a metre or more of rise, and the plan that defends the club’s frontage does not promise the money.

The club’s waterfront is held by a public plan that does not promise to pay for holding it. The shoreline plan for Hamble village leaves most of the shore to adapt naturally and holds the line only at the Quay and Rope Walk, where the clubhouse stands, while stating that a hold-the-line policy does not secure public funding (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010). Meanwhile the water is rising faster: two-thirds of the UK’s 20.1 cm rise since 1901 came in the last three decades (Met Office, Jul 2026), and warm seas raised Storm Babet’s surge by about 20% (Met Office, Jul 2026). Part of today’s position is held up by something that can turn: a soft marine insurance market with falling rates, in which insurers already make storm plans a condition of cover (Marine Industry News, Jul 2026). October’s briefing found higher seas and stricter rules moving towards the Hamble; looking to 2087 turns that into a question about the club’s own frontage.

Several things are open. Will the refreshed shoreline plan keep the Quay and Rope Walk on hold the line, and will public money follow? How fast will the sea rise: about half a metre by 2100 if emissions pledges are met (University of Bristol, Jul 2026), or towards the 1.9 metres planners are asked to test (Unda Consulting, on the Environment Agency, Jul 2026)? Will storms bring more frequent damage to the haven? And will warmer water mean more sailing days, or more days when the water is not fit to use?

Looking ahead to 2087, the Environment Agency projects seas in the western Solent over 1 metre higher by 2120 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026), winter windstorms over the UK are projected to become more frequent by 2061 to 2080 (Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership, Jun 2026), and the national climate risk assessment expects up to 35% fewer fish in the northwest Atlantic by 2100 (Plymouth Marine Laboratory, on the UK Climate Change Risk Assessment, Jun 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the club’s frontage will need much more defending within the life of what it builds now. If emissions pledges are met, the rise by 2100 is 0.4 to 0.6 metres (University of Bristol, Jul 2026), and the plan has held the line at the Quay and Rope Walk in every period it covers (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010). In that world, modest raising of existing defences may be enough.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Put the club’s frontage and its cost into the Hamble’s shoreline plan refresh. The plans are being put into action by the end of 2028, and policy is not funding.
  2. Agree a storm plan for the yacht haven, and the insurer’s terms, before the next renewal. Readiness is becoming a condition of cover.
  3. Set the height that every new waterside building and pontoon must clear. What is rebuilt now will still stand when the water is far higher.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. The public plan for the Hamble’s shoreline will decide what gets defended, and the club needs a say in it

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The Environment Agency and the Southern Coastal Group on the plan; Hampshire County Council as harbour authority; national budgets on the money · Room to act: Moderate: the club cannot set the policy, but it can take part, make its case and decide what it pays for itself

The clubhouse and yacht haven sit on Rope Walk, one of two short stretches of Hamble village where the shoreline plan says defences will be held; the rest of the village shore is left to adapt naturally. The plan is being refreshed and put into action now, and its own wording says that holding the line is not a promise of public money. Whether the club’s frontage stays defended, and who pays, will be settled in public processes the club can join or leave to others.

Sources: (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010); (Environment Agency, Jun 2026); (Environment Agency, Jun 2026). The plan describes its periods as 0 to 20, 20 to 50 and 50 to 100 years; the Environment Agency dates the current medium term from 2025 to 2055.

  • The plan holds the club’s stretch, and only that. For Satchell Marshes to Hamble Common Point it sets no active intervention, with the line held locally at the Quay and Rope Walk, in every period to about a century ahead; landowners keep their right to maintain their own defences (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010).
  • Policy is not funding. The plan states that holding the line does not mean public money is secured or guaranteed (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010), while the national programme of at least £10.5 billion to 2036 funds schemes case by case (Environment Agency, Jun 2026).
  • The plans are being rewritten now. The current medium term runs from 2025 until 2055, and the project to put the refreshed plans into action runs to the end of 2028 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026).
  • Next door, a 100-year plan shows the stakes for sailors. At Hurst Spit and Lymington the number of properties at risk rises from 35 today to 360 in 100 years (Environment Agency, Jun 2026), and the future of the moorings behind the spit at Mount Lake cannot be guaranteed, with full funding unlikely to come from central government (Yachting Monthly, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The plan asks for a study that could change Rope Walk’s status. The policy for the club’s stretch carries a note calling for more detailed study of how Hamble Point’s shoreline will evolve, to settle the longer-term management of the frontage and the river (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010). No such study was found.

Outlook to 2087

Plan: The plan’s last period runs from 2055 to about 2105, so the club’s 250th year falls in the period its policy covers least firmly (Environment Agency, Jun 2026).

Abroad: France adopted a national coastline strategy in August 2026 that plans for moving development back from the coast, but created no dedicated fund; its coastal mayors say the decree leaves lasting funding unresolved (Banque des Territoires, in French, Sep 2026). The Dutch Delta Commissioner says water spending must at least double, and that not everything can be protected everywhere (NOS, in Dutch, Sep 2026).

What it means: Treat the end of 2028 as the deadline to get the club’s frontage, and the cost of holding it, into the refreshed plan.

Counter-argument

Rope Walk has been held in every period of the plan since it was adopted (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010), and the national programme is the biggest ever (Environment Agency, Jun 2026). The hard fights over realignment are on long, lightly built shores like Lymington’s (Environment Agency, Jun 2026), not on a developed village quay. If the refresh confirms the local hold and a public scheme funds it, the club’s own role is small; but the plan itself says policy is not funding.

On the System Map: loop B1 (flooding builds the case) is priming: risk is rising and the plans are being refreshed, but no money is committed to the Hamble frontage; leverage point 1 sits on the shoreline plan.

2. Stronger storms and surges will test pontoons, moorings and the racing calendar

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The weather; insurers on terms; the club on its pontoons, moorings and calendar · Room to act: High on preparation: the club decides how its haven is built and run, and what cover it buys

The yacht haven’s pontoons and moorings face storms that hit harder at the coast when the sea is warm, and insurers are turning storm readiness into a condition of cover. For the club the risk is threefold: damage to the haven, tighter or dearer cover, and more lost race days.

  • Storm surges are a feature of every winter. The most severe surges of 2025 came from storms Éowyn in January, Amy in October and Bram in December (Met Office, Jul 2026).
  • Warm seas make storms worse at the coast. During Storm Babet, warm water raised wave power by around 9%, river flows by 12% to 18% and storm surge by about 20% (Met Office, Jul 2026).
  • The 2026 to 2027 season opened on the south coast. Storm Austen brought a warning for southern coastal counties, with gusts above 70mph possible on exposed coasts (Radio X, Oct 2026); across the Channel it knocked over yachts in the marina at Le Crouesty, and owners’ claims depend on the storm cover in their policies (France Épargne, in French, Oct 2026).
  • Insurers are changing the terms. Marine underwriters are adding named-storm deductibles and wind or flood exclusions, and say preparedness is now a prerequisite for coverage (Marine Industry News, Jul 2026); damage to pontoons from vessel impact is not always covered by standard policies (WTW, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2087

Storms: Winter windstorms over the UK are projected to become more frequent in 2061 to 2080 than in 1981 to 2000, with the heights of the most extreme waves rising (Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership, Jun 2026).

Marinas: The same review notes that smaller harbours and marinas may also feel the effects of climate change (Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership, Jun 2026).

Losses: Natural catastrophes caused more than $100 billion of insured losses in 2025, the sixth year in a row above that level (WTW, Jul 2026).

What it means: Plan the haven, its cover and the racing calendar for more storm days and insurer conditions, not for today’s weather.

Counter-argument

The 2025 surges mostly came on small or moderate tides, which limited their impact (Met Office, Jul 2026), and the broad marine insurance market is still soft, with rates falling (Marine Industry News, Jul 2026). The projected rise in storm frequency is decades away (Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership, Jun 2026). A sheltered river haven may see little change for years; the case for acting now rests on insurers’ conditions, not on the weather alone.

On the System Map: loop B2 (insurers demand storm readiness) is turning: insurers already ask for storm plans, which pushes owners to invest before a loss; leverage point 2 sits on the haven’s storm plan and cover.

3. The water at the clubhouse could rise by a metre or more before 2100

Longer-Range

Weight: high · Who decides: Global emissions on the rate; the Environment Agency on planning allowances; the club on what it builds · Room to act: Low on the rise itself, high on the height of what the club builds next

Whatever the club builds or rebuilds on its waterfront now, a clubhouse floor, a slipway, a pontoon, will meet much higher water within its working life. How much higher depends on global emissions, but the planning numbers already in use assume about a metre or more, and ask planners to test a case near two.

Sources: (Met Office, Jul 2026); (University of Bristol, Jul 2026); (Environment Agency, Jun 2026); (Unda Consulting, on the Environment Agency, Jul 2026). The sources measure from different starting years, so the bars show each estimate as stated rather than a single forecast; the western Solent bar marks “over 1 metre”.

  • The rise is speeding up. UK sea level has risen about 20.1 cm since 1901, two-thirds of it in the last three decades (Met Office, Jul 2026).
  • Planning numbers start at a metre. The Environment Agency’s allowances give roughly 1.0 to 1.6 metres of rise between 2000 and 2125, planners are asked to test 1.9 metres by 2100, and no national guidance exists beyond 2100 (Unda Consulting, on the Environment Agency, Jul 2026).
  • The Solent’s own figure is over a metre. The Environment Agency projects seas in the western Solent over 1 metre higher by 2120, on a 100-year planning horizon (Environment Agency, Jun 2026).
  • Emissions decide the size. A July study finds UK seas rising 0.4 to 0.6 metres by 2100 if current pledges are met, and 1.2 to 1.8 metres by 2300 (University of Bristol, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2087

By 2100: Some researchers argue that up to 1.6 metres by 2100 is more plausible than the central figures (Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership, Jun 2026).

Beyond: Continued sea-level rise will occur beyond 2100 (Marine Climate Change Impacts Partnership, Jun 2026), and around 1.4 million more people in the UK look set to face coastal flood risk by 2200 (University of Bristol, Jul 2026).

Abroad: The Dutch government’s sea-level programme finds its current defences could technically cope with up to 3 metres of rise, and probably 5 (Dutch government (IPLO), in Dutch, Jun 2026): holding a frontage is possible, at a price.

What it means: Set the height of anything rebuilt on the waterfront against at least a metre of rise, and check it against 1.9 metres.

Counter-argument

If emissions pledges are met, the rise by 2100 is 0.4 to 0.6 metres (University of Bristol, Jul 2026), within reach of today’s defences with modest raising, and the plan holds the line at Rope Walk (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010). The larger numbers are allowances and worst cases chosen to be safe, not central forecasts. The case for a height rule is that it costs little at the time of a rebuild and a great deal afterwards.

On the System Map: the outside switch, global emissions, sets the pace of loop B1 (flooding builds the case) and of loop B3 (what members can pay); leverage point 3 sits on the height of what the club builds.

Decision link: Strategic Implications 3 and 1.

4. Warmer seas will lengthen the sailing season but bring poorer water and new marine life

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: The climate on temperature; Southern Water and the regulators on sewage; the club on its season and water rules · Room to act: Moderate: the club sets its calendar, its youth activities and whether it tests its own water

Warmer, sunnier seasons can give members more days on the water. The same warmth brings marine heatwaves, blooms and shifts in marine life, and sewage spills keep the Solent’s water poor on many days. Whether members and young sailors can safely use the water becomes part of planning the season.

  • The seasons are warmer and sunnier. 2025 brought the UK’s warmest spring and summer on record, and its sunniest year since 1910, with 1645 hours, 117% of the average (Royal Meteorological Society, Jul 2026).
  • The sea is setting records. Northwest European seas had 297 marine heatwave days in 2025 (Royal Meteorological Society, Jul 2026), and Poole Bay reached 21.7°C on 9 July 2026, about 3 to 4.5°C above average (Cefas, Sep 2026).
  • Marine life is shifting. An octopus bloom off the south-west has sent more than 100 tonnes through Brixham’s market in a day, while scallops have almost disappeared from some bays (Plymouth Marine Laboratory, Jul 2026); across the Channel, French scientists called May’s marine heatwave the most damaging since 1960, and whelk catches in the bay of Granville have fallen 80% since 2017 (Le Figaro Nautisme, on Ifremer, in French, Jul 2026).
  • The water is not getting cleaner. Sewer overflows into the Solent spilled for 36,327 hours in 2025, close to the 35,754 hours of 2022 (Solent Protection Society, Aug 2026), and Southern Water logged 485 dry day spills from January to May 2026, against 418 in all of 2025 (ITV News Meridian, Oct 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A neighbouring club is testing the river itself. Warsash Sailing Club is testing water to bathing water standards for a health risk assessment for its members, and is considering seeking bathing water designation for Kipper Pond on the Hamble (Warsash Sailing Club, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2087

Heatwaves: Marine heatwaves that are now considered exceptional may become increasingly typical later this century (Met Office, Jul 2026), and UK seas already see around four more a year than in the 1980s (Plymouth Marine Laboratory, on the UK Climate Change Risk Assessment, Jun 2026).

Marine life: The national climate risk assessment expects a 12% average loss of fish in the northwest Atlantic by mid-century and up to 35% by 2100 (Plymouth Marine Laboratory, on the UK Climate Change Risk Assessment, Jun 2026).

What it means: Plan for a longer season with more days lost to poor water, and decide what standard the club applies before young sailors go on or in the water.

Counter-argument

Warmer seas do not by themselves cause blooms, which also depend on food, currents and predators (Plymouth Marine Laboratory, Jul 2026), so not every change can be laid at warming’s door. And the estuary is recovering in places: seagrass is growing on the Hamble for the first time in nearly 100 years (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026). If spills fall and restoration spreads, warmer seasons could bring more good days than bad.

On the System Map: no loop yet: warmer seas lengthen the season and worsen water quality at the same time, so member activity rises and falls with the water; leverage point 4 sits on the season and the water rules.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Put the club’s frontage and its cost into the Hamble’s shoreline plan refresh

The plan holds the line at the Quay and Rope Walk but says that policy does not secure public funding (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010), and the refreshed plans are being put into action by the end of 2028 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026). The club should know what its own defences need, register as a voice in the refresh, and ask for the local hold, and the money for it, to be written in.

Action: Flag officers to commission a study of the club’s defences on Rope Walk and the yacht haven against a metre of rise, and register the club with the Environment Agency and the harbour authority as a consultee in the shoreline plan refresh, by 30 June 2027.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 3. On the System Map: leverage point 1, the shoreline plan, where loop B1 (flooding builds the case) either reaches the club’s frontage or passes it by.

Implication 2: Agree a storm plan for the yacht haven, and the insurer’s terms, before the next renewal

Warm seas raised Storm Babet’s surge by about 20% (Met Office, Jul 2026), and marine insurers now say preparedness is a prerequisite for coverage, with named-storm deductibles (Marine Industry News, Jul 2026). The haven should have a written storm plan and know, in writing, what its cover pays for damage to pontoons and moorings.

Action: Yacht haven management to write a storm plan for the pontoons and moorings and obtain the insurer’s written terms on storm deductibles and pontoon damage, before the 2027 renewal, by 31 March 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 2. On the System Map: leverage point 2, the haven’s storm plan and cover, which keeps loop B2 (insurers demand storm readiness) working in the club’s favour.

Implication 3: Set the height that every new waterside building and pontoon must clear

Planning allowances give roughly 1.0 to 1.6 metres of rise between 2000 and 2125, with 1.9 metres by 2100 as the case to test (Unda Consulting, on the Environment Agency, Jul 2026), and the western Solent is projected over 1 metre higher by 2120 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026). A height rule agreed now costs little; a building that sits too low costs a great deal.

Action: The Executive Committee to adopt a design rule that nothing rebuilt on the waterfront sits below a level allowing for a metre of rise, checked against 1.9 metres, by 31 December 2027.

Prepare Draws on Themes 3 and 1. On the System Map: leverage point 3, the height of what the club builds, which limits how hard loop B3 (what members can pay) bites later.

Implication 4: Watch sea temperature and water quality, and set a rule for when youth sessions move

Poole Bay reached 21.7°C in July, about 3 to 4.5°C above average (Cefas, Sep 2026), and Southern Water logged 485 dry day spills from January to May (ITV News Meridian, Oct 2026). The sailing committees should track both each season and agree in advance when sessions move or stop.

Action: The sailing and youth committees to review sea temperature and water quality data each spring from 2027, with a written rule for moving or stopping youth sessions on the water.

Monitor Draws on Themes 4 and 2. On the System Map: leverage point 4, the season and the water rules, where warmer seasons and poorer water act on members’ time on the water.

The Walled Quay

↗ Shift from todayFrom a plan that holds the Quay and Rope Walk without promising the money (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010) to a funded public scheme along the village waterfront.

In 2087 Hamble village sits behind higher public defences along the Quay and Rope Walk, built in stages as the water rose. The club celebrates its 250th year where it has stood for a century and a half, its haven protected by a wall it helped to argue for but did not pay for. The shore beyond the village has been left to the river, and saltmarsh has returned there.

Early indicators: The refreshed plan names a scheme for the Quay and Rope Walk; national budgets keep rising; the club is a named consultee.

The Club’s Own Wall

↗ Shift from todayFrom landowners’ right to maintain their own defences (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010) to members paying to hold a frontage facing seas over 1 metre higher (Environment Agency, Jun 2026).

In 2087 the policy still says hold the line, but public money went elsewhere, and the club and its neighbours have raised and maintained their own defences. The clubhouse stands where it always has, at a cost carried in members’ fees and appeals. Storm-ready pontoons and strict insurer terms are part of club life, and membership is smaller and wealthier.

Early indicators: Bids for a Hamble scheme fail; insurers attach storm conditions; the club builds a defence reserve.

The Planned Step Back

↗ Shift from todayFrom 35 properties at risk at Lymington today rising to 360 in 100 years (Environment Agency, Jun 2026) to managed realignment reaching the Hamble with public help.

In 2087 public money pays to move rather than to hold. Parts of the Hamble’s waterfront have been given back to the river as saltmarsh, and the club’s buildings have been raised or rebuilt further back with public support. The haven floats on longer piles. The club marks its 250th year on a reshaped site, as it did when it moved from Southampton in the 1930s.

Early indicators: The refresh changes the policy for the club’s stretch; a Hamble Point study recommends realignment; adaptation funding grows.

The Floating Club

↗ Shift from todayFrom storm cover that is becoming conditional on preparedness (Marine Industry News, Jul 2026) to a club whose buildings float or stand above the 2087 water at its own expense.

In 2087 neither a public wall nor public help arrived. The club has adapted on its own: a raised or floating clubhouse, pontoons built for higher water and harder storms, and a smaller footprint on land. It is leaner and has spent heavily, but it has kept its place on the river, possibly on a different site.

Early indicators: No public scheme is named; storm deductibles rise; the club adopts a height rule and builds to it.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes. In November the reading leans towards the club paying for its waterfront, and sits near the centre on whether it is held or moved back.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

The refreshed plan stops holding the line at Rope Walk

Window: 2029 to 2035 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: The club’s frontage has been held in every period of the plan, and members assume it always will be.

The plan itself calls for a more detailed study of Hamble Point’s shoreline before the frontage’s longer-term management is settled (Environment Agency, North Solent Shoreline Management Plan, Dec 2010), and next door the future of the moorings behind Hurst Spit cannot be guaranteed (Yachting Monthly, May 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2028: a study of Hamble Point’s shoreline or the River Hamble frontage is commissioned or published; evidence: Hampshire County Council or the Southern Coastal Group.
  • By December 2028: the refreshed North Solent plan changes the policy for Satchell Marshes to Hamble Common Point; evidence: the Environment Agency’s Shoreline Management Plan Explorer.

What it touches today: Any spending on the clubhouse, slipways or haven that assumes the frontage will always be defended.

Emerging

Insurers stop covering storm damage to Solent pontoons as standard

Window: 2031 to 2038 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: Storm cover for a sheltered river haven is treated as always available, at a price.

Marine underwriters are adding named-storm deductibles and wind or flood exclusions (Marine Industry News, Jul 2026), and pontoon damage from vessel impact is not always covered by standard policies (WTW, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a UK marina insurer or The Yacht Harbour Association introduces or recommends named-storm deductibles for south coast marinas; evidence: the insurer’s or association’s published terms.
  • By December 2028: a Solent marina reports storm cover refused or excluded at renewal; evidence: the yachting or insurance press.

What it touches today: The yacht haven’s insurance renewal and the club’s reserves for uninsured damage.

Weak signal

A summer bloom closes the Solent to swimming and dinghy sailing for weeks

Window: 2030 to 2037 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: The Solent’s water problems are seen as short spills after rain, not as weeks of warm, stagnant water.

Poole Bay reached 21.7°C in July 2026, about 3 to 4.5°C above average (Cefas, Sep 2026), and blooms off the south-west are already large enough to strand on beaches (Plymouth Marine Laboratory, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By September 2028: the Environment Agency or the UK Health Security Agency warns against water contact in part of the Solent because of an algal bloom; evidence: the agency’s published warning.
  • By September 2029: a Solent regatta or youth event cancels or curtails sessions because of water quality; evidence: the organiser’s notice.

What it touches today: The summer youth programme and the club’s duty of care to young sailors in the water.
