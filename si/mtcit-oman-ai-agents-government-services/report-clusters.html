Key risks

  • Citizens whose cases an automated system gets wrong lose money and trust when no fast review route exists. Australia’s automated Robodebt process unlawfully asserted at least A$1.763 billion of debts against about 433,000 people (Federal Court of Australia, Jun 2026). See Theme 1 →

Why This Matters Now

Governments are starting to let AI do things, not just answer questions: file the form, check the case, issue the permit. The question for every digital government is no longer whether to use AI, but which tasks it may complete on the state’s behalf.

Oman’s neighbours have set dates for this, and Estonia is writing the law for it. Oman already has a single portal for government services, its own Arabic language model and a new special zone for AI firms. What it does not yet have is enough services joined up behind the screen for an AI to finish them, or agreed rules for when a machine may decide.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: choose the first services an AI may complete, decide which decisions stay with officials, and set how mistakes will be caught and put right, before the agents go live. Watch how quickly the UAE’s first agents scale, and whether they stumble.

Executive Synthesis

Which digital plans on your desk this week assume the hard part of AI in government is the model?

The evidence here says the hard part is joined-up services, a legal basis and catching errors.

AI in government is moving from answering questions to completing tasks, and the model is no longer what holds it back. The UAE has given itself two years from May 2026 to move 50% of government services and operations to agentic AI (Emirates News Agency, Oct 2026), and Estonia’s bill on automated decisions passed its first reading on 6 May 2026 (Estonian justice ministry, in Estonian, May 2026). Oman’s portal drew more than 285,000 users in its first year (Times of Oman, Mar 2026), but an agent can finish only a service joined up behind the screen, and most listings are not yet.

Some of this is settled: Oman has a national model built from more than 3,000 local datasets (Times of Oman, Jul 2026) and an AI Special Zone set up by royal decree (Royal Decree 50/2026, Apr 2026). Three things are not. Which decisions may an agent make without an official? How will errors be caught, when government testing put the UK’s chatbot at around 90% accurate (Tax Adviser magazine, Jul 2026)? And whose models and firms will build Oman’s agents?

Looking ahead to 2030, Gartner expects at least 80% of governments to deploy AI agents to automate routine decisions by 2028 (Gartner, Mar 2026), Estonia’s draft would allow automated decisions in proceedings starting from 1 July 2027 (Estonian draft act, in Estonian, Feb 2026), and Oman’s 2026 to 2030 programme targets a RO1.5 billion contribution to GDP (Oman Observer, Oct 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that rules and joined-up services must come before scale. The UAE is testing the opposite, with four agents live within days of a retreat of more than 400 ministers and officials (Khaleej Times, May 2026). If its agents run at scale by 2028 without a visible failure, sequencing will have cost Oman time and investors for little gain.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Draw the line on automated decisions. Say which an agent may assist, complete with consent, or decide.
  2. Join up the first services. Rank listed services by readiness for agents.
  3. Set how errors are caught. Agree accuracy measures, a named reviewer and an appeal route.
  4. Link demand to the AI Special Zone. Give zone firms named government services to build for.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Citizens may arrive with their own agents. Israel’s National Digital Agency is building an architecture in which agents run by national government, local government and citizens themselves could interact (OECD working group, Sep 2026). Outlook to 2030

Adoption: Gartner expects at least 80% of governments to deploy AI agents to automate routine decisions by 2028, and 70% of agencies to require explainable AI and human-in-the-loop checks by 2029 (Gartner, Mar 2026).

Rules: Estonia’s draft would apply automated decisions to proceedings beginning on or after 1 July 2027, and never to appeals (Estonian draft act, in Estonian, Feb 2026).

Investment: Oman’s 2026 to 2030 programme targets RO300 million in technology investment and 3,000 direct jobs a year (Oman Observer, Oct 2026).

Precedent: Australia’s automated Robodebt process (2015 to 2019) asserted at least A$1.763 billion of debts without a proper legal basis, and redress was still being approved in 2026 (Federal Court of Australia, Jun 2026). Oman can set appeal rules first.

What it means: on this briefing’s reading, agents will complete routine Gulf services by 2030; settle in 2027 which decisions they may take and how errors are put right, so Oman can scale without a reversal.

Counter-argument

Speed may matter more than sequence. The UAE put four agents to work within days of a retreat of more than 400 ministers and officials (Khaleej Times, May 2026), AI made up 66% of Saudi government projects in emerging technologies (Al Watan, in Arabic, Aug 2026), and users already rate Oman’s portal services highly (Times of Oman, Mar 2026). The test of this theme is whether rules and joining up written first slow Oman by more than the errors they prevent.

On the System Map: loop B1 (errors bring rules) is turning abroad, while loops R1 (use pays for joining up), R2 (public demand builds the zone) and B2 (mistakes cost trust and use) are priming in Oman; leverage points 1 to 4 sit on the rules for automated decisions, joined-up services, accuracy measures and government demand.

The theme runs through four loops. Use pays for joining up (R1): joined-up services let agents finish tasks, and use builds the case to join up more. Public demand builds the zone (R2): agents in government create demand that draws investment into the AI Special Zone and strengthens the national model. Two loops push back: errors bring rules (B1), which limit what agents may do, and mistakes cost trust and use (B2), which slows everything else.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Decide which decisions AI agents may complete, and give each tier a legal basis

Neighbours are moving to agents that act (Emirates News Agency, Oct 2026), and Estonia is legislating for automated decisions while keeping appeals for people (Estonian draft act, in Estonian, Feb 2026). A tiered list (assist, complete with consent, or decide) with the legal basis for each lets every ministry build to one line instead of negotiating it project by project.

Action: MTCIT, with the Ministry of Justice and Legal Affairs, to publish a tiered list of automated decisions and the legal basis for each by 30 June 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 1, rules for automated decisions, where loop B1 (errors bring rules) is set in advance rather than after a failure.

Implication 2: Join up the services agents will need before scaling agents

The portal lists more than 3,200 services, of which 80 are integrated through one platform (Times of Oman, Mar 2026), and the World Economic Forum advises sequencing agentic AI from high-readiness functions first (World Economic Forum, Apr 2026). Joining up the highest-volume services first decides how much of the portal agents can ever reach.

Action: Digital transformation teams to rank listed services by readiness for agents and join up the first 50 by the end of 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 2, services joined up end to end, the step loop R1 (use pays for joining up) needs to turn.

Implication 3: Set accuracy measures, a named reviewer and an appeal route before any agent goes live

US auditors found a government chat service expanded without performance measures (US tax inspector general, Jun 2026), and only 10 of 36 OECD countries report any impact measurement of government AI (OECD Digital Government Outlook, Jun 2026). An error target, a named reviewer and an appeal route catch mistakes before they become a pattern.

Action: Each delivering ministry to set an error target, a reviewer and an appeal route for every agent service, checked by MTCIT before launch, from the first pilot in 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 3, accuracy measures and human review, which weaken loop B2 (mistakes cost trust and use).

Implication 4: Use government demand to anchor the AI Special Zone and the national model

The AI Special Zone is developed in coordination with MTCIT (Royal Decree 50/2026, Apr 2026), Mu’een already serves government staff (Times of Oman, May 2026), and the UAE’s lab lets ministries develop and test agent services with partners (Emirates News Agency, Oct 2026). Naming the first services zone firms may build for gives investors a buyer and the model real work.

Action: MTCIT and the Public Authority for Special Economic Zones and Free Zones to open an agent lab with zone tenants on three priority services, using Mu’een where it fits, in the first half of 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 4, government demand for agent services, which starts loop R2 (public demand builds the zone).

Rank listed services by readiness for agents and integrate the top ones.

Trade-off: Needs ministries to share data, but works in every future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Rebuild services around life events

Bundle services around events such as a birth or a new business, delivered without being asked.

Trade-off: The biggest gain for citizens, but a long rebuild.

Buy global assistants off the shelf
