Key risks

Key opportunities

Why This Matters Now

Denmark is buying pieces of digital sovereignty: a supercomputer under Danish control, a share of Europe’s new AI factories and a plan to depend less on a few suppliers. But the models, the chips and the terms on which they are supplied still come from a handful of American companies and from Washington.

Europe is answering with graded rules rather than bans. Sweden wants a separate classified cloud for its armed forces, Norway is mapping every dependency before it decides, the Netherlands is building its own government cloud, and Germany runs American technology in sealed rooms staffed by its own people. NATO is building one transatlantic cloud and says nothing about sovereignty.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: decide which layers of the AI stack Denmark’s defence research community will own, share with Nordic partners, or rent on conditions.

Executive Synthesis

Which plans on your desk assume that a supercomputer standing in Denmark makes Denmark’s defence AI sovereign?

The evidence in this briefing says the models, the chips and the terms still come from abroad, and the terms can change.

Denmark is buying sovereignty in pieces, and the keys have not moved. Danish Defence’s AI centre gets Gefion, under sovereign Danish control according to the Defence Command (Danish Defence Command, June 2026, Danish), and the government has pledged up to DKK 750 million for EU gigafactory capacity (Danish Ministry of Research, Education and Digitalisation, July 2026, Danish). Yet three non-EU hyperscalers control over 70% of the European cloud market (European Commission, Cloud and AI Development Act proposal, June 2026), Microsoft France could not guarantee under oath that data would never be handed over on a US order (French Senate inquiry into public procurement, June 2025, French), Microsoft has cut services to a defence customer (Microsoft, September 2025), and Washington now curates allied access to American AI (US Department of Commerce, March 2026).

Settled: Denmark will neither build frontier AI alone nor be cut off by its closest ally. Not settled: where classified defence AI will run, with the Defence Minister keeping the door open to Palantir (Altinget, June 2026, Danish), and whether the Nordics end up with one classified cloud or four (SVT, April 2026, Swedish).

Looking ahead to 2030, LUMI-AI enters operation in the second half of 2027 (Sigma2, September 2026), the EU gigafactories are expected to begin operations within 18 months of selection in early 2027 (EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, July 2026), and NATO is working towards an alliance-wide cloud by 2030 (AFCEA Signal, June 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 8 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

This reading assumes US terms for supplying AI to European defence customers stay conditional. If by the end of 2027 the US codifies an allied licence regime naming EU states and a hyperscaler accepts a court-tested duty to refuse foreign orders, Denmark should simply buy American technology in sealed rooms, as the Bundeswehr does (Bundeswehr, June 2025, German).

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Choose which layers of the AI stack the fifteen institutions will own, share or rent. Gefion, the gigafactory offtake and European preference are three answers to one question.
  2. Give the universities and RTOs an assurance and exit remit. Germany’s criteria let buyers measure a service’s sovereignty level (Germany’s Federal Office for Information Security, April 2026, German).
  3. Put Washington’s export terms on the watch list. The old chip-diffusion rule is unrescinded and unreplaced (Miller & Chevalier, August 2026).

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Denmark is buying sovereign compute in pieces while the keys to frontier AI stay with US firms and Washington

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Copenhagen on what runs where; the EU on assurance rules; Washington and the hyperscalers on access terms · Room to act: Wide on what Denmark builds and buys; narrow on the terms set abroad

For the National Defence Technology Centre, the next three years are less about whether Denmark can own a computer and more about what sits on it, who can switch it off and who writes the rules of admission. Denmark has moved faster than most small states: a supercomputer under sovereign Danish control, a five-year offtake of European gigafactory capacity and a funded plan to depend less on a few suppliers (Danish Defence Command, June 2026, Danish; Danish Ministry of Research, Education and Digitalisation, July 2026, Danish; Danish Ministry of Digital Affairs, February 2026, Danish). Yet the government concedes that American companies dominate cloud in Europe (Danish Ministry of Digital Affairs, February 2026, Danish), and the Defence Minister will not rule out Palantir (Altinget, June 2026, Danish). The dilemma is not whether to depend, but on what terms.

How six European governments and the EU are answering cloud and AI dependence, 2026

Sources: the government, parliamentary and Commission documents in the Source Confidence Register (2025 to 2026). Filled: adopted or announced; outlined: proposed or recommended.

  • The hyperscalers’ promises stop at the courtroom door: under oath, Microsoft France could not guarantee that data would never be transmitted on a US government order (French Senate inquiry into public procurement, June 2025, French); Microsoft’s later commitment is to contest such orders, not refuse them (Microsoft, April 2026).
  • Service continuity is conditional on the vendor’s own policy: Microsoft ceased and disabled services to a unit within the Israel Ministry of Defense (Microsoft, September 2025), and France’s parliamentary inquiry found the US CLOUD Act reaches data not hosted in the United States (French National Assembly inquiry into digital dependencies, July 2026, French).
  • Washington is curating allied access: from 1 April 2026 US consortia could propose full-stack AI export packages for trusted foreign buyers (US Department of Commerce, March 2026), while the AI Diffusion Rule has yet to be formally rescinded (Miller & Chevalier, August 2026).
  • Europe is grading, not banning: the Commission’s proposal sets four assurance levels for public buyers (European Commission, Cloud and AI Development Act proposal, June 2026), Germany published criteria for a service’s sovereignty level (Germany’s Federal Office for Information Security, April 2026, German), and the Bundeswehr is installing Google cloud instances with no connection to the Google network, due by the end of 2027 (Bundeswehr, June 2025, German).

Outlook to 2030

Shared European capacity arrives in stages, and the rules are written first. LUMI-AI enters operation in the second half of 2027 with ten times today’s capacity (Sigma2, September 2026), up to seven EU gigafactories begin operations within 18 months of selection in early 2027 (EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, July 2026), and NATO works to an alliance-wide cloud by 2030 (AFCEA Signal, June 2026).

What it means: the Centre has until about 2028 to decide which workloads go to Gefion, which to Nordic and EU capacity and which stay on vendor clouds.

Counter-argument

The strongest challenge is that sovereignty costs more than it protects: by the Commission’s own estimate, migrating the public sector to sovereign services could cost up to €86 billion (Bruegel, June 2026), member states already hold agreements worth at least €10.8 billion with the US cloud giants (Centre for European Reform, July 2026), and NATO itself is building a transatlantic warfighting cloud (NATO Ankara Summit Declaration, July 2026). On that reading a small state should buy American technology in sealed rooms. This briefing’s reading is that conditions are only as good as the people who can enforce them.

The Centre should bring the Defence Command, the ministry and the first-ring institutions to one decision by mid-2027: which workloads run on Danish compute, which on Nordic and EU capacity, and which on vendor clouds under conditions. Danish Machines, American Models

↗ Shift from todayDanish defence AI moves onto Gefion and the gigafactory share, today a pledge of up to DKK 750 million, while the models on them stay licensed from US firms.

By 2030 Denmark’s defence AI runs on Danish and Nordic compute, and research means adapting foreign models to Danish data. Sovereignty is real for data and hardware, nominal for everything that thinks.

Early indicators: Gefion used mainly to fine-tune US models; multi-year licences with US AI vendors.

A Nordic-European Stack

↗ Shift from todayFrom today’s separate Swedish, Norwegian and Danish plans, the Nordics share a classified cloud and buy models under EU assurance levels rather than vendor contracts.

By 2030 the Nordics run one classified cloud for defence AI, drawing on LUMI-AI and the gigafactories, and buy foreign models only at EU assurance levels. Danish RTOs supply the testing know-how. Costs are higher, but the terms are written in Europe.

Early indicators: A Nordic ministers’ statement on shared classified compute; a Danish RTO accredited to assess cloud sovereignty.

Inside the Alliance Cloud

↗ Shift from todayNATO’s transatlantic warfighting cloud, today a declaration, becomes the place where Danish defence AI actually runs, on US vendor terms.

By 2030 Danish Defence does most of its AI work inside NATO’s shared cloud, supplied largely by US hyperscalers; Gefion serves research. Denmark’s leverage is the alliance’s: considerable while Washington stays engaged, negligible if not.

Early indicators: NATO cloud contracts awarded to US hyperscalers; Danish classified workloads migrated to it.

European Rules, Allied Machines

↗ Shift from todayAllied and vendor clouds still host Danish defence AI, but every service has passed a sovereignty assessment, moving from today’s proposal to binding practice.

By 2030 Denmark runs defence AI on certified allied and vendor clouds in sealed instances. The rules are European, the machines American. If Washington narrows the trusted-buyer list, the certified services are the ones Denmark cannot replace.

Early indicators: Danish Defence contracts specifying assurance levels; a US sovereign-cloud offer certified at a high EU level.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

A US cloud company switches off a Danish defence unit under its own terms of service

Window: 2029 to 2036 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: Danish planning assumes an ally’s companies will keep serving an ally’s armed forces.

Microsoft has already ceased and disabled services to a unit within the Israel Ministry of Defense (Microsoft, September 2025); its European commitment is to contest government orders, not refuse them (Microsoft, April 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a second hyperscaler publishes terms restricting specified military uses by any government customer.
  • By December 2028: a European defence ministry reports a service withdrawn by a US vendor on policy grounds.

What it touches today: Every AI contract signed without a tested exit route.

Emerging

Washington makes allied access to frontier AI conditional on buying the whole American stack

Window: 2029 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: European plans assume models and chips can be bought piecemeal.

The US export programme turns full-stack packages into the US Government’s offerings to allies, for trusted foreign buyers (US Department of Commerce, March 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2027: the US publishes the first approved full-stack export packages and the criteria for trusted buyers.
  • By December 2027: a European government reports that chip or model licences were tied to taking a bundled package.

What it touches today: Whether Gefion is designed to run American models or without them.

Emerging

Denmark’s classified defence AI runs on a jointly owned Nordic cloud rather than a national one

Window: 2031 to 2038 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Classified systems have always been national, and each Nordic state plans its own.

Sweden is sketching a national classified cloud (SVT, April 2026, Swedish), and Norway already buys pooled LUMI-AI capacity in Finland (Sigma2, September 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: Nordic defence ministers reference shared classified computing in a joint statement.
  • By December 2028: Sweden’s classified cloud procurement includes an option for partner-nation use.

What it touches today: Whether Denmark designs classified AI for one user or four.

What We Are Not Planning For

Alliance politics

A US embargo on AI exports to Denmark

NATO allies at Ankara committed to eliminate defence trade barriers among themselves (NATO Ankara Summit Declaration, July 2026); Washington’s export programme exists to supply allies (US Department of Commerce, March 2026).

↺ Reinstated if a Danish institution is refused a US chip or model licence, or Denmark is left off a published trusted-buyer list.

European capacity

The EU gigafactory programme failing outright

The procurement expects more than €20 billion of private investment and selects in early 2027 (EuroHPC Joint Undertaking, July 2026); LUMI-AI is funded and dated (Sigma2, September 2026).

↺ Reinstated if fewer than four gigafactories are selected by mid-2027, or Denmark’s chosen facility loses its consortium.

Supplier origin

Chinese models entering Danish defence systems

The Defence Intelligence Service treats China as the technology-dependence risk (Danish Defence Intelligence Service, December 2025), and only 2% of AI-using German companies use DeepSeek (Bitkom, September 2026, German).

↺ Reinstated if a Danish ministry or RTO approves a Chinese-origin model for sensitive public use.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle’s evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If Gefion is Danish but the models on it are American, which do we mean when we call Danish defence AI sovereign?
  2. Would we rather pay more for a Nordic classified cloud whose terms we help set, or less for allied clouds whose terms we accept?
  3. Do we know what Danish Defence and the fifteen institutions actually depend on, and who would compile the list?
  4. The Palantir relationship surfaced through invoices rather than policy: what conditions should every foreign AI contract carry, and who checks them?
  5. Which of our current activities looks least wise in these futures?
