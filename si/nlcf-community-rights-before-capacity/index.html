Why This Matters Now

Communities in England now have a legal right to step in when a valued local building comes up for sale, and long-dated pots of money that residents themselves control. The difficulty is that the rights and the money are arriving faster than the local organisations able to use them.

Three forces are in play at once. A new devolution settlement hands rights downward while merging councils upward. Funding has turned from grants for projects towards endowments for places. And a new set of digital and deliberative tools is making it far easier to gather what people think than to act on what they say.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: what decides whether these rights get used is whether a neighbourhood has a capable local body ready on the day an asset comes up for sale. Ask how many of the places the Fund backs could name theirs.

Executive Synthesis

Which places on your grant list could name the body that would step in if the local pub went up for sale?

The evidence in this briefing says far fewer than the new law assumes.

England has just given communities a statutory right of first refusal over local assets, and most neighbourhoods are not yet equipped to use it. The English Devolution and Community Empowerment Act 2026 received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026 (legislation.gov.uk, April 2026) and creates a Community Right to Buy that gives local people "the first right of refusal for valued community assets such as shops and community centres when they are put up for sale" (GOV.UK, April 2026). Power to Change and Locality place "up to 190,000 assets" within scope of that right (Power to Change, July 2026). The appetite is there: 59% of people aged 16 and over in England now say it is important to be able to influence decisions affecting their local area, up from 50% a year earlier, while only 26% agree that they actually can (Community and Engagement Survey, July 2026). The gap between wanting a say and believing you have one widened over the year in which the rights were legislated.

The rights: the community measures are genuine, and an independent reading of the statute records that under the Act "local authorities will be required to establish effective neighbourhood governance arrangements" (UK Constitutional Law Association, May 2026), a duty the same reading notes the Act leaves largely unspecified, and one that sits inside a settlement organised around strategic authorities and mayors. The money: funding has begun to run from project grants into place endowments, with the £175 million Community Wealth Fund allocating "between £1 million and £2.5 million to each beneficiary community" over ten years, half of it the Fund's own money (The National Lottery Community Fund, September 2025), while Great British Energy states that "by 2030, every community in the UK will have the opportunity to benefit from, and own, a local energy project" (Great British Energy, evergreen). The delivery layer: the councils that community groups negotiate with are being rebuilt at the same time, with 21 invited areas submitting "over 60 plans" and new unitary authorities assuming full legal powers on 1 April 2028 (House of Commons Library, July 2026).

The uncomfortable finding this cycle is that the long-running experiment in building neighbourhood capacity has just ended. Big Local closed across 150 areas by March 2026, leaving "at least 123 organisations" and 60 community assets in resident hands (Local Trust, May 2026). That is a real legacy and a small one beside the opportunity now in law. The right to buy is a stock, the ability to buy is a flow, and nobody is funding the flow at the scale the stock implies.

Where this analysis could be wrong

This briefing assumes that organisational readiness, rather than money or appetite, is what will limit the exercise of the new rights. The strongest evidence against that reading is that the Community Ownership Fund was "oversubscribed by more than 12 times" (Power to Change, July 2026), which suggests ready groups already exist in numbers and were turned away for want of capital rather than capability. Formal volunteering also rose in 2025/26 (Community and Engagement Survey, August 2026), which is not the shape of a collapsing volunteer base. The reading here would be materially wrong if the first two years of Community Right to Buy attempts show completion rates above roughly one in three, with failures clustering on finance rather than on governance, valuation or insurance. Two years of transaction data, which does not yet exist, would settle it.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Fund readiness, not only projects. The right of first refusal is worthless to a neighbourhood that cannot assemble a board, a valuation and a lender inside the statutory window.
  2. Sequence the England portfolio around vesting day on 1 April 2028. Every relationship a funded group holds with a district council is a relationship with a body that will not exist in its present form.
  3. Decide now what the Fund will be able to say about who decided. Resident-controlled allocation is already committed for ten years through the Community Wealth Fund, and the evidence question it answers has to be specified before the money is spent, not after.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

1. Community rights are arriving faster than the capacity to exercise them

Immediate

The Fund's England portfolio now sits against an opportunity it did not create and cannot meet at current shape. A statutory right of first refusal over community assets exists, the addressable estate runs to six figures, and the organised local capacity able to use that right is concentrated in the places that already had it. The sector that would hold these assets is overwhelmingly small, with "there are 165,758 voluntary organisations in the UK, of which 96% are small charities" (House of Commons Library, March 2026), which is the scale at which valuation, borrowing and insurance decisions have to be made. The immediate consequence is that the Fund's traditional unit of decision, the project grant, is a poor instrument for the moment a building comes up for sale on a statutory clock. What this theme forces the Fund to confront is whether it treats readiness to hold an asset as a fundable outcome in its own right, and whether it can say, place by place, who is ready.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal Participation has entered the audit function of the state. France's supreme audit institution closed its 2026 citizen campaign on 22 June with 1,000 contributions, and "Les enquêtes seront ensuite réalisées à partir de 2027" (Acteurs Publics, July 2026, French). This gains weight if a UK audit or inspection body opens its work programme to public nomination.
  • Weak signal AI has reached the plumbing of participation rather than its front end. The OECD "builds on desk research and analysis of 50 AI use cases in citizen participation processes from 22 OECD Member and partner countries" (OECD, June 2026), while Carnegie argues that "The most important near-term investments needed, therefore, are not in AI capability development but in democratic governance infrastructure" (Carnegie Endowment, May 2026). This gains weight if a UK funder or council adopts machine-synthesised deliberation as the formal record of a consultation.

Counter-argument

The strongest challenge to this reading is that the capacity base is improving, not thinning. Formal volunteering rose in 2025/26, with "Engagement has risen in 2025/26 for formal volunteering at least once a month (18%) and at least once in the last 12 months (31%)" (Community and Engagement Survey, August 2026), and the share of people who believe they can influence local decisions rose 3 points in the same year. The same release also cuts against a simple equity reading: "people living in the most deprived areas (1st decile, 28%) were more likely to agree that they can personally influence decisions affecting their local area than those living in the least deprived areas (9th decile, 25%)". On that reading the Community Ownership Fund being "oversubscribed by more than 12 times" (Power to Change, July 2026) is evidence of ready groups queuing for capital, and the binding constraint is money rather than capability. This briefing's reading is that both are binding, and that capital without readiness produces failed purchases rather than community ownership, but the point is genuinely open until transaction data exists.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's evidence into the 2027-28 England funding plan. Each names the move, an owner, a horizon and a decision posture.

SI 1: Fund readiness to hold an asset as a product in its own right

The England leadership team should carve a named share of the England portfolio into pre-transaction readiness: governance, valuation, insurance, borrowing capability and the legal work a community body needs before a statutory clock starts. The case for acting this cycle rather than next is that the right is already live, and a neighbourhood that misses a first refusal window does not get a second one for that asset. Power to Change and Locality put "up to 190,000 assets" in scope (Power to Change, July 2026), against a Big Local legacy of 60 community assets across 150 funded areas (Local Trust, May 2026). Power to Change and Locality ask government, funders and investors for "a £1 billion community assets fund" (Power to Change, July 2026), and readiness is the half of that ask a funder can move on without waiting for a spending review. The cost of not acting is that the new rights are exercised mainly where capability already exists, and the Fund's money follows rather than corrects that pattern.

Action: By the 2027-28 England funding plan, the England leadership team commits a defined share of the portfolio to pre-transaction readiness, with eligibility open to places rather than to projects.

Decide Draws on Theme 1

SI 2: Sequence England funding around vesting day on 1 April 2028

Programme teams should treat the reorganisation timetable as a hard planning constraint rather than background noise. Twenty-one invited areas submitted "over 60 plans", first elections fall in May 2027, and new unitary authorities "assume full legal powers on 1 April 2028" (House of Commons Library, July 2026). Every asset transfer, lease and partnership a funded group holds with a district council is a relationship with a body whose successor is not yet named, and asset registers, covenants and disposal decisions all move with it. Grants that land in the eighteen months either side of vesting day should carry an explicit continuity condition rather than assume the counterparty survives.

Action: By March 2027, programme teams flag every England grant whose delivery depends on a district-council counterparty and add a continuity condition ahead of the May 2027 elections.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1

SI 3: Specify now what the Fund will be able to prove about resident-led allocation

The Fund is running a large live experiment in resident-controlled allocation, with the £175 million Community Wealth Fund putting "between £1 million and £2.5 million" into each beneficiary community over ten years (The National Lottery Community Fund, September 2025). The evidence question, whether residents allocating money produce different and better decisions than a funder allocating it, cannot be answered retrospectively from grant records. Impact and evaluation leads should fix the comparison, the counterfactual and the data collection before the second wave of allocations, because the alternative is a decade of spend with a story rather than a finding at the end of it.

Action: Before the next Community Wealth Fund allocation wave, the evaluation lead publishes the comparison design and the decision-quality measures the Fund will report against.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1

SI 4: Track AI-mediated participation without adopting it yet

Policy and evidence teams should watch, and not yet buy, the tools now entering participation practice. The OECD has analysed "50 AI use cases in citizen participation processes from 22 OECD Member and partner countries" (OECD, June 2026), and the most useful available reading is that "The most important near-term investments needed, therefore, are not in AI capability development but in democratic governance infrastructure" (Carnegie Endowment, May 2026). For a funder, the risk is asymmetric: a tool that raises the volume of consultation without raising the capacity to act on it widens exactly the gap this cycle identifies. The trigger for revisiting is adoption by a UK public body as a formal decision record.

Action: Through 2027, the policy team reports quarterly on UK public-body adoption of machine-synthesised deliberation, and returns to the Board only when a formal decision record uses it.

Scenario Matrix

Two uncertainties will shape the next three to seven years more than any others. The vertical axis is neighbourhood capability, thickening at the top and thinning at the bottom: whether organised, governance-ready local bodies exist in enough places to act. The horizontal axis is how binding the new rights prove in practice, weak at the left and strong at the right: whether commencement regulations, valuation rules and council behaviour make the right of first refusal real or nominal. Ready and Waiting

Capability thickens because funders, including this one, put money into governance and transaction readiness, but the rights themselves under-deliver. Commencement regulations narrow the asset classes, valuation disputes drag, and councils treat first refusal as a procedural step rather than a transfer route. Communities that are ready find little to buy, and the frustration lands on the funders who told them to get ready. The Fund's readiness investment still pays, because capable bodies win contracts, hold leases and run services, but the asset-ownership story it was sold on does not materialise. Advocacy effort shifts from capital to commencement detail, and the Fund's most useful asset becomes its evidence on where capability now sits.

Early indicators: Commencement regulations exclude major asset classes; first-refusal windows expire without completion in more than half of triggered cases; community bodies redirect toward service contracts.

Communities Take Ownership

Capability and rights arrive together. Readiness funding, the Community Wealth Fund and a workable commencement regime mean that first refusal converts into completed purchases at scale, and community ownership moves from a few thousand assets to a visible national estate. Local energy ownership adds a second asset class with its own revenue. The Fund's problem becomes success management: demand for follow-on capital, asset-management capability and insurance outstrips what any grant-maker can supply, and the sector needs a lender more than a funder. In this scenario the Fund should decide whether to become, or to underwrite, that lender, because no other body is positioned to.

Early indicators: Completion rates above one in three triggered rights; a national community assets fund reaches the £1bn mark; community energy schemes begin cross-subsidising asset purchase.

Rights on Paper

Neither side moves. Capacity stays concentrated where it already is, the rights prove hard to exercise, and the 2026 Act joins a long line of community empowerment measures that changed little outside a few well-organised places. Perceived influence stays close to a quarter of respondents, and the gap with the 59% who say influence matters keeps widening. The political risk is that the failure is read as evidence that communities do not want power, when the evidence says the opposite. For the Fund, this is the scenario in which its own longitudinal evidence becomes its most valuable output, because it is the only body positioned to show why the rights went unused.

Early indicators: Perceived influence flat or falling in successive surveys; fewer than 100 completed community right-to-buy purchases by 2029; no national readiness offer emerges.

Open Doors, Few Takers

The rights bite, and most neighbourhoods cannot use them. Assets come to market with a genuine first-refusal window, and the places that complete are those with existing development trusts, professional staff and access to finance. Community ownership grows while becoming more unequal, and the map of who owns their local assets tracks the map of who already had capacity. This is the scenario that should worry a funder with an equity mandate most, because every individual transaction is a success story and the aggregate is a widening gap. It is also the scenario the current evidence points at most directly.

Early indicators: Completions cluster in the least deprived deciles; Mission Critical neighbourhoods record near-zero completions; readiness support remains locally funded and patchy.

What We Are Not Planning For

Legislative reversal

Repeal or material weakening of the Community Right to Buy before 2030

The right has cross-party antecedents stretching back to the 2011 community rights and commands no organised opposition. Planning for its withdrawal would divert attention from the commencement detail, which is where the real risk to its usefulness sits.

↺ Reinstated if a commencement instrument excludes the main asset classes, or a new administration signals repeal in a manifesto or King's Speech.

Fiscal step-change

National funding for neighbourhood capacity at the scale ICON recommends

The Independent Commission on Neighbourhoods asks for investment "worth £2-2.5bn a year over the next twenty years" (Independent Commission on Neighbourhoods, January 2026). Nothing in the current fiscal position suggests a commitment of that order, and planning against it would misdirect the Fund's own allocation choices.

↺ Reinstated if a spending review commits multi-year neighbourhood funding above roughly £1bn a year, or dormant assets policy is expanded to a comparable scale.

Technology substitution

AI-mediated deliberation displacing face-to-face community decision-making in funded places

The OECD evidence describes AI in participation as an emerging practice concentrated in analysis and synthesis, not in the decision itself. The near-term risk to the Fund is procurement noise, not displacement of the local meeting.

↺ Reinstated if a UK local authority or national funder adopts a machine-synthesised deliberation as the formal basis for an allocation decision.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If readiness rather than capital is the binding constraint, what proportion of the England portfolio should shift from projects to capability, and what does the Fund stop doing to pay for it?
  2. Big Local produced 123 organisations and 60 assets across 150 areas over fifteen years. Is that the return the Fund expects from the Community Wealth Fund, and if not, what has to be done differently?
  3. The Fund can either spread readiness support thinly across many places or concentrate it where a transaction is imminent. Which failure would the Board rather defend?
  4. What does the Fund need to know about a place before 2028 that it does not currently collect, and who is accountable for collecting it before the councils change?
  5. If citizen participation is moving into audit and scrutiny functions, as the French campaign suggests, should the Fund be funding communities to hold public bodies to account as well as to run assets?

Superlatives used

The briefing carries no unsourced primacy claims. Earlier drafts described the 2026 Act as "the strongest set of asset rights in a generation", Big Local as "the largest single experiment in building neighbourhood capacity" and the Community Wealth Fund as "one of the largest live experiments in resident-controlled allocation in Europe"; none of those rankings appears in any source and all three were removed before delivery. What remains is comparative but not superlative: Big Local is described as "the long-running experiment in building neighbourhood capacity" and as "the neighbourhood endowment that ran across 150 areas", both of which the Local Trust record supports, and the Independent Commission on Neighbourhoods is described as "the most detailed independent recommendation on the capacity gap", which is analyst characterisation of the evidence base assembled here rather than a claim about the field.

Analytical synthesis claims

The organising thesis, that the binding constraint on community power has moved from permission to organised local capacity, reads across the Act and its GOV.UK summary, the Power to Change and Locality scale estimate, the Community and Engagement Survey influence figures, and the Local Trust account of Big Local's closure. No single source states it, and the briefing marks it as the analyst's reading in the Executive Synthesis and the counter-argument. The juxtaposition of "up to 190,000 assets" with Big Local's 60 assets sets a potential scope against a realised output from one programme; the two figures are not a like-for-like ratio and the text does not present them as one. The claim that readiness rather than capital is binding is explicitly contested in the counter-argument and in the stress-test box, and the falsification test proposed there (completion rates and failure causes over the first two years) does not yet have data behind it. The reading that "the gap between wanting a say and believing you have one widened over the year" is arithmetic on the survey's own two figures (an 8 percentage point rise against a 3 percentage point rise), not a finding the survey states. "Across 150 areas" and the March 2026 closing date come from the Local Trust record's key claim rather than from a captured verbatim span; both are stated on the source page and neither is load-bearing for the argument. Two sources carry a conflict worth naming. The Community and Engagement Survey release states an 8 percentage point rise in the importance measure in its headline and a 9 percentage point rise for the same 50% to 59% move in its time-series text; this briefing cites the two levels rather than either delta. The same release reports that people in the most deprived decile were more likely than those in the least deprived decile to feel they can influence local decisions, which cuts against a simple reading in which perceived agency tracks advantage, and it is quoted in the counter-argument rather than left out. The survey figures are reported for people aged 16 and over in England, and the briefing uses that wording rather than "adults". The scenario matrix, the exclusions, both figures and the Futures Wheel are analyst constructions.
