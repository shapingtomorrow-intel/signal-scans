Why this matters now

For twenty years the rich world has assumed that when it runs short of workers it can go and recruit some. That assumption stopped working this year, and not in one country.

Governments are now capping how many people they will admit, setting a price for entry, and making access conditional on employers training people at home first. At the same time they have turned inward for supply: legalising the people already working without papers, and paying pensioners to stay at their desks. Further out, the countries that used to send the workers are having far fewer children of their own.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: any workforce plan that closes its gap by hiring from abroad now depends on a permission somebody else rations. Find the roles where that is true, name them, and decide what happens if the permission does not come.

Executive Synthesis

Which vacancies on your plan assume you will be allowed to recruit someone from abroad?

The evidence in this report says that permission is now rationed, priced and, in one case, already refused.

The central tension of this cycle is that the labour shortage has stopped being treated as a recruitment problem by the governments experiencing it. Between March and September 2026, four advanced economies moved in the same direction at once: they capped, priced or conditioned the workers they would admit, and went looking instead for supply already inside their own borders. The last cycle in this series, in July, described immigration hardening from a lever into a political ceiling. That reading now needs correcting in both directions. The ceiling has become numerical rather than rhetorical, and the domestic reservoir the July cycle judged close to exhausted turns out to have had one large untapped seam in it: people already resident and already working, without papers or past pension age.

Capping: Japan stated its restaurant sector's acceptance projection as an acceptance ceiling of 50,000, found roughly 46,000 already in place at the end of February, and refused Certificates of Eligibility received on or after 13 April 2026 (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, March 2026, Japanese). Pricing: the UK's Migration Advisory Committee now derives the general Skilled Worker salary threshold from a percentile rule, reporting that the 30th percentile of 2025 earnings "produces a general threshold of £44,000 (rounded to the nearest £100) using ASHE 2025." (Migration Advisory Committee, May 2026). Conditioning: the same committee recommended 28 of 82 occupations for eighteen months of shortage-list access and none for full access, cutting roughly 10,000 visas a year to about 4,000, conditional on an employer plan to use British workers (Temporary Shortage List: Stage 2 report, July 2026). Turning inward: Spain drew nearly 1.2 million applications to its 2026 regularisation, "Nearly 1.2 million people applied by the June 30 deadline, more than double the government's original expectation of around 500,000, and two-thirds of applicants were from Central or South America." (Migration Policy Institute, July 2026), and Germany exempted the first €2,000 a month of earnings from tax for anyone working past the statutory pension age (Bundesministerium der Finanzen, June 2026, German).

The labour shortage has stopped being treated as a recruitment problem by the governments experiencing it.

The second movement is quieter and changes what the monthly data means. In the United States, break-even employment, the number of jobs needed each month to hold unemployment steady, "The break-even rate peaked at about 250,000 jobs per month in 2023, fell to roughly 10,000 by July 2025, and declined to near zero thereafter, averaging about –3,000 jobs per month from August to December 2025, indicating, if anything, a modest net jobs loss over this period." (Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, March 2026). Oxford Economics forecasts it falling from 50,000 now to zero next year and slightly negative by 2028 (Oxford Economics, August 2026). A print that would have read as a stall in 2023 now reads as adequate. The catch is that the new yardstick is far less knowable than the one it replaced: alternative population vintages move break-even estimates by up to 66,000 jobs a month, around 60% of mean monthly gains over the post-pandemic period (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, August 2026).

These are not two stories. A government that caps admissions has to find the workers somewhere, and every one of them reached for the same place: the people already inside. Spain converted irregular residents into contributors, Germany is paying its pensioners to stay, and the euro area's recent employment gain rests on workers aged 55 to 74 (European Central Bank, May 2026). On the available evidence the operative constraint has moved from how many people you can recruit to how many you can keep, and almost no plan written before this year was built around that distinction.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The load-bearing assumption is that these caps and prices are durable settings rather than the top of a cycle. Two pieces of evidence in this briefing cut against it. Japan's ceilings rest on pandemic-era projections and the restaurant quota was held unchanged at 50,000 despite the first phase under-delivering, the signature of a number nobody has revisited rather than a considered limit (East Asia Forum, May 2026), so a routine upward revision would dissolve the theme. In the UK, the price is set by a rule designed to move: the threshold re-derives at each ASHE update and shortage-list access runs for eighteen months rather than indefinitely, so a rule that tracks the labour market can track it back down. The claim that would force a material revision is a sectoral cap raised on demand within a fiscal year, or a UK threshold reset downward at the next ASHE update.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Name every role in the plan whose supply depends on a permission you do not control. Caps, thresholds and conditional lists now sit between a funded vacancy and a person in it.
  2. State, in writing, the break-even employment number your reaction function uses. If it is the one you used in 2023 you are reading every payroll print against a threshold that has fallen by two hundred thousand.
  3. Build the domestic-workforce plan before you need the visa, not after. In the UK it is now the precondition for access rather than the alternative to it.

Themes

Five themes, ranked by how soon they change a decision. Each leads with the consequence, carries its own counter-argument, and points to the Strategic Implication it feeds.

1. Countries are growing their workforce from people already inside it

Immediate

The largest single addition to a European workforce this year came from people who were already living and working in the country, and who were given papers. Spain's regularisation has put hundreds of thousands of existing workers onto the contributory rolls in months, at a speed no recruitment campaign matches. If your labour plan treats the resident population as fixed and the recruitment pipeline as the variable, this cycle has the relationship the wrong way round. The same manoeuvre is available at continental scale, and the EU has just decided the terms on which it will and will not use it.

  • Nearly 1.2 million people applied to Spain's 2026 regularisation, more than double the government's expectation, with two-thirds of applicants from Central or South America (Migration Policy Institute, July 2026).
  • The Spanish government reports that almost 340,000 of August's registered affiliates came from the extraordinary regularisation process, with 337,917 regularised workers affiliated at end-August on the ministry's own count (Ministerio de Inclusión, Seguridad Social y Migraciones, September 2026, Spanish; Funcas, September 2026, Spanish).
  • Of Spain's 679,000 annual rise in affiliation, 482,000 were foreign nationals, and in August the entire seasonally adjusted increase sat with foreign affiliates while Spanish nationals fell by 4,000 (Funcas, September 2026, Spanish). On one newspaper's own calculation, regularised foreign nationals account for 43.7% of all new affiliations in 2026 to date (El Español, September 2026, Spanish).
  • The same instrument sits unused at scale in the EU: "On 30 June 2026, a total of 4.41 million non-EU citizens who fled Ukraine had temporary protection status in the EU." (Eurostat, August 2026), with Germany hosting 1,286,230 of them. On 15 July member-state ambassadors extended protection to 4 March 2028 but excluded men aged 23 to 60 who cannot show an exit stamp or exemption from March 2027 (Euronews, July 2026).

The counter-argument

Two of this theme's own sources say the gain is smaller and more fragile than it looks. Strip out the regularisation effect and Funcas finds the underlying Spanish labour market decelerating, so much of the recorded growth is work reclassified rather than work created. COMPAS adds a design flaw with a date on it: "The permit does not automatically convert into permanent residence, meaning beneficiaries must transition into ordinary immigration categories under existing rules without any guarantee of renewal." (COMPAS, University of Oxford, April 2026). That is a 2027 renewal cliff, not a permanent addition to supply, and the July decision to exclude military-age Ukrainians shows conversion answering to something other than labour need.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal The countries that supply the workers are thinning too. Philippine total fertility is "Among women aged 15 to 49 years, the TFR in the Philippines was 1.7 children per woman for the three-year period preceding the survey, indicating a continued decline from 4.1 children per woman in 1993." (Philippine Statistics Authority, March 2026), below the conventional replacement level, and Colombian births fell for a fourth consecutive year to their lowest general fertility rate in a decade (DANE, March 2026, Spanish).
  • Weak signal Morocco has fallen to 1.97 children per woman and Tunisia to an estimated 1.53 (Institut national d'études démographiques, May 2026, French), though that is true of the Maghreb and not of North Africa as a bloc: the Institute for Security Studies puts regional fertility at "North Africa currently has the lowest fertility rate (3.0 births per fertile woman) among all the regions in Africa, ranging from 4.6 in Mauritania to 2.2 in Tunisia." and does not expect the region to reach replacement until 2043.
  • Weak signal Not every supplier is contracting. Indonesia is targeting 500,000 skilled placements between 2026 and 2029 through training tied to confirmed vacancies (Migrant Times, July 2026), and UNFPA argues falling fertility becomes an economic problem through institutional failure to adapt rather than automatically (UN News, April 2026). These bite after 2030, not inside this cycle.

2. Germany is paying pensioners to stay at work

Immediate

Germany has put a price on working past pension age, and the early evidence is that price is not the binding constraint. That matters well beyond Germany, because the tax exemption is the cleanest natural experiment any advanced economy has run on whether older workers can be bought back into the labour force. If a €2,000 monthly exemption moves single-digit thousands of people, then every workforce plan that assumes retention of older staff can be solved with money needs a different instrument. The German evidence points at hours, workload and employer practice instead.

  • The Aktivrente took effect on 1 January 2026: for people who have reached the statutory retirement age and remain in employment subject to social insurance, wage income up to €2,000 a month is tax-free (Bundesministerium der Finanzen, June 2026, German).
  • First payroll-data estimates, attributed to Datev's chief economist, put the measure's effect at roughly 9,000 additional employed old-age pensioners, on the basis that working pensioners average about twenty hours a week (t-online, June 2026, German).
  • Set against that, Germany's labour supply in 2026 falls by 470,000 for demographic reasons, rises 260,000 for behavioural reasons and 170,000 from migration, so behaviour is already contributing roughly one and a half times what migration does (IAB-Kurzbericht 5/2026, March 2026, German).
  • 14% of German pensioners aged 65 to 74 were in work on first results of the 2025 Mikrozensus, up from 13%, but 65% of those in dependent employment were in marginal work and 40% worked under ten hours a week (Statistisches Bundesamt, August 2026, German).

The counter-argument

Germany has tried this before and got the opposite of what it intended. When earnings limits for early retirees were abolished in January 2023, the share of new long-insured retirees with substantial additional earnings rose to 26.4% by 2024 from 10.0% in 2018, but their average retirement age fell, which IW says undermines the goal of longer working lives (Institut der deutschen Wirtschaft Köln, June 2026, German). Bertelsmann points the same way from the other end: 37% of the early-retired would work again on the right terms, and what they name is control over hours and workload, not pay (Bertelsmann Stiftung, March 2026, German). That study covers early retirees while the Aktivrente starts at the statutory age, so it qualifies the policy's logic rather than testing it.

3. A weak jobs number no longer means a weak labour market

Immediate

The number of jobs the United States must add each month to hold unemployment steady has fallen to around zero, and may go negative. Any forecast, covenant, hiring plan or rate expectation calibrated on a two-hundred-thousand-a-month norm is now reading the same data against a threshold that no longer exists. The harder problem is the second one: the replacement threshold is itself unstable, because the population denominator gets revised after the fact by enough to move the answer materially. Both facts have to be carried at once.

The threshold has collapsed, and the estimates of where it now sits do not agree.

  • Break-even employment "The break-even rate peaked at about 250,000 jobs per month in 2023, fell to roughly 10,000 by July 2025, and declined to near zero thereafter, averaging about –3,000 jobs per month from August to December 2025, indicating, if anything, a modest net jobs loss over this period." (Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, March 2026); net unauthorised immigration averaged minus 55,000 a month in the second half of 2025.
  • Oxford Economics has "the breakeven pace of monthly employment growth forecasted to decline from 50,000 today to zero next year, and potentially turn slightly negative by 2028" (Oxford Economics, August 2026), a multi-year recalibration rather than a 2026 anomaly, now reported in the general business press as well as in central-bank research (Fortune, August 2026). The Budget Lab puts the same collapse at "By one measure, the so-called breakeven rate of monthly employment growth has fallen from above 150,000 in 2024 to below 20,000 in 2026", with 2026 estimates running from zero to above 80,000 depending on the immigration scenario chosen (The Budget Lab at Yale, July 2026).
  • The Kansas City Fed puts the labour force at or near a structural peak, projecting it "This continued aging is projected to weigh on the labor force, offsetting the CBO's projections for immigration and keeping the labor force about flat over the next five years." as ageing offsets projected immigration (Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, June 2026).
  • Yet the estimate is unstable at source: "alternative vintages produce breakeven estimates that differ by up to 66,000 jobs per month, around 60% of the mean monthly jobs gains over the post-pandemic period, according to our analysis." (Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago, August 2026). Before 2020, average year-to-year population revisions were only 270,000 people.

The counter-argument

Reading every soft print as arithmetic rather than weakness is the obvious way to get this wrong. The Budget Lab supplies the evidence against it: the hiring rate was 3.1% in February 2026 against a 2022 average of 4.2%, the job-finding rate for unemployed workers fell from almost 30% to around 24%, and average unemployment duration rose to nearly 26 weeks from under 20 in early 2023. Job-to-job transitions held steady throughout. The deterioration is real, concentrated on people already out of work, and invisible to an aggregate break-even argument (The Budget Lab at Yale, March 2026).

4. Japan is capping the workers it spent a decade recruiting

Near-Term

Japan has turned a planning projection into an enforceable ceiling and used it to shut a sector's intake mid-year. For any organisation with Japanese operations, or with a supplier whose staffing runs through one visa category, the constraint has moved from whether workers can be found to whether the administrative number has room left in it. The cap bound in the sector with the most acute shortage, in the same year the foreign resident population set a record above four million.

A projection used as a limit: the sector was within about four thousand of its ceiling when the notice was issued.

  • The Immigration Services Agency stated the food-service sector's acceptance projection as an acceptance ceiling of 50,000, recorded roughly 46,000 residents in the sector at end-February 2026, and refused Certificates of Eligibility received on or after 13 April 2026 (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, March 2026, Japanese).
  • This happened at a record high of foreign residents: 4,125,395 at end-2025, up 9.5% and above four million for the first time (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, March 2026, Japanese), against 2,571,037 foreign workers on the mandatory employer-notification series (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, January 2026, Japanese, structural anchor).
  • The quotas rest on pandemic-era projections: the restaurant sector projected 53,000 in the first phase but hired about 35,000, and the second-phase quota was left unchanged at 50,000, with one noodle chain running roughly 500 of its 660 full-time staff on the route (East Asia Forum, May 2026).
  • The successor system is being built out even as intake tightens: Japan and Thailand signed the first memorandum of cooperation on the Employment for Skill Development system on 2 June 2026 (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, June 2026, Japanese), and a government expert panel on future acceptance opened on 7 August with a basic policy due within the fiscal year (NHK, August 2026, Japanese).

The counter-argument

A ceiling nobody intended as one can be raised as easily as it was applied. The 50,000 is a second-phase quota carried over unchanged from an under-delivering first phase, and Japan is widening the successor route through bilateral agreements, so April could be queue management rather than restriction. What makes an upward revision costly now is political: East Asia Forum reports immigration hardliners appointed to key positions after the far-right Sanseito party's 2025 gains. Two related readings sit alongside it: that Japan is restricting through administrative discretion rather than legislation, with naturalisation scrutiny stretched towards a decade (East Asia Forum, May 2026), and the successor system carries limits of its own, since "it also introduces stricter language requirements and admission caps" (East Asia Forum, April 2026).

5. Work visas are now rationed by rule, not by shortage

Near-Term

The UK has stopped deciding case by case who may be recruited and started publishing the rule that decides it. The threshold is a percentile of national earnings, so it moves with the labour market rather than with a minister, and shortage-list access runs eighteen months and only where the employer has a plan to use British workers, so asserting a shortage no longer obtains it. The work that used to happen after a shortage was declared now has to happen before it. Keep this separate from the contested lifetime fiscal argument alongside it: the published rule allocates access, the fiscal case is offered to justify the wider settlement regime. The EU runs the same instrument outward, naming visa policy as leverage over third countries.

  • Setting the general Skilled Worker threshold at the 30th percentile of full-time earnings for eligible occupations "produces a general threshold of £44,000 (rounded to the nearest £100) using ASHE 2025.", with occupation-specific thresholds worked at the 25th percentile (Migration Advisory Committee, May 2026).
  • "We recommend 28 occupations for 18-month access." and none for full access, with 18 refused; the 82 occupations considered represented about 10,000 visas a year and those recommended about 4,000, conditional on a Jobs Plan to maximise use of the UK workforce (Temporary Shortage List: Stage 2 report, July 2026).
  • The EU makes the same linkage explicit at the border: the Commission states that its migration diplomacy "This includes the use of incentives, such as readmission instruments, financing tools, visa and trade policy as part of the EU's migration diplomacy outreach to third countries." (European Commission, June 2026), alongside a Return Regulation permitting return hubs in third countries unrelated to a person's origin (Euronews, March 2026).
  • MAC is replacing the stay-duration assumptions inside that fiscal modelling with observed data on 916,000 Skilled Worker journeys, and the data cut against the assumptions: "Taking into account the fact that low earners and care workers are more likely to stay in the UK longer-term would reduce the projected fiscal benefit of the Skilled Worker route relative to those same workers having shorter stay durations." (Who stays, who leaves?, May 2026).

The counter-argument

The challenge is aimed at the fiscal case, not at the rule. The Migration Observatory shows lifetime fiscal estimates are artefacts of the assumptions chosen, reporting the OBR finding that a migrant arriving at 25 on average UK earnings contributes more over a lifetime than a UK-born worker on the same salary (Migration Observatory, University of Oxford, June 2026). Sunder Katwala argues the overhaul is politically rather than fiscally driven, and that IPPR and others have shown "the £10 billion figure is a mirage" (UK in a Changing Europe, April 2026); CEPS makes the parallel case on the EU side, that conditionality rules manufacture the irregularity they then police (Centre for European Policy Studies, March 2026). That is a strong argument the fiscal arithmetic should not be doing this work, but not an argument that the threshold and the shortage list are cosmetic: those are published, dated, and already cut roughly 10,000 visas a year to about 4,000, whichever way the dispute settles.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's evidence into a plan. Each names the move, a horizon and a decision posture.

SI 1: Re-base the workforce plan on retention and conversion, not on recruitment volume

Every government here has stopped solving its shortage by recruiting and started working on the people already inside the perimeter. Do the same arithmetic on your own workforce before the next planning round: of the roles scheduled for external hire, how many could be filled by retaining someone already leaving, extending someone past retirement, or converting a contingent worker. Germany is the warning attached to it. Pay alone moved single-digit thousands, while hours, workload and employer practice are what binds, so a retention plan built on a salary increment will produce the German result.

Action: the CHRO should split every funded vacancy into recruited, retained, converted and automated by 31 December 2026, costing the retention lines on working conditions rather than pay.

Decide Draws on Themes 1, 2 and 4

SI 2: State the break-even employment assumption your forecasts are using, and put a range on it

A model treating 150,000 monthly US payroll gains as the neutral line is mis-specified by roughly that entire amount, and the correction is not a single new number: contemporaneous 2026 estimates here differ by about 50,000 jobs a month, and population revisions alone move it by up to 66,000. Write the assumption down, carry it as a range rather than a point, and decide in advance what a negative print with flat unemployment means for hiring authority, capital commitments and rate expectations. The alternative is to discover the assumption when it has already produced the wrong reaction.

Action: the CFO should have the FY27 planning pack state its break-even assumption as a sourced range, re-tested at each CBO or Census population revision.

Decide Draws on Themes 3 and 5

SI 3: Build the domestic-workforce plan before you need the visa

UK shortage-list access now depends on an employer plan to use the domestic workforce, and runs eighteen months rather than indefinitely. That inverts the old sequence, in which a company demonstrated shortage and then received access. The plan is the entry ticket now, and it takes longer to build than the application takes to file. Treat training, apprenticeship and progression commitments as part of the immigration file rather than a separate corporate-responsibility exercise, in the UK and in any jurisdiction adopting conditionality. The same discipline guards against the Japanese failure mode, where one visa route carries a concentration of headcount an administrative notice can close.

Action: the CHRO and public affairs lead should produce, by 31 March 2027, a domestic-workforce plan for each sponsored occupation and a concentration limit for any single visa route.

Prepare Draws on Themes 5, 4 and 1

SI 4: Put the dates on which these rules reset into the planning calendar

Every constraint in this briefing carries a review date, and none of them has an owner. The UK threshold re-derives at the next ASHE update, shortage-list access runs eighteen months, Japan's sectoral ceilings can be revised inside a fiscal year, and Spain's regularisation permits renew at twelve. Each is already an early indicator in the scenario matrix below, which is what makes this cheap: the watchlist exists and needs a name against it, rather than being rediscovered when a route closes.

Action: the head of workforce planning should own a dated reset calendar by 31 January 2027, covering the ASHE update, the shortage-list review, Japanese ceiling revisions and the Spanish renewal window.

Hard Ceiling

Caps, thresholds and conditional lists hold or tighten, and the domestic response does not arrive: tax incentives move small numbers, regularisation stalls at renewal, and older-worker participation plateaus. Labour-intensive sectors ration output rather than wages, and the binding constraint on growth becomes headcount rather than demand. Wage pressure concentrates in the occupations that cannot be automated or offshored, particularly care, food service, construction and logistics. Public finances absorb the cost twice, through lower contributory receipts and through service rationing. Organisations that concentrated headcount in a single visa route discover the concentration when an administrative notice closes it, as Japan's food-service employers did in April 2026.

Early indicators: a second Japanese sector reaching its ceiling; UK shortage-list refusals exceeding grants at the next review; Spanish regularisation renewals falling materially below first-grant volumes in 2027.

The Inside Track

Admission stays rationed, but the inward turn works. Regularisation converts durably rather than expiring, older-worker retention responds to redesigned hours and workload rather than to tax relief, and participation among the groups furthest from the labour market rises. Labour supply holds up without net inflows, which removes the political cost of the shortage and entrenches the caps. Employers who invested early in retention, conversion and progression take disproportionate advantage, because the marginal worker is sourced internally now and few organisations do it well. A workforce function stops being administrative overhead here and starts being a competitive asset, and the gap between organisations that built one and those that did not shows up in output rather than in cost.

Early indicators: German working-pensioner numbers rising faster than the Datev estimate implies; Spanish regularised affiliates sustained through the 2027 renewal window; euro-area older-worker participation continuing to outpace statutory-age effects.

Back to the Old Lever

Caps prove to be unrevised administrative numbers rather than policy, and are raised when sectors complain loudly enough, while the domestic response disappoints. Recruitment resumes as the primary instrument and the 2026 tightening reads in hindsight as a queue-management episode. The risk in this environment is complacency: the sending-side arithmetic in the weak signals continues regardless, so the corridor reopens into a supply base that is ageing and, in several named countries, already below replacement. Costs rise through competition for a shrinking pool rather than through permission, and the constraint reappears later in the decade in a less negotiable form.

Early indicators: a Japanese sectoral ceiling raised mid-fiscal-year; UK salary thresholds reset downward at the next ASHE update; EU temporary protection converted to work status without the March 2027 conditions.

Both Taps Open

Access eases and domestic participation also materialises: the most benign quadrant here, and the least likely on current evidence. Supply recovers from both directions, wage pressure in shortage occupations subsides, and the fiscal argument for restricting entry weakens as contributory receipts rise. The planning risk inverts. Organisations that over-invested in retention and automation on an assumption of permanent scarcity carry cost they cannot shed quickly, and advantage swings back to those who stayed flexible. Even here the sending-side constraint sets a horizon, so read this as a decade of relief rather than a return to the pre-2024 baseline, and size the relief accordingly.

Early indicators: US break-even employment revised upward on a higher immigration path; UK Temporary Shortage List access extended beyond eighteen months at review; a major receiving economy announcing a second regularisation.

What We Are Not Planning For

Technological substitution

Automation closing the gap in care, food service and construction inside the horizon

The July cycle in this series found that automation gains land in cognitive white-collar work while the demographic pressure sits in care, trades, delivery and hospitality. Nothing in this cycle's evidence base changes that, and the sectors where caps actually bound this year, Japanese food service and UK care, are precisely the ones where substitution is slowest and most capital-intensive. Planning for a substitution effect inside eighteen months would misallocate capital away from the retention and conversion work that Themes 1 and 2 show is available now.

↺ Reinstated if a major receiving economy revises a sectoral labour projection downward citing deployed automation rather than demand.

Fertility policy

Pronatalist measures changing labour supply within the planning horizon

A child born in response to a policy announced today enters the labour force in the 2040s, which is outside every horizon in this briefing. The evidence base contains a live argument about whether fertility decline reflects preference or affordability, and it is a serious one, but it bears on the 2040s rather than on the next planning round. We are excluding it not because it is unimportant but because it cannot move a decision this cycle, and treating it as though it can crowds out the levers that operate on a two-year timescale.

↺ Reinstated if a sending country ties emigration policy or bilateral labour agreements explicitly to domestic fertility targets.

Political discontinuity

A coordinated reopening of migration routes across receiving economies

Four jurisdictions moved in the same restrictive direction within six months, driven by domestic politics that are not synchronised and would not unwind together. Individual reversals are entirely plausible and are covered in the Back to the Old Lever scenario; a simultaneous one is not, and planning for it would remove the pressure to do the retention and conversion work that pays off under every other scenario in the matrix. This is a deliberate asymmetry: the cost of preparing for scarcity that eases is lower than the cost of assuming ease that does not arrive.

↺ Reinstated if two of the four jurisdictions in this briefing loosen a numerical or price constraint in the same quarter.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If we could fill only half the roles in next year's plan that require a visa, which half would we choose, and does anyone currently own that ranking?
  2. Our forecasts imply a view on how many jobs the economy must add each month to hold unemployment steady. What is that number, who set it, and when was it last changed?
  3. What is the largest share of one site's headcount we are willing to have sitting inside a single visa route, and are we already above it anywhere?
  4. Germany bought older workers with a tax exemption and moved a very small number. If we needed to keep a hundred people past their planned retirement date, what would we change first, and would it be pay?
  5. Which of our long-horizon plans names a source country whose fertility is already below replacement, and would we notice if the corridor narrowed before the plan did?

The central claim of this briefing is a synthesis no single source makes: that Japan's caps, the UK's thresholds and conditional shortage list, the EU's visa leverage, Spain's regularisation and Germany's Aktivrente constitute one direction of travel rather than five national policies.
