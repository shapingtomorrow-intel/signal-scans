Why This Matters Now

Most organisations read defence news as somebody else's business. That reading is now out of date, and there is a date on it.

On 10 November a Chinese export rule may return that catches products containing even a small share of Chinese-processed minerals, which sit in motors, magnets and scanners. Europe's rearmament now competes for the same parts and people, NATO aircraft shoot down drones over Europe, and the EU's AI rules do not exempt a product just because one customer is a defence ministry.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: you do not have to be in defence to be caught by it, and the cheapest step this quarter is to find out whether you are.

Executive Synthesis

Which of the products your organisation sells contains a magnet or coating whose rare earth content was processed in China?

If nobody can answer that by early October, the answer on 10 November will be supplied by a customs authority rather than by you.

Most organisations still read defence news as somebody else's business. This edition's reading is that the security build-out now reaches them through inputs and rules rather than budgets. Europe's spending has become dated programmes: £298bn in the UK to 2029/30 (UK Ministry of Defence, Jun 2026), a French budget rising from €57.1bn in 2026 to €76.3bn in 2030 with a legal mechanism to give military orders priority over civil ones (Ministère des Armées, Apr 2026, French), and 16 more German projects worth over €9.5bn cleared in one sitting (hartpunkt, Jul 2026, German). Output is not keeping pace: Rheinmetall's order backlog reached €80.5bn against half-year sales of €5.2bn (Rheinmetall, Aug 2026).

Some of this is settled: the money is committed, and the EU AI Act's defence exemption does not cover a product placed on the market for both civilian and military purposes (European Commission, AI Act Service Desk, Recital 24). Three things are not. Will China let its export rule return on 10 November, catching any foreign-made item where Chinese-origin controlled inputs reach 0.1% of its value (MOFCOM, Oct 2025, Chinese), when the United States still imports 71% of its rare earths from China (CSIS, Apr 2026)? Will drone incursions keep rising, after Romania logged 23 airspace violations by mid-August against 18 in the four years before (ABC News, Aug 2026)? And who carries a loss caused by navigation jamming, now recorded around the clock across southern and central Finland (Traficom, Apr 2026, Finnish)?

Looking ahead to 2030, McKinsey estimates that European NATO defence spending could reach €800bn under the 3.5% of GDP benchmark (McKinsey, Feb 2026), the UK plans at least six new energetics factories (UK Ministry of Defence, Jun 2026), the G7 aims for no single country outside the bloc to control over 60% of rare earth and magnet supply (BOFIT, reporting the IEA, Aug 2026), and the EU's high-risk AI rules now apply from 2 December 2027 (Cuatrecasas, Jul 2026). None of these is certain, and each is a point where readers can still choose their position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that China lets the controls return. Heavy rare earth prices were falling in early August, with dysprosium down 11.6% (Rare Earth Mining, directional, Aug 2026), which suggests traders expect another extension. If Beijing extends, theme 1 becomes a 2027 problem, although screening a bill of materials still costs little.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Screen the bill of materials against the 0.1% threshold before 10 November.
  2. Re-run 2027 to 2030 lead-time and labour plans as if defence orders are served first.
  3. Classify every AI-enabled product sold to both civilian and defence buyers.
  4. Treat navigation jamming as an operating condition.

1. A Chinese export rule returns on 10 November and reaches far beyond defence

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: China's Ministry of Commerce; customs authorities enforce · Room to act: Moderate: firms can screen and switch suppliers

China suspended its expanded rare earth export controls for twelve months in November 2025, and the suspension lapses on 10 November 2026. What returns is a value test, not a defence control: it catches foreign-made goods containing Chinese-processed material, wherever they are made. The threshold is low, the materials sit in motors, magnets, coatings and optics, and almost nobody outside the defence primes has checked.

  • The operative text sets the threshold by value, not by end use. It catches foreign-manufactured listed items that contain, incorporate or are mixed with Chinese-origin controlled inputs, where those inputs reach 0.1% or more of the finished item's value, and requires a licence regardless of where the exporter sits (MOFCOM, October 2025, Chinese).
  • Enforcement has moved from announcement to practice, and the exposure is not confined to named targets: The extraterritorial reach of its jurisdiction to anyone dealing in China-origin rare earth material means the compliance exposure is not limited to targeted companies. (Morgan Lewis, July 2026).
  • Diversification has moved US dependence very little, with the United States still importing 71% of its rare earth consumption from China after $7.3bn of federal financing committed across agencies (CSIS, April 2026).
  • The most determined national effort anywhere bought time rather than exit. China's share of Japan's rare earth imports declined from approximately 90 percent to less than 60 percent by 2020., yet Japan remains dependent at the processing and magnet stage (Observer Research Foundation, July 2026).

Weak signals to watch

  • Emerging One element moved against the basket. While heavy rare earth prices fell in early August, Gallium led the declines at -18.8%, while erbium was the standout gainer at +17.6%, the only element to post a double-digit rise. (Rare Earth Mining, August 2026, Tier 4, carried directionally). That is consistent with targeted stockpiling ahead of the deadline, though it is thin evidence and could be ordinary volatility.

Outlook to 2030

The G7 has set a goal that by 2030 no single country outside the bloc should control over 60% of the supply of rare earths and permanent magnets, while China today refines 84% of the rare earths used in magnets (BOFIT, reporting the IEA, Aug 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the dependence will have narrowed but not ended, so licensing risk stays in supply plans.

What it means: Treat screening against the 0.1% rule as a standing control, not a one-off exercise before November.

Counter-argument

The market is not behaving as though reimposition is coming: in early August heavy rare earth prices were falling, with dysprosium down 11.6% and terbium down 7.19%. Traders may expect another extension, or reimposition may already be priced into contracts. The probability is uncertain; the asymmetry is not, because screening a bill of materials costs little and discovering the answer at a border costs a great deal.

2. Europe's defence plans have turned into firm, dated orders

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Defence ministries, parliaments and prime contractors · Room to act: Low over orders; moderate over lead times and hiring

In June this briefing argued that factories, not money, had become the constraint. Two months later the plans behind the money are signed, costed and dated across three of Europe's four largest defence spenders. A decade of guaranteed demand now sits on an industrial base that also supplies civil manufacturing, and one government has written into law which of the two is served first.

  • The United Kingdom committed £298bn to 2029/30, with a stated intention that By 2030, we will have built at least six new energetics factories and increased our national munitions production capacity. (UK Ministry of Defence, June 2026).
  • France legislated the uplift rather than announcing it, lifting the defence budget from €57.1bn in 2026 to €76.3bn in 2030, and creating strategic stockpiles with a legal mechanism to give military orders priority over civil ones (Ministère des Armées, April 2026, French).
  • In its last sitting before the summer recess the Bundestag budget committee approved a further 16 major procurement projects worth over €9.5bn, including four frigates at €6.3bn (hartpunkt, July 2026, German), with roughly 100 further major projects expected in the second half of the year (bundeswehr-journal, August 2026, German).
  • The order book shows the absorption problem: As at 30 June 2026, Rheinmetall Backlog increased significantly to €80.5 billion (previous year: €56.0 billion)., against half-year sales of €5.2bn (Rheinmetall, August 2026, company reporting, treated directionally).
  • Fragmentation compounds it: Limited capacities, high unit costs, and long delivery times also mean that the mobilization of large financial resources for defence has generated only modest economic stimulus, since this too depends critically on additional investments to expand production. (Kiel Institute, March 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The UK plans at least six new energetics factories by 2030 (UK Ministry of Defence, Jun 2026) and France's defence budget reaches €76.3bn in 2030 (Ministère des Armées, Apr 2026, French). On this briefing's reading, defence demand remains a standing claim on civil industrial capacity until at least 2030.

What it means: Plan civil lead times and hiring to 2030 on the assumption that defence orders are served first.

Counter-argument

Capacity is being built, not merely promised: Rheinmetall has opened a plant in Lithuania and the UK plans at least six new energetics factories by 2030. Against that, McKinsey's reading of the same period is that Order backlogs are expanding, a substantial share of equipment continues to be sourced outside Europe (McKinsey, February 2026, carried outside the six-month window). On current numbers the backlog is growing faster.

3. NATO has started shooting down drones over Europe

Immediate

Weight: medium · Who decides: NATO air forces and national regulators · Room to act: Low over incursions; high over planning for jamming

Since June the threshold has moved from tolerating incursions to destroying them, in at least three NATO states, and the tempo is rising rather than falling, with Romania the main point of contact. NATO's eastern missions are now standing rather than temporary. Alongside the incursions, satellite-navigation interference has become a continuous background condition across parts of northern Europe. Regulators say safety margins are holding, but in at least one case jamming pushed a drone off course into defended airspace. For anyone flying, sailing or running infrastructure in the region, that makes jamming a condition to plan for rather than an incident.

  • Volume is the clearest signal. Romania logged 23 airspace violations by 17 August 2026 against 18 across the whole of 2022 to 2025, with 27 drone or fragment discoveries and four drones shot down (ABC News, August 2026).
  • Interceptions have followed. On 19 May a Romanian F-16 flying Baltic Air Policing destroyed a drone over Põltsamaa in Estonia (ERR, May 2026, Estonian).
  • The Latvian case shows one mechanism at work. On 20 August Latvia's National Armed Forces confirmed that the drone destroyed over Rugāji on 14 August was a Ukrainian unmanned aircraft that had entered Latvian airspace from Belarusian territory, most probably under the influence of Russian electromagnetic warfare (LSM.lv, August 2026, Latvian).
  • Navigation denial is now continuous rather than episodic. Finland's regulator reports GNSS interference around the clock across southern and central Finland at cruise altitudes (Traficom, April 2026, Finnish), and Norway is adding monitoring stations after jamming was recorded as low as 500 metres over a region with 11 airports (The Barents Observer, May 2026).
  • The standing missions have become permanent architecture. Since January 2025, Baltic Sentry has enhanced the security of critical undersea infrastructure by deploying a range of assets, including frigates, maritime patrol aircraft and a fleet of naval drones., with Eastern Sentry running since September 2025 and Forward Land Forces Finland standing up in June 2026 as the Alliance's ninth multinational battlegroup (NATO, June 2026).
  • If an F-16 fires an AIM-9X Sidewinder missile against a Shahed drone, it applies a $400,000 solution to a $35,000 problem., against $40bn pledged over five years for layered counter-drone defence that will take years to field (The Kyiv Independent, August 2026).

Sources: European Commission DG CONNECT (February 2026); Traficom (April 2026); ERR (May 2026); The Barents Observer (May 2026); NATO (June 2026); LSM.lv (August 2026); ABC News (August 2026).

Weak signals to watch

  • Emerging Displacement as a mechanism with no attacker. If jamming pushes third-party aircraft into defended airspace more often, the legal question is not who attacked, but who is responsible for a loss with no attacker. No framework currently answers that, and the Latvian attribution is the first case to state the mechanism explicitly on the record.

Outlook to 2030

$40 billion has been pledged over five years for layered counter-drone defence that will take years to field (The Kyiv Independent, Aug 2026). On this briefing's reading, incursions on NATO's eastern flank and navigation jamming in northern Europe both remain standing conditions through 2030.

What it means: Build resilience to positioning loss, and clear insurance wording, into operating plans now rather than waiting for new air defences.

Counter-argument

The safety picture has not deteriorated in step with the interference. Finland's regulator says aviation and maritime safety margins are holding, and Norway has responded with detection stations rather than interceptions. The Latvian attribution also cuts against an escalation reading: the aircraft destroyed was a displaced Ukrainian drone, not an attacking Russian one, so not every incursion is a deliberate attack.

4. The AI rules exempt defence, but probably not your dual-use product

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The European Commission and national market regulators · Room to act: High: firms decide how they classify products

The EU AI Act became generally applicable on 2 August 2026, although an amending law has since moved its high-risk rules to 2 December 2027. A widespread assumption in defence-adjacent industry is that the Article 2 carve-out puts military work outside the Regulation. The exemption attaches to the purpose for which a system is placed on the market, not to the identity of the company, and a system placed on the market for both military and civilian purposes is inside the Regulation.

  • Article 2(3) disapplies the Regulation only where a system is placed on the market, put into service or used exclusively for military, defence or national security purposes, regardless of the type of entity carrying out those activities (European Commission, AI Act Service Desk, Regulation text, a standing legal reference).
  • The recital states the dual-use position directly: AI systems placed on the market or put into service for an excluded purpose, namely military, defence or national security, and one or more non-excluded purposes, such as civilian purposes or law enforcement, fall within the scope of this Regulation and providers of those systems should ensure compliance with this Regulation. (European Commission, AI Act Service Desk, Recital 24, a standing legal reference).
  • Post-application guidance has not resolved it. The Commission's own FAQ, updated five days after the general application date, restates only that AI systems that are exclusively designed for military, defence or national security purposes are also exempt, regardless of the type of entity carrying out those activities. and is silent on dual-use (European Commission, August 2026).
  • Suppliers are already being advised accordingly. Named German counsel state that a supplier offering an AI system on the civilian market and also supplying it to the Bundeswehr needs full AI Act compliance, and that the Cyber Resilience Act exemption turns on the manufacturer having developed the product exclusively for defence purposes (LUTZ ABEL, March 2026, German).
  • Academic reading agrees the text is narrow while warning the boundary is unstable: AI systems used for both civilian and military, defense, or national security purposes remain within the scope of the Regulation insofar as they are deployed for civilian, humanitarian, law-enforcement, or public-security functions., with no operational definition of national security to fix the edge (European Governance Lab, June 2026).

Outlook to 2030

An amending law has moved the AI Act's high-risk application date to 2 December 2027 (Cuatrecasas, Jul 2026), so the main obligations for dual-use AI products arrive inside this planning horizon. On this briefing's reading, by 2030 classifying a product as dual-use will be a routine step in bringing it to market.

What it means: Classify dual-use products before the high-risk rules apply, not after.

Counter-argument

There is an asymmetry in industry's favour. A system placed on the market for civilian or law-enforcement purposes and later used, with or without modification, for military purposes does not fall within scope. Civilian first, then military, is out; both at once is in. Practitioners argue the boundary bites in the other direction too, so that A system may initially fall outside the AI Act because it is developed for military purposes, but if it is then used, even briefly, for civilian purposes, the AI Act applies (Tech Policy Press, March 2026). The absence of a settled definition of national security leaves member states room to widen it in practice.

5. The defence-tech boom is funding very few winners

Near-Term

Weight: low · Who decides: Investors and procurement agencies · Room to act: High for investors; low for others

Funding for defence technology is at record levels, but exits are less settled than the headline totals suggest: broad exit records come mostly from companies outside defence, while defence-specific sources describe slowing trade sales and listings still to come. What the 2026 data shows most clearly is concentration: a few companies take most of the money, and conversion into contracted programmes of record is visible only at the very top of the market.

  • Funding has already broken the annual record with seven months to run: Already this year, more than $14.6 billion in venture investment has gone into companies in Crunchbase's military, national security and law enforcement categories, past the previous full-year record of $9.6bn (Crunchbase News, June 2026).
  • Broad exit data look healthier than the defence-specific picture: Even excluding SpaceX's $1.69 trillion IPO, H1 exit value reached roughly $72 billion, already exceeding every prior full year, with major exits including Cerebras Systems, X-energy, Armis, Fervo Energy, and HawkEye 360. (PitchBook, July 2026), but most of the named exits are outside defence, so this shows the exit market reopening rather than defence exits recovering.
  • But the distribution is extreme, with Anduril's $5bn Series H alone accounting for roughly a third of second-quarter deal value and the top ten rounds taking 62%.
  • Conversion into large contracts is becoming a pattern, but so far it is visible mainly at the top of the market. The US Army awarded Anduril a contract with a ceiling of up to $20bn opening with an $87m task order, and Massive enterprise agreements with tech providers are no longer one-off flukes; there is now a pattern of VC-backed platforms winning prime-like enterprise deals that let them compete directly with the old guard. (Fortune, March 2026). A ceiling is permission to spend, not spend.
  • Europe's own fund is candid about the gap: While capital continues to flow into a sector that has never been more strategically important, investment alone doesn't automatically translate into stronger European defence capabilities. What matters is how effectively DSR startups and scaleups are turning funding into real technologies, real contracts, and real operational impact. (NATO Innovation Fund with Dealroom, February 2026, a window exception).
  • Investment and exits have decoupled: record venture funding in 2025 came alongside slowing acquisition activity, so the trade-sale route to an exit lost momentum (S&P Global Market Intelligence, March 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Leading German venture investors expect a wave of defence listings within 24 months (Handelsblatt, Feb 2026, German). On this briefing's reading, if that wave arrives, by 2030 a small group of listed defence-technology companies holds most programmes of record, with a long tail of firms holding prototypes.

What it means: Judge holdings and suppliers by contracted programmes, not by funding raised.

Counter-argument

The exit question is not settled either way. German venture investors reported almost no exits after two years of heavy investment and expect a listing wave only within 24 months (Handelsblatt, February 2026, German), and acquisition activity slowed through 2025 (S&P Global Market Intelligence, March 2026). If that listing wave does not arrive, the problem is a shortage of exits as well as concentration. Readers should treat any single funding total with care: the four figures cited across this cycle come from four vendors with different sector definitions and are not comparable with one another.

6. Japan is now openly planning for a long war

Longer-Range

Weight: low · Who decides: Japan, its allies, cable owners and insurers · Room to act: Moderate: firms choose routes and cover

Japan's 2026 Defense White Paper, distributed to cabinet on 4 August, moves from deterrence to sustainment: planning for a long conflict rather than a short one. The shift is regional, and Australia has already acted on it by releasing fuel reserves. The commercial constraints it exposes are live now for anyone with shipping, manufacturing or data routes through the region: cable repair capacity, the cable-vessel fleet and conflict insurance.

  • The Defence Minister described a security environment that is the most severe and complex since the war, and states hurrying to prepare for new ways of warfare including mass drone employment and for long wars (Ministry of Defense, Japan, August 2026, Japanese).
  • The commercial transmission channel is subsea. cables in the Southwest Pacific region (including the South China Sea) will require 141 repairs (representing 49 percent of global faults) in 2040., against a world fleet of 62 cable vessels of which only 19 are contracted for maintenance, with repair-vessel insurance premiums up as much as 50% for conflict-zone entry (CSIS, March 2026).
  • Fuel reserves are already being treated as a live instrument elsewhere in the region: Australia's National Cabinet agreed to release up to 20% of diesel and fuel reserves two weeks into a Middle East conflict, and The Commonwealth will appoint Anthea Harris as Fuel Supply Taskforce Coordinator to support coordination across governments and sectors. (Prime Minister of Australia, March 2026).

Outlook to 2030

CSIS forecasts that cables in the Southwest Pacific will need 141 repairs in 2040, 49% of global faults, against a world fleet of 62 cable vessels (CSIS, Mar 2026). On this briefing's reading, repair capacity and conflict insurance, not defence budgets, set the commercial cost of the region's security to 2030.

What it means: Price route diversity and repair time into Asia-Pacific data and shipping plans now.

Counter-argument

Japan has not funded the doctrine it has written. The 2026 budget request contained no new procurement of PAC-3 MSE interceptors, against a wargame scenario consuming roughly 318 of them in about one week to counter 508 incoming missiles, and Peacetime stockpiling is therefore more important for Japan than it has been for Ukraine. (Hudson Institute, June 2026). That qualifies Japan, but not the regional shift, which Australia's fuel-reserve release shows independently, nor the cable and insurance constraints, which are set by fleet capacity and underwriting.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Screen the bill of materials against the 0.1% threshold before 10 November

The test is mechanical and needs no view on Chinese policy. For each product line, find the components containing permanent magnets, phosphors, polishing compounds or specialist alloys, and ask the supplier where the rare earth content was processed; where a supplier will not answer, that is itself the finding.

Action: the chief operating officer or procurement lead to produce a product-level exposure list by 9 October 2026, a month before the expiry, with named single points of failure. Decide Theme 1

Implication 2: Reprice the assumption that defence demand is somebody else's competition

A decade of guaranteed defence orders now sits on an industrial base that also serves civil manufacturing, and France has legislated priority for military orders. Lead-time and labour assumptions to 2030 for castings, forgings, energetics, power electronics and skilled trades should be re-run against a scenario in which defence orders are served first.

Action: operations and capital planning to re-run 2027 to 2030 lead-time and labour assumptions against a defence-priority scenario, reporting at the next capital committee. Prepare Themes 2 and 5

Implication 3: Establish whether any product you sell is dual-use in the regulatory sense

The AI Act exemption follows the purpose for which a product is placed on the market, not the company selling it. On the current legal reading, an AI-enabled product offered to civilian customers and to a defence ministry needs full compliance, and the Cyber Resilience Act and NIS2 do not switch off in step.

Action: general counsel and the product owner to classify every AI-enabled product against Article 2(3) and Recital 24, and to record the answer, by 31 December 2026. Decide Theme 4

Implication 4: Treat navigation denial as an operating condition, not an incident

Satellite-navigation interference across northern Europe is continuous. Organisations running aircraft, vessels, offshore assets, surveying equipment or timing-dependent systems in the Baltic, Nordic and Black Sea regions should test what fails without positioning, and check how a loss caused by jamming rather than attack would be insured.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

Losses caused by jamming become an insurance class of their own

Window: 2030 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Insurers sort losses into accidents and acts of war, and a loss caused by navigation jamming has no attacker.

The drone Latvia destroyed was most probably pushed off course by Russian electronic warfare (LSM.lv, Aug 2026, Latvian), and Finland records satellite-navigation interference around the clock (Traficom, Apr 2026, Finnish).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2029: a court or regulator in Europe rules on who is liable for a loss caused by navigation jamming.

What it touches today: Insurance cover for aircraft, vessels and offshore assets in the Baltic and Nordic regions.

Licences for Chinese rare earths become a permanent feature of trade

Window: 2030 to 2037 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Cable repair capacity, not navies, decides Asia-Pacific connectivity in a crisis

What We Are Not Planning For

Escalation

A NATO Article 5 contingency arising from the current incursion pattern

Nothing in this year's interceptions shows a deliberate campaign against NATO territory, and planning for Article 5 would not improve any decision available today.

↺ Reinstated if an incursion produces casualties on NATO soil, or if two or more member states invoke Article 4 consultations within a 30-day window.

Supply

A complete Chinese embargo on rare earth exports to Western buyers

A full embargo would hurt Chinese processors and speed up the diversification Beijing wants to slow. The instrument in play is licensing, so this briefing plans for rationing and delay.

↺ Reinstated if MOFCOM suspends licence issuance to a named allied jurisdiction, or if export volumes to the EU or United States fall by more than half in a single quarter.

Regulation

A blanket European exemption that puts all defence-adjacent AI outside the Regulation

No authoritative reading supports a broad exemption, and the Commission's guidance declined to widen it. Waiting for a carve-out would leave a supplier non-compliant in the meantime.

↺ Reinstated if the Commission or the AI Office issues guidance explicitly bringing mixed-purpose defence products outside scope, or if an amending regulation is tabled.

Claim-Fidelity Appendix

Where this briefing extrapolates beyond its sources

Three claims go further than any single source states. First, that firms outside defence are broadly unaware of their exposure to the 0.1% rule: no survey of bill-of-materials screening exists, so this is inference from the enforcement literature and from the absence of any published count, and it is flagged rather than asserted. Second, that defence demand will materially lengthen civil lead times: France has legislated priority and backlogs are growing faster than sales, but no source quantifies the civil effect. Third, that stated doctrine precedes budget in the Japanese case: this is an analyst's reading of how defence planning documents usually behave, offered against evidence that FY2026 contained no new interceptor procurement.

Where sources disagree

The four defence-tech funding totals cited come from four vendors with different sector definitions and are not comparable. Crunchbase reports $14.6bn over five months on a narrow, US-weighted definition; PitchBook reports $35.4bn for H1 on a broad definition that sweeps in space and dual-use; Dealroom reports $8.7bn for Europe in 2025. They are presented separately and never netted. Several outlets republishing the Crunchbase series were excluded as non-independent.

Where a correction was made before publication

An initial reading of a parliamentary summary attributed £7.3bn to the UK Digital Targeting Web. The Defence Investment Plan itself gives £1.8bn in Table 4, with a total of £297.7bn in Table 1. The primary document governs and the figure was removed.

Stated limits

Arctic Sentry could not be corroborated from a primary NATO source in this cycle and is not relied on. Shipping and marine-insurance exposure to GNSS denial is thinly evidenced here, with the strongest available source falling outside the window. No 2026 Korean defence white paper exists, so Korea qualifies rather than supports any region-wide claim. One source, the Japanese Ministry of Defense, dates itself only in the imperial era and is attested separately in the cycle's audit overrides. Non-English sources are quoted in English translation in the body and named with their language in the citation; the original-language spans are held in the evidence base rather than set inline, where they broke the reading line.
