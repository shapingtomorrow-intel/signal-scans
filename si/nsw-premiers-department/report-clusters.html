Why this matters now

NSW is cutting the number of senior executives and asking what capabilities the survivors need. The published numbers suggest the second question is downstream of a first one nobody has answered in public: which parts of the sector actually carry fewer leaders now, and which carry more.

Three things are happening at once. The executive layer is being reshaped very unevenly. Executives are being made accountable for work produced by systems they cannot personally inspect, and the Auditor-General has already found that accountability is not evidenced. And the junior end of the workforce, where public-sector judgement was traditionally learned, is thinning while the senior end stays put for longer.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: a capability model cannot fix a distribution problem, and NSW currently has both.

The NSW Leadership Academy is being asked to design for 2030 at the moment the shape of the leadership population is changing faster than the content of leadership roles. Decisions taken in the next two budget cycles about who is developed, where, and to what standard will be made against an executive layer that has already moved, and against an assurance obligation that is about to be audited.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week assume the senior executive reduction was spread across the sector rather than concentrated in one service?

Transport Service accounts for 199 of the 244 FTE reduction in 2025, while Public Service senior executives rose by 30 FTE and the Premier and Cabinet portfolio rose from 154 to 214 FTE (NSW Premier’s Department workforce data, January 2026).

The brief that prompted this cycle asks what capabilities will matter most for NSW public sector leaders by 2030. It is the wrong first question, and the government's own workforce data shows why. At June 2025 the public sector employed 4,121 FTE senior executives, down from 4,365 (NSW Public Sector Report 2025, January 2026). That reduction is real. It is also almost entirely one service. Transport Service senior executives fell from 1,126 to 926 FTE, a fall of 199.4 FTE, against a total public sector reduction of 244.1 FTE. Public Service senior executives rose, from 2,595 to 2,625. The Premier and Cabinet portfolio rose from 154 to 214 FTE (additional workforce profile data, January 2026).

So the sector has not been flattened. One service has been restructured, and the centre has grown. Any capability answer built on the premise of a uniformly thinner executive layer will be designing for a sector that does not exist. The prior public framing, that headcount fell 8.3 per cent from a June 2023 peak against a 15 per cent first-term target (NSW Government, January 2026), is accurate at the aggregate and misleading at the level where development decisions are actually made.

So the sector has not been flattened. One service has been restructured, and the centre has grown.

The second thing that changed is the nature of executive accountability. NSW already mandates that every use of AI apply the AI Assessment Framework, with high and critical systems referred to an independent review committee (Digital NSW). On 12 August the Auditor-General reported that across the 26 largest agencies, governance is not keeping pace with the speed of adoption and agencies did not consistently register all AI use cases (Audit Office of New South Wales, August 2026). A whole-of-government performance audit of agencies' AI systems already sits in the three-year work plan (Audit Office of New South Wales, July 2026). Executives will shortly have to evidence, in front of Parliament, control over work they did not do and cannot personally check.

The third change is at the other end of the workforce. NSW job advertisements fell from 37,511 in 2024 to 17,757 in 2025 and openings from 47,915 to 29,916 (additional workforce profile data, January 2026), while median agency tenure fell to 6.4 years from 6.9 (NSW Public Sector Report 2025, January 2026). Only 5.0 per cent of the sector is aged 15 to 24, against 14.9 per cent of NSW employed persons. At the same time the senior end is staying: retirements at age 60 fell from 725 in 2016 to 277 in 2025, while retirements at 67 rose from 176 to 477. The bench is ageing in place.

Read together, these are not three workforce facts. They describe a service whose leadership population is being redistributed rather than reduced, whose accountability is being extended into work that cannot be inspected, and whose replacement pipeline is narrowing while its incumbents stay longer. Capability content is the last of those four problems to bind, and the only one the Academy currently owns.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The load-bearing assumption in this report is that the 2025 service and portfolio splits describe a deliberate redistribution rather than a one-off restructuring artefact. Transport Service was in the middle of a large reorganisation, and a single year of census FTE cannot distinguish a policy pattern from a machinery-of-government event. If the 2026 workforce profile shows Public Service executive numbers falling and the Premier and Cabinet portfolio returning toward 154 FTE, the distribution reading in Theme 1 weakens sharply and the capability question becomes the right first question after all. We would revise on that evidence, and it arrives in about five months.

Themes

The cycle's signals are organised into six themes, ranked by impact on NSW Premier's Department decisions over the next two to five years.

1. Fewer executives, and the work did not shrink with them

Immediate

The Academy should not design a capability answer until it knows where the leadership population actually is. In 2025 the NSW public sector shed 244.1 FTE senior executives, and 199.4 of those came from a single service. Public Service senior executives went up. The centre went up faster than anything else went down in proportional terms. That is a distribution question, and it determines which cohorts need what, in which agencies, before any question of curriculum arises.

Where the 2025 senior executive reduction fell, census FTE change by service

Source basis: NSW Premier's Department, additional workforce profile data, Table 3.1 (January 2026).

  • Senior executives fell to 4,121 FTE in 2025 from 4,365, with women at 48.4 per cent of the cohort (NSW Public Sector Report 2025, January 2026).
  • Transport Service fell from 1,126 to 926 FTE, a fall of 17.7 per cent, while Public Service rose from 2,595 to 2,625 and the Premier and Cabinet portfolio rose from 154 to 214 FTE (additional workforce profile data, January 2026).
  • The government's public framing is an 8.3 per cent fall from a June 2023 peak against a 15 per cent first-term target and savings above $100 million a year (NSW Government, January 2026).
  • The sector was growing while the executive layer shrank: headcount rose 2.5 per cent in 2025 to about 470,000 (Government News, February 2026), and NSW state government employee jobs rose from 557,300 to 566,100 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, November 2025).
  • Employee-related costs reached 43.5 per cent of total state operating expenses in 2024-25, up 4.9 per cent (Audit Office of New South Wales, December 2025), so executive headcount is a small lever on a large and rising bill.

Counter-argument

The concentration may be an artefact rather than a pattern. Transport Service was undergoing a large reorganisation, and one year of census FTE cannot separate a machinery-of-government event from a deliberate redistribution. There is also a reading in the other direction: reporting on the pre-budget announcement argues the roles removed sit below the senior executive band altogether (The Mandarin, June 2026), which would make the aggregate figure softer than either interpretation assumes. The UK offers a cautionary analogue: a year into an explicit administrative-cost reduction programme, civil service headcount reached 557,825 and the share at Executive Officer grade and above rose again to 75.5 per cent (Cabinet Office, July 2026). Announced reductions and realised grade mix are different things.

2. Assurance, not adoption, is the AI leadership job

Immediate

NSW has already written the obligation. Every use of AI must run the AIAF, high and critical systems go to an independent review committee, and the Auditor-General has now found that agencies cannot show it is working. The capability this puts on executives is not fluency with the tools. It is the ability to take accountability for output they did not produce and cannot personally inspect, and to evidence that accountability to Parliament. No NSW capability level names that.

  • All NSW government use of AI across the full lifecycle must apply the AIAF under Circular DCS-2024-04, with high and critical systems referred to the AI Review Committee (Digital NSW).
  • The AIAF was rebuilt in January 2026 to cut assessment time from days to under 30 minutes and route higher-risk systems automatically (NSW Government, January 2026).
  • Across the 26 largest agencies the Auditor-General found weaknesses at 15, repeat findings rising from 33 to 42 per cent of the total, and no consistent registration of AI use cases (Audit Office of New South Wales, August 2026).
  • A whole-of-government performance audit of agencies' AI systems is scheduled in the three-year plan (Audit Office of New South Wales, July 2026), and NSW aligned the AIAF to the national assurance instrument agreed by the Data and Digital Ministers Meeting (Department of Finance, June 2024).
  • The UK's national auditor has published guidance aimed squarely at audit and risk assurance committees rather than at delivery teams (National Audit Office, May 2026), which is where the accountable conversation is landing.

Counter-argument

The OECD's read is that assurance is not the binding constraint at all. Across 36 countries, 35 already use AI in government but only 14 require pre-deployment risk assessment and 11 conduct post-deployment audits, and the OECD names skills gaps rather than assurance gaps as the most common barrier to successful adoption (OECD, June 2026). On that view NSW has over-built the control layer and under-built the capability to use the technology at all, and an Academy that teaches assurance will be equipping executives to supervise something their agencies never deploy. The counter has force on outcomes. It has less force on accountability: the audit is scheduled either way, and executives will answer for the register before they answer for the benefit.

3. The rung where judgement was learned is being removed

Near-Term

Public-sector judgement has historically been acquired below the executive band, on the job rather than in a classroom. That rung is thinning, and NSW now shows it in its own data. Why it is thinning is genuinely open: the strongest single estimate available attributes most of the early-career collapse to remote work rather than to automation, other institutional work points to AI absorbing routine junior tasks, and fiscal restraint removes coordination roles first in any case. The Academy's exposure does not depend on which mechanism dominates. It depends on the observable, which is that fewer people are arriving, staying or moving, so the organisation produces less judgement as a by-product of work than it used to.

  • NSW job advertisements fell from 37,511 in 2024 to 17,757 in 2025 and openings from 47,915 to 29,916 (additional workforce profile data, January 2026), with the report attributing part of the fall to Education roles filled internally before advertisement.
  • Median agency tenure fell to 6.4 years from 6.9, and time to hire barely moved at 42.9 days (NSW Public Sector Report 2025, January 2026).
  • In the Australian Public Service the probability of reaching EL1 or above fell 23 per cent in two years and EL2-to-SES odds narrowed from one in 38 to one in 47 (The Canberra Times, April 2026).
  • The UK's flagship graduate pipeline has roughly halved, from a 2018 peak of 1,411 recommendations to 754 for the 2025 cohort, against a record 72,691 applications (Institute for Government).
  • The best-identified estimate of the cause points away from automation: remote work explains 64 per cent of the rise in unemployment among young college graduates, and the deterioration predates rapid AI diffusion (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, June 2026). If proximity rather than automation is the mechanism, the remedy is how work is supervised, not what is taught.
  • The competing institutional reading is that junior roles must be redesigned around judgement rather than removed, or the talent pyramid breaks: US entry-level postings fell about 35 per cent in 18 months, with the work migrating upward to managers (World Economic Forum, March 2026).
  • Whichever mechanism dominates, the loss is invisible to current succession systems: only 22 per cent of organisations plan succession with AI readiness in mind, and fewer than three in ten have visibility of potential at mid and emerging-leader level (Korn Ferry, May 2026). Judgement accrues before promotion, so the shortfall surfaces years after the decision that caused it.
  • Manager headcount across the wider labour market fell 6.1 per cent between May 2022 and May 2025 (Forbes, June 2026).

Counter-argument

The rung may not be thinning at all. The NSW report attributes part of the fall in advertisements to Education roles filled through internal processes before advertisement (NSW Public Sector Report 2025, January 2026), which would make the halving a change in recruitment method rather than a narrowing of entry. The UK case cuts the same way: a year into an explicit cost-reduction programme, civil service staffing reached 555,000, its highest since 2006, and the auditor's finding is that workforce planning cannot yet model grade-mix effects at all (National Audit Office, July 2026). On that reading the pipeline is being reshaped by hiring practice and measurement gaps, not depleted, and a development response built on scarcity would be solving a problem the data has not established. The NSW tenure fall, from 6.9 to 6.4 years, is the harder number to explain away, and is why this theme stays in the cycle.

4. Leadership development returns in-house

Near-Term

Comparable services have decided this, and they have decided it the same way. The UK is standing up a national school and its head of service has placed it at the centre of a named reform programme rather than treating it as training. Australia runs an academy alongside an explicit in-sourcing rule with a dollar figure attached. In both cases leadership capability is being treated as core infrastructure to be built inside the service, because the reform agenda depends on it. NSW currently sits in the middle, with an in-house Academy and leadership standard alongside purchased ANZSOG and university provision, and has not made the choice explicitly. The question for the Premier's Department is whether to commit on the same reasoning, and what it costs to hold that commitment through a budget cycle.

  • The UK's head of service frames the new national school as core capability infrastructure for a named service-wide reform programme, not as a training add-on (UK Civil Service, May 2026), which is the strategic argument for building rather than buying.
  • It is on the parliamentary record: announced 20 January 2026 and scheduled to launch later in the year, though no funding envelope or governance model has been disclosed (UK Parliament, March 2026).
  • Cross-government learning is already being in-housed ahead of the school, with courses developed by civil servants for civil servants and leadership courses made free to book from April 2026 (Government Skills, May 2026).
  • Australia has made the same call deliberately: the APS Academy builds craft capability inside the service, and under the Strategic Commissioning Framework agencies planned to bring $527,553,088 of core work in-house in 2024-25 (Australian Public Service Commission), with capability assessment run as a permanent central function staffed by serving senior public servants rather than consultants (Australian Public Service Commission).
  • NSW runs Executive Excellence, Aboriginal Career and Leadership Development and Executive Essentials in-house while directing executives to ANZSOG and university programmes (NSW Public Service Commission).

Counter-argument

The disconfirming case is Canada, and it is a serious one. Its mature in-house school plans spending down from $117,006,926 forecast in 2025-26 to $88,055,308 in 2026-27 and $80,282,397 by 2028-29, with about 66 fewer full-time equivalents, funding for the Advanced Leadership Program sunsetting, and supervisor-reported improvement at 71.0 per cent against a 75 per cent target (Canada School of Public Service, February 2026). A service can decide leadership capability is core infrastructure and still cut it four years later, because an in-house school is a standing cost line with published indicators while a purchasing relationship is not. That does not weaken the case for building. It prices it: committing in-house means committing to defend a budget annually, which is a governance decision for the Premier's Department rather than a design decision for the Academy.

5. Pay, performance and removal are being wired to delivery

Near-Term

A capability model binds only where it connects to how leaders are paid, promoted and removed. The UK has just made that connection explicit for its senior service. NSW has moved in the other direction, from an executive pay freeze to a tribunal-determined uplift with no performance lever attached. The Academy can define capability; it cannot make capability consequential on its own.

  • The UK set the SCS paybill up 3.5 per cent for 2026-27 but base pay at 2.5 per cent, ring-fencing 1 per cent for the first performance-based progression for the senior service (Cabinet Office, May 2026).
  • Progression payments run from £750 to £4,000 on moderated ratings, and consolidated awards are withheld from anyone under formal poor performance procedures (Cabinet Office, May 2026).
  • The performance system sets an expected distribution of 15 per cent Exceeded, 20 per cent High Performing, 60 per cent Achieving and 5 per cent Partially Met, and asks departments to record dismissals for poor performance and other indicators of moving poor performers out (Civil Service, May 2026).
  • NSW senior executive remuneration for 2026-27 adjusts discretionary ranges and job evaluation formulas for a 3 per cent tribunal determination, within statutory bands (Office of the Public Service Commissioner, July 2026).

Counter-argument

Australia already demonstrates that rigorous senior appraisal can be built with no pay consequence at all. Departmental secretaries are assessed annually against statutory roles and a leadership charter with a 270-degree behavioural evaluation, yet individual performance does not influence classification or pay point and bonuses are not available (Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet, November 2025). That is a serious counter-position: the wiring may be unnecessary, and the UK experiment is untested in core public-service roles where outputs are hard to observe. This report's reading is that the direction of instrument design has changed in one comparable service and not in NSW, and that the difference will show up in what leaders optimise for, not necessarily in what they achieve.

6. The succession arithmetic behind an ageing executive cohort

Longer-Range

Behind the capability question sits an arithmetic one. The NSW sector has a median age of 43 and recruits young people at about a third of the rate of the NSW labour market, while its senior cohort now leaves roughly seven years later than it did in 2016. That combination does not produce a retirement cliff. It produces something harder to plan for: a bench that neither turns over nor renews, where development spend has to work on people who will still be in post in 2035.

  • Median age is 43 across the public sector and 44 in the Public Service, with 48.1 per cent of non-casual employees aged over 43, and 15 to 24 year olds at 5.0 per cent against 14.9 per cent of NSW employed persons (additional workforce profile data, January 2026).
  • Retirements at age 55 fell from 40 in 2016 to 14 in 2025 and at 60 from 725 to 277, while retirements at 67 rose from 176 to 477.
  • Separation from agency was 9.5 per cent across the public sector, exit from the sector 8.6 per cent and movement within it 0.8 per cent, so internal mobility is close to negligible.
  • The Australian Public Service shows the same shape: 45.4 per cent aged 45 or older, 5.4 per cent under 25, and engagement, mobility and separation rates all lower than the prior year (Australian Public Service Commission, November 2025).
  • New Zealand's service reached an average age of 44.6 years with new recruits at 12.5 per cent of all employees, the lowest since 2010 (Te Kawa Mataaho Public Service Commission).

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalInternal movement within the NSW public sector has fallen to 0.8 per cent of employees, against 8.6 per cent exiting the sector entirely. If mobility is the mechanism by which cross-agency judgement is built, it has effectively stopped. This would gain weight if the 2026 workforce profile shows movement below 1 per cent for a second year while exit stays above 8 per cent.
  • Weak signalThe Premier and Cabinet portfolio grew 39 per cent in senior executive FTE in a year of sector-wide reduction. If the 2026 profile shows the same portfolio growing again while others fall, the pattern is a central-agency consolidation rather than a Transport restructuring, and Theme 1 becomes a governance question rather than a workforce one.

Counter-argument

The replacement schedule is softening rather than binding. Australians aged 45 and over now intend to retire at 65.6 years on average, up from 65.4 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, October 2025), and the NSW retirement distribution confirms it locally. A cohort that stays is a cohort that can be developed, and the sector gains years of institutional memory it would otherwise lose. The risk is not a cliff but a plateau: the same people in the same roles, with fewer arrivals underneath, which is a slower and more manageable problem than the demographic framing usually implies.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle into the 2027-28 capability plan. Each names the move, the owner and the trigger.

SI 1: Settle the distribution question before committing the 2027-28 programme mix

The Academy is being asked to answer a capability question against a leadership population whose shape changed sharply in one year and unevenly across services. The Premier's Department should commission and publish senior executive counts by service, portfolio and band for 2024, 2025 and 2026, so that development investment can be targeted where the layer actually thinned rather than spread evenly across a sector that did not thin evenly. This is a Decide because the 2027-28 programme mix is set inside this budget cycle and cannot be re-cut afterwards.

Action: Publish the three-year executive distribution by service, portfolio and band before the 2027-28 programme mix is signed off.

Decide Draws on Theme 1

SI 2: Name an owner for assurance of machine-assisted decisions at executive level

A performance audit of agency AI systems is scheduled, the Auditor-General has already found registration and cost tracking inconsistent, and the obligation sits on agencies rather than on technologists. Secretaries should name, in writing, the executive accountable for the AI audit record in each agency, and the Academy should build the explain-and-evidence capability into executive programmes rather than into digital training. Absent a pay or performance lever in NSW, naming the owner is the instrument available.

Action: Each agency names an executive owner of the AI audit record before the Audit Office fieldwork opens.

Decide Draws on Themes 2 and 5

SI 3: Choose whether NSW builds leadership development or buys it, and cost both

The UK is standing up a national school, Australia is in-sourcing core work at scale, and Canada is cutting a mature school and sunsetting its flagship leadership programme. NSW runs a hybrid. The Premier's Department should model both paths over five years, including what each looks like in an expenditure review, and decide deliberately rather than by drift. Prepare rather than Decide, because the UK school's funding and governance model has not been published and is worth watching before committing.

Action: Model in-house against purchased provision over five years, including an expenditure-review stress case, by the 2027-28 budget submission.

Prepare Draws on Themes 3, 4 and 6

SI 4: Track whether internal mobility recovers, and treat it as a development instrument

Movement within the NSW public sector ran at 0.8 per cent of employees against 8.6 per cent exiting the sector entirely, while advertised entry halved and the senior cohort retires later. If cross-agency movement is how judgement is built at pace, it has close to stopped, and no classroom substitutes for it. Monitor for now: one year of data cannot separate a hiring pause from a change in behaviour.

Action: Report internal movement and exit rates alongside development metrics in the next Academy performance review.

Monitor Draws on Themes 3 and 6

The Centre Holds

The executive layer stays concentrated in central agencies and a few large delivery services, and the AI performance audit lands hard. Leadership becomes an assurance function: a smaller number of executives carry accountability for a much wider surface of machine-assisted work, and the scarce capability is the ability to explain a decision to a committee. Development spend moves toward evidence, documentation and challenge skills, and away from generic executive presence. Agencies without a named owner of the AI audit record are exposed publicly. The Academy's value is measured by whether its graduates survive scrutiny.

Early indicators: A qualified or adverse AI performance audit finding; a named executive sanctioned over an AI-assisted decision; central agency executive numbers rising again in the 2026 profile.

Thin and Exposed

Executive numbers stay concentrated but external accountability stays light, and the audit produces recommendations rather than consequences. The risk here is quiet: a smaller executive layer absorbs a widening span of assured work without the scrutiny that would force capability investment, and the gap only surfaces when something fails. Development budgets are the first casualty of the next expenditure review, because nothing external is demanding the capability. The sector discovers the shortfall through an incident rather than an audit.

Early indicators: AI audit closes with recommendations and no findings; Academy budget reduced in the 2027-28 allocation; no agency publishes an AI use-case register.

Spread and Accountable

The reduction generalises across services in 2026 and 2027 while the audit bites. This is the most demanding environment and the one the current brief implicitly assumes: every service carries fewer executives, each with more assured surface, and capability requirements genuinely do differ by level because every level is carrying more. Development has to be delivered at scale and at pace, which favours in-house capacity over purchased programmes, and favours structured exposure over courses.

Early indicators: Public Service executive numbers fall in the 2026 profile; the 15 per cent target is met across services rather than in Transport; agencies publish AI registers ahead of fieldwork.

Slow Renewal

The reduction generalises but accountability stays light and retirement keeps deferring. The sector ages in place with fewer executives, little internal movement and a thin junior intake. Nothing breaks, and nothing renews. Capability models are rewritten periodically without changing who holds the roles, and the succession problem compounds quietly into the 2030s. This is the least dramatic scenario and the hardest to build a business case against, because its costs are all deferred.

Early indicators: Internal movement stays below 1 per cent for a second year; retirements at 67 and above keep rising; job advertisements do not recover from the 2025 fall.

What We Are Not Planning For

Political discontinuity

A change of government resetting the executive reduction programme

NSW goes to an election within this cycle's horizon and a different administration might abandon the 15 per cent target. We are not planning for it because the three findings that drive this report do not depend on the target. The audit obligation is statutory and already scheduled, the demographic profile is fixed for a decade, and the recruitment fall is a labour-market and budget phenomenon rather than a policy one.

↺ Reinstated if an incoming administration publishes a materially different senior executive headcount target with a funded recruitment programme attached.

Technology discontinuity

A step change in AI capability that removes the assurance problem

A sufficiently reliable and explainable system could in principle make the assurance question trivial. We are not planning for it because the obligation is procedural rather than technical: the AI Assessment Framework requires documented sign-off regardless of how good the system is, and the Auditor-General audits the record, not the model.

↺ Reinstated if the NSW AI Assessment Framework is amended to waive assessment for a defined class of systems.

Public trust shock

A collapse in public confidence forcing a leadership legitimacy agenda

An earlier draft of this cycle carried a theme on public trust and the legitimacy of machine-assisted decisions. It was set aside because the NSW-specific evidence is thin and the international trust data moves slowly. The capability implication, that executives must defend decisions in public, is already carried inside Theme 2 without needing a separate trust thesis.

↺ Reinstated if a NSW AI-assisted decision becomes the subject of a parliamentary inquiry or a sustained public campaign.

Discussion Points for the Leadership Team

  1. If the 2026 workforce profile shows the Premier and Cabinet portfolio growing again, what is the department's public account of applying executive discipline to everyone except itself?
  2. Would you rather have an Academy graduate who can design a capability model, or one who can survive two hours in front of the Public Accounts Committee explaining an AI-assisted decision?
  3. Internal movement is 0.8 per cent against 8.6 per cent exiting the sector entirely. Is that a hiring pause, or has cross-agency development effectively stopped?
  4. If NSW built a national-school equivalent, what would you cut to fund it, and would it survive the expenditure review that is currently cutting Canada's?
  5. Capability requirements by level assume the levels persist. If advertised entry stays at half its 2024 volume, which level disappears first, and what replaces the judgement it used to build?

The window is honoured unevenly and deliberately: 20 records are structural anchors, because Australian public-sector workforce evidence publishes on annual statutory cycles whose latest editions predate the window. Three sources were dropped at the audit gate for exceeding the permitted anchor age. The two load-bearing NSW records, the NSW Public Sector Report 2025 and its companion data workbook, were supplied directly by the analyst after the sourcing pass could not fetch them, and every figure drawn from them is traceable to a published table. Two sources conflict on the composition of the executive reduction: the government's own release presents it as a whole-of-sector achievement, while trade reporting argues the removed roles sit below the senior executive band. This report weights the published service and portfolio tables over both. Author-type coverage is thin on innovators, investors and practitioner-scientists; the base is regulators, auditors, statistical agencies and central agencies, which suits a governance question and would not suit a technology-adoption one.

Source tiers: Tier 1, governments, regulators and intergovernmental bodies. Tier 2, think-tanks, academic institutes, major consultancies and quality data providers. Tier 3, quality journalism and specialist trade press. Tier 4, vendor, company and practitioner sources, used only as directional corroboration.

Claim-fidelity self-disclosure

This appendix records every claim in the report that goes beyond what a cited source literally states.

Cycle-over-cycle continuity claims

None. This is the inaugural cycle for NSW Premier's Department, so there is no prior memory to trace against and no Continuity Ledger.

Superlatives used

The Premier and Cabinet portfolio is described as rising faster in proportional terms than any other portfolio in 2025. That is an analyst calculation from Table 3.2 of the published workbook, not a characterisation any source makes. Internal movement is described as close to negligible; the source reports 0.8 per cent without characterising it. New Zealand's new-recruit share is described by its own source as the lowest since 2010.

Analytical synthesis claims

The report's central claim, that the capability question is downstream of a distribution question, is an inference across the NSW workforce tables, the ministerial release and the trade reporting; no single source makes it. The reading that assurance rather than adoption is the binding executive capability draws on the NSW assessment regime, the Audit Office findings and the scheduled performance audit together, and is explicitly contested by the OECD source cited in Theme 2. The claim that the pipeline effect in Theme 3 holds whichever mechanism is correct is the analyst's, and is marked as such in the counter-argument. The Futures Wheel in the contents panel was authored to the generator's schema and passed with --tree, because the expansion model could not be reached from the composition environment; its nodes are analyst constructions grounded in the cycle's themes and carry no separate source record. The two figures in this report are analyst constructions built from published table values; they carry no separate source record.
