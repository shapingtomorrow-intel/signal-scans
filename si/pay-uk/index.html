At a glance

The UK is rebuilding the machinery of national payments at the same time as the country is asking that machinery to do more, more safely, for more kinds of money. The Payments Forward Plan has set the sequence, a new Delivery Company will build the next-generation interbank platform, and the regulator that oversees Pay.UK is itself being absorbed into the FCA.

Around that rebuild, three forces are pressing in at once: money is fragmenting into many forms (stablecoins, tokenised deposits, a possible digital pound), payments are becoming instant and commercial through open banking, and the safety of every payment now depends on identity and data layers that sit largely outside Pay.UK's walls. Fraud reimbursement has already moved the cost of getting this wrong onto payment providers.

For Pay.UK the task is not transformation or stewardship. It is both at once: keep Bacs and Faster Payments flawless while the platform beneath them, and the rules around them, are replaced. This briefing reads the six themes as one interlocking system and sets out where the decisions can no longer wait.

1. Executive Synthesis

Which assumptions in your three-year plan still treat Pay.UK as the fixed point the system is built around?
The evidence in this cycle says Pay.UK is now the moving part: the platform, the regulator and the forms of money it carries are all changing at once.

Two things became settled fact this cycle. First, the National Payments Vision moved from ambition to delivery sequence: the Payments Vision Delivery Committee published the Payments Forward Plan, retiring the New Payments Architecture in favour of an industry-led Interbank Infrastructure Renewal, standing up a new Delivery Company to procure and build, and confirming the Payment Systems Regulator's consolidation into the FCA (FCA, February 2026). Pay.UK keeps its role as operator of the existing interbank systems, with a mandate sharpened toward resilience and optimisation of Bacs and Faster Payments (HM Treasury, February 2026). Second, account-to-account payments crossed from utility into commercial product: the UK Payments Initiative launched the first industry commercial-VRP scheme (FCA, June 2026), on rails Pay.UK runs, with standard VRP already at about 5.5 million payments a month (UK Finance, 2026).

Pay.UK's defining task this cycle is not transformation or stewardship; it is both at once, keeping the rails flawless while the platform beneath them is replaced.

Beneath those headlines, money itself is multiplying. The Bank of England is finalising a systemic-stablecoin regime (Bank of England, November 2025), softening its holding-limit stance by May (Fox Williams, May 2026); the ECB has closed the digital-euro preparation phase ahead of a parliamentary vote (European Central Bank, October 2025); the US has enacted the GENIUS Act dollar-stablecoin framework (The White House, July 2025); and the BIS has shown tokenised commercial-bank and central-bank money settling on one platform (Bank for International Settlements, May 2026). The strategic consequence is singular: Pay.UK's platform must become a money-agnostic settlement layer or risk being routed around. Meanwhile the safety of instant payments has migrated upstream into identity and verification (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, December 2025), and fraud reimbursement has repriced failure onto providers, with 88% of APP losses (about £173m) reimbursed in year one (Payment Systems Regulator, 2025).

Where this analysis could be wrong

The central judgment is that concentration during the rebuild is a fragility. That is falsifiable: if the Payments Forward Plan delivers clean sequencing with a single, named point of accountability for the transition, the same concentration becomes a strategic advantage, letting the UK move to a money-agnostic platform faster than fragmented markets can. The briefing would also be wrong if account-to-account adoption stalls on user trust, in which case the instant-economy theme is over-weighted and the cash and inclusion theme under-weighted.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle
  1. Name the transition accountability. Agree, in writing, how risk and decision rights split between Pay.UK, the Delivery Company and a merging regulator before build begins, because an accountability gap is the single largest systemic risk.
  2. Commit to money-agnostic settlement. Make the renewed platform's design explicitly able to settle tokenised deposits, regulated stablecoins and a potential digital pound, or accept the risk of disintermediation.
  3. Treat trusted data as core infrastructure. Define Pay.UK's dependency on identity and verification layers as a first-order resilience exposure, not a partnership footnote.

3. Signal Themes

Operating the core through its own reconstruction Immediate

Pay.UK's institutional position, not just its technology, is being redrawn this cycle, and the redraw lands before the new platform exists.

The Payments Forward Plan retires the New Payments Architecture, creates an industry-led Delivery Company to build next-generation interbank infrastructure, and consolidates the PSR into the FCA, while confirming Pay.UK as operator of the existing systems (FCA, February 2026; Lewis Silkin, March 2026). National rails are simultaneously being knitted into cross-border networks: the ECB is interlinking TIPS with India's UPI and the BIS Nexus initiative (European Central Bank, November 2025), and Nexus Global Payments, incorporated by five founding central banks, is set to go live by 2026 (Bank for International Settlements, 2025). The EU has also made instant euro payments universal and name-checking mandatory through Verification of Payee (European Central Bank, October 2025), the template the UK is measured against.

Counter-argument: the structure may be a strength, not a strain. A clean split between an operator (Pay.UK) and a delivery vehicle, under one regulator, could move faster than the stalled NPA ever did. The risk is execution and accountability during hand-over, not the design.

Decision link: SI 1 (transition accountability), SI 2 (money-agnostic platform).

The instant economy moves from utility to commercial product Immediate

The economics of immediacy are now contestable, and they run over the Faster Payments rail Pay.UK operates.

The UK Payments Initiative launched the first industry commercial-VRP scheme, with 31 founding firms including every major retail bank, and the FCA will consult on a long-term open-banking framework by end-2026 (FCA, June 2026). Standard VRP already runs at about 5.5 million payments a month, over 16% of Pay by Bank volume (UK Finance, 2026), and 2026 is widely read as the year account-to-account turns commercial (The Payments Association, 2026).

Counter-argument: adoption, not capability, is the binding constraint. cVRP could stall if merchant economics and consumer trust do not materialise (TrueLayer, 2026), in which case the threat to card networks is slower than the launch implies.

Decision link: SI 2, SI 3 (trusted-data dependency).

Bad money and bad actors: fraud repriced onto the rails Near-Term

Reimbursement has turned fraud from a customer problem into a balance-sheet problem for payment providers, and the threat originates outside payments.

In year one of mandatory APP reimbursement, 88% of losses, about £173m, were reimbursed under a cap up to £85,000, with most claims settled within five business days (Payment Systems Regulator, 2025). Fraud is now the most common crime and a cross-sector problem (House of Commons Library, March 2026), while stablecoins have become a leading channel for illicit flows and sanctions evasion (CoinDesk, reporting FATF, March 2026; FATF, June 2025).

Counter-argument: reimbursement may be self-correcting. By making providers bear the cost, it has sharpened prevention incentives at the sending and receiving banks; the rail operator's role could remain enabling rather than accountable for losses originating elsewhere.

Decision link: SI 3, SI 4 (cross-sector fraud).

The importance of trusted data Near-Term

As payments become instant and irrevocable, their safety migrates into identity and verification layers that sit largely outside Pay.UK's perimeter.

The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 established a statutory Digital Verification Services trust framework (in force December 2025) and GOV.UK One Login is consolidating government identity (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, December 2025). The EU has set the cross-border benchmark, requiring every member state to offer a Digital Identity Wallet by the end of 2026 (European Commission, April 2024; EU Perspectives, January 2026), binding verified identity directly to payments (Kennedys, 2026).

Counter-argument: this is an opportunity, not just a dependency. If Pay.UK helps anchor verification at the rail (building on Confirmation of Payee), trusted data becomes a source of resilience and differentiation rather than an external single point of failure.

Decision link: SI 3.

How money itself is changing Longer-Range

Multiple new forms of money are arriving on a 2026 to 2029 horizon, and each makes the same demand of national infrastructure: settle here, or be bypassed.

The Bank's systemic-stablecoin regime is being finalised, with holding limits softened toward aggregate issuance caps (Bank of England, November 2025; Fox Williams, May 2026), with final UK crypto rules expected during 2026 (Skadden, April 2026). The digital euro and the GENIUS Act dollar-stablecoin regime advance in parallel (European Central Bank, March 2026; OCC, March 2026), and tokenised deposits plus central-bank money can now settle on one platform (Bank for International Settlements, May 2026).

The money-change horizon, 2025 to 2029

Counter-argument: commercial bank money settled over existing rails will remain dominant for years; the multi-money world may arrive far more slowly than the policy activity suggests, making a costly early platform bet premature.

Decision link: SI 2.

The end-user lens Longer-Range

The Vision's real test is user outcomes, choice, low friction and inclusion, not transaction counts, and Pay.UK is steward of a shared public utility.

Cash fell to 9% of transactions while mobile-wallet registration reached 57% of adults (UK Finance, October 2025), and HM Treasury's Financial Inclusion Strategy targets account access, cash access and digital inclusion (HM Treasury, November 2025). Adoption and trust, not technology, will decide whether the transformation lands.

Counter-argument: user demand for instant, lower-cost payments may pull adoption faster than inclusion concerns slow it, making this a tailwind rather than a constraint, provided cash access is protected for those who need it.

Decision link: SI 4.

4. Strategic Implications

SI 1: Pin down transition accountability before build begins Decide

The single largest systemic risk this cycle is not technical but governance: decision rights and liabilities are temporarily diffused across Pay.UK, the new Delivery Company and a regulator merging into the FCA. The Board should secure a written allocation of risk ownership and escalation for the transition window, so that no failure can fall between the entities.

Action: by Q3 2026, agree and document a transition accountability and risk-ownership framework with the Delivery Company and the FCA, owned by the Pay.UK COO.

Draws on Theme 1.

SI 2: Commit the renewed platform to money-agnostic settlement Prepare

Stablecoins, tokenised deposits and a potential digital pound each demand settlement, and cross-border schemes are interlinking national rails. Pay.UK should ensure the renewal design can settle multiple forms of regulated money and connect cross-border, positioning its platform as the neutral centre rather than one option among several.

Action: by end-2026, publish design principles requiring the renewed platform to support tokenised deposits, regulated stablecoins, cross-border interlinking and a potential digital pound; owned by Strategy with the Delivery Company.

Draws on Theme 1, Theme 5.

SI 3: Treat trusted-data dependency as a resilience exposure Prepare

The safety of instant payments now rests on identity and verification infrastructure Pay.UK does not fully control. The CRO should map this dependency explicitly and pursue rail-level verification (extending Confirmation of Payee) so that a weakness in shared identity infrastructure cannot propagate unchecked into fraud losses and lost adoption.

Action: by Q4 2026, deliver a board-level dependency map of identity and verification infrastructure with mitigation options; owned by the CRO.

Draws on Theme 2, Theme 4.

SI 4: Lead the cross-sector fraud and inclusion agenda Monitor

Fraud originates upstream in social media and telecoms, and reimbursement alone cannot fix it; inclusion and cash access remain the public test of the transition. Pay.UK should use its convening position to push fraud prevention beyond banking and to keep end-user outcomes central, monitoring for the trigger that moves this from advocacy to a binding obligation.

Action: through 2026, sustain a cross-sector fraud-data and inclusion engagement programme; owned by External Affairs, reviewed each cycle.

Draws on Theme 3, Theme 6.

5. Interdependencies, Emerging Risks and Systemic Consequences

The six themes are not parallel tracks; they are one system, and the most consequential risks for Pay.UK sit at their intersections rather than inside any single theme.

The first interdependency is rebuild-while-running. The Payments Forward Plan concentrates change into a narrow window: the NPA is retired, a Delivery Company stands up in 2026, and the PSR is consolidated into the FCA (FCA, February 2026). That governance and execution load lands in the same year account-to-account volume steps up, with commercial VRP going live (FCA, June 2026). A delivery slip, an accountability gap between Pay.UK, the Delivery Company and a merging regulator, or a resilience event during cut-over would not stay contained: it would propagate into adoption, fraud exposure and public trust at once.

The second is money-form proliferation onto a single rail. A systemic-stablecoin regime, a possible digital pound and tokenised deposits all point the same way (Bank of England, November 2025; Bank for International Settlements, May 2026). The Bank's softening of stablecoin holding limits (Fox Williams, May 2026) signals deposit-to-stablecoin migration is being managed rather than blocked, coupling Pay.UK's rail economics to bank-funding stability and to the account-to-account business case at the same time.

The third, and sharpest, is the instant-trust-fraud triangle. Instant, irrevocable payments raise the cost of every fraudulent transaction, and mandatory reimbursement has repriced that cost onto providers (Payment Systems Regulator, 2025). That makes the trusted-data layer load-bearing for the whole instant economy, even though much of it sits outside Pay.UK's control (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025, December 2025). Taken together, the sources suggest shared identity and data infrastructure is the system's true single point of failure: a weakness there would surface first as fraud losses, then as stalled adoption, undoing the competition-with-cards rationale of the Vision. The financial-crime vector compounds it, as stablecoins become a leading laundering channel (FATF, June 2025).

The systemic consequence is concentration. The UK is converging more money, more value and more trust onto fewer, more critical national rails, operated through a transitional governance structure, at the moment those rails are being rebuilt. The rails are becoming critical national infrastructure carrying public-money-grade expectations, while accountability is temporarily diffused. This report's reading is that the risk which would force a revision is the benign one: clean delivery sequencing with single-point accountability would convert this concentration from a fragility into a strategic advantage.

6. Scenario Matrix

The two critical uncertainties: whether the multi-money transition is fast or slow, and whether transition governance is unified or fragmented. The four scenarios below are planning aids, not forecasts.

Vertical axis: pace of the multi-money transition (top row fast, bottom row slow). Horizontal axis: transition governance (left column fragmented, right column unified).

Routed Around

Fast money change, fragmented governance.

New money forms scale while governance gaps slow the platform; private and cross-border rails capture settlement and Pay.UK is disintermediated at the edges. Indicators: stablecoin and tokenised-deposit volumes rising outside the national rail; delivery milestones slipping; bank-led alternatives emerging.

Neutral Centre

Fast money change, unified governance.

Pay.UK's renewed platform settles tokenised deposits, regulated stablecoins and a digital pound, with clear transition accountability. The rail becomes the trusted multi-money hub and the competition-with-cards goal is met. Indicators: money-agnostic design principles published; a single accountable transition owner named; cVRP adoption climbing.

Drift and Exposure

Slow money change, fragmented governance.

Little new money but a messy transition: resilience risk rises during cut-over, fraud and trust pressures persist, and the rebuild over-runs without clear ownership. Indicators: governance disputes; a resilience or fraud incident; adoption plateauing.

Orderly Renewal

Slow money change, unified governance.

The multi-money world arrives gradually; a well-governed rebuild delivers resilient, optimised rails and incremental open-banking growth. Pay.UK consolidates its core role. Indicators: stable delivery cadence; account-to-account growth steady; stablecoin adoption modest.

7. What We Are Not Planning For

  1. A near-term digital-pound launch. The Bank's own timetable points to issuance no earlier than the end of the decade, conditional on legislation, so this cycle plans for optionality, not launch. Reinstate if: HM Treasury and the Bank announce a firm build-and-issue decision with dates.
  2. Wholesale displacement of cash. Cash use is declining but remains significant and politically protected through the inclusion agenda. Reinstate if: legislation removes cash-acceptance or access protections.
  3. A single global cross-border standard. Interlinking (TIPS, Nexus) is advancing as a network of bilateral and multilateral links, not one universal scheme, so planning assumes interoperability, not convergence. Reinstate if: a dominant global instant-payment standard emerges with UK participation.

8. Discussion Points for the Leadership Team

  1. If transition accountability cannot be cleanly split with the Delivery Company and the FCA, do we slow the rebuild or accept the governance risk?
  2. Should we commit capital now to money-agnostic settlement capability, before demand for stablecoins and tokenised deposits is proven?
  3. How much of the safety of a payment are we willing to let depend on identity infrastructure we do not own, and at what point does that become unacceptable?
  4. Is Pay.UK's role in fraud enabling or accountable, and would we accept a formal obligation to act on fraud that originates outside payments?
  5. From the non-consensus reading: if account-to-account adoption stalls on trust rather than technology, what in our plan changes first?
