Why This Matters Now

Electricity buyers spent most of 2026 discovering that money is no longer the binding constraint. Prices hit their ceilings, bills went up, and almost no new power arrived, because what is actually scarce is the equipment, the permission and the water to run a plant, not the willingness to pay for one.

Three responses are under way at once. Regulators on three continents have rewritten what a large new customer must sign before it can connect. Governments have started deciding in public who carries the cost of the network that customer triggers. And the manufacturers have sold out their factories to 2030.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: treat a connection as a long contract with obligations, not as a place in a queue, and check whether anything you own or finance depends on a river behaving the way it used to. The early warning is in the terms of the contract, not the price.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week assume that a high enough price will bring the power?

The evidence in this report says the highest price the rules allow bought 317 megawatts.

This cycle the electricity system reached the top of its price signal and got almost nothing back. PJM, which runs the grid for 13 US states and Washington DC, held its auction for power in 2028/2029: it cleared at the highest price its rules allow, finished 6,831.3 MW short of what reliability requires, and brought forward 317.1 MW of new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). Money is no longer what is scarce. Equipment, permission to connect and cooling water are.

Three terms are being rewritten at once. Regulators in the United States, Britain and Japan have turned a request to connect a large new customer, usually a data centre, into a long contract with conditions (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026) (Ofgem, Jul 2026). Governments are deciding in public who pays for the network that customer needs: twenty-three US states now have a special tariff for large loads (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026). And the turbine makers have sold their output to the end of the decade (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). A dry summer added a warning of its own, as low and warm rivers took nuclear and coal plants offline in Romania, France and along the Rhine (Euronews, Aug 2026) (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026).

Looking ahead to 2030, the IEA expects data centres worldwide to use about 950 TWh of electricity, roughly double 2025, with 15 to 27 GW of it possibly supplied by gas plants built on site (IEA, Apr 2026). Developers are chasing 63 GW of new gas capacity for 2026 to 2030 (Wood Mackenzie, reported by Utility Dive, Apr 2026), while GE Vernova plans to reach 30 GW of annual output only by 2030 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 7 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The analysis assumes the shortfall is a delivery problem, not a forecasting one. If the large loads being planned for do not arrive, the auctions are pricing demand that never comes: the Texas grid's queue holds around 460,000 MW of interest against a system that peaked at 91.3 GW (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026). The warning sign would be the largest cloud companies cutting their spending plans for two quarters running. So far spending is still rising, but more of it is borrowed: debt now funds 32% of it, against 9% two years earlier (FactSet, Jul 2026).

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Treat every large new connection as a long contract, not a place in a queue. Oregon's approved terms run up to thirty years, with minimum charges set at 90% of the capacity the customer contracts.
  2. Secure a delivery slot for equipment rather than a choice of technology. With the turbine makers sold out to 2030 and large transformers taking four years, the manufacturing window is the scarce asset.
  3. Test every river-cooled plant and barge-supplied fuel contract against a dry summer. The Rhine and the Danube took generation and fuel deliveries offline this summer.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

Audience Snapshots

Four cards, one per reader group. Each says what is changing for that group and suggests one question for the next meeting, so a team can take its card as an agenda item without reading the whole report.

If you are trying to connect a large new load

Connecting to the grid now means signing a long contract with conditions

What is changing: Oregon's approved terms run 10 to 30 years, with the customer paying all distribution upgrade costs (Utility Dive, May 2026), and Texas requires a large load to be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes (White & Case, Aug 2026).

Question for your next meeting: Does our site still pay off with a thirty-year minimum charge at 90% of contracted capacity, and who inside the business owns that liability?

What it is for: Turns each connection into a liability that a named person owns, before the next capital approval.

If you set or approve network tariffs

Four jurisdictions have chosen four different ways to share the cost of the grid

What is changing: Twenty-three US states now have a special tariff for large loads (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026), Spain would make network access itself revocable (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026), and Germany is subsidising transmission charges to hold bills down (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026).

Question for your next meeting: If the forecast load does not arrive, who pays for the network built for it: the developer or the household?

What it is for: Shows whether a tariff protects households when demand disappoints, not only when it arrives.

If you finance or insure generation assets

The scarce asset is a delivery slot, not the plant

What is changing: Turbine order books are full to the end of the decade (GE Vernova, Jul 2026), new gas plants cost at least $2,500 per kW (IEEFA, Apr 2026), and what came online in the first half of 2026 was mostly solar and batteries (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026).

Question for your next meeting: Does our capital plan assume a gas unit we cannot take delivery of before 2030, and what does it cost to bridge the gap with solar and storage?

What it is for: Separates the plans that depend on equipment nobody can deliver from those that do not.

If your plant depends on river or sea water for cooling

Low rivers and warm water now take plants offline for weeks

What is changing: Romania's Cernavoda nuclear plant went fully offline (Euronews, Aug 2026), a record 15.6% of French nuclear capacity was missing in July (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026), and low water on the Rhine cut coal barge loadings by about three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026).

Question for your next meeting: What happens to our revenue and reliability if our water limit binds for six weeks rather than six days, and does any contract we hold price that?

What it is for: Puts a number on a risk that most valuation models leave out.

1. Paying more did not buy more power

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Grid operators such as PJM, and the state governments that negotiate their price caps · Room to act: Low for most buyers, who pay the price set; medium for large buyers able to contract for supply directly

The price your investment plan relies on has stopped bringing in new supply, and that changes what a charge for capacity tells you. PJM's 2028/2029 auction hit the price cap set by its rules, closed 6,831.3 MW below the amount it needs to keep the lights on, and brought forward only 317.1 MW of new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). Any plan that reads a rising price as a sign that supply is coming has it the wrong way round: it is a sign that supply is missing. The cost of the shortage now lands on household bills and in politics, rather than paying for new plants.

PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction: what the ceiling price procured

Source: PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, July 2026. Bars are to scale against each other.

  • The auction hit its price cap for the third time running, so the price no longer tells anyone how short of power the system is (Utility Dive, Jul 2026).
  • PJM says the auction is working as designed, not failing. Its paper on market design describes a trap in which the high prices needed to attract investment provoke political intervention that scares investment off, and states that “The market is generating the price signal it was designed to generate during a shortage.” (PJM, Powering Reliability Through Market Design, May 2026)
  • The cost is already in household bills. New Jersey regulators put the increase at around 20%, with capacity charges accounting for 15% to 20% of a residential bill (New Jersey Board of Public Utilities, Aug 2026).
  • Texas shows the contrast. Its grid operator, ERCOT, met a record 91.1 GW of demand in one hour on 22 July, 6% above the 2023 record, mainly with gas at 48% and solar at 32% (US Energy Information Administration, Aug 2026), and still had more than 20,000 MW of spare capacity lined up when the record was set (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

PJM itself sets out three ways to reform its market, including openly rationing power by place or by type of customer (PJM, Powering Reliability Through Market Design, May 2026). In Texas, the grid operator is planning for demand of roughly 175,000 MW by 2032, almost double this summer's record (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026).

What it means: By 2030 the question is less what capacity costs than who gets it when there is not enough. Buyers who contract directly for supply now are less exposed to how that rationing is designed.

Counter-argument

The strongest challenge is that an auction with a price cap cannot be judged on what it bought, because the cap itself kept new supply out: a higher price might have attracted more, and the states that negotiated the cap own that choice. Sierra Club argues the opposite, that annual customer costs in PJM have risen by $12.5bn since June 2025 and the cap is too loose rather than too tight (Sierra Club, Jul 2026). Both readings can hold, and they converge on the same test: whether the 2029/2030 auction, run under whatever cap then applies, brings forward materially more than 317.1 MW. If it does, the cap was the problem. If it does not, the problem is getting new plants built, and this theme holds.

2. The regulator rewrote the rules for every large load

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Regulators: the US federal energy regulator, the Texas utility commission, Ofgem and Japan's energy agency · Room to act: High for developers, who choose where to connect and on what terms

Within four months, four jurisdictions each turned a request to connect a large customer from an engineering question into a contract with conditions, without coordinating with each other. If your growth plan assumes the terms you signed under, or expected to sign under, it is already out of date in at least one market you operate in. In practice, the question has moved from whether power is available to what you must accept in order to get it.

  • On 18 June FERC, the US federal energy regulator, ordered all six regional grid operators to justify or change how they connect very large users, with a 17 August deadline to respond (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026); it chose regional rules over a national standard and kept the option to step in later (Utility Dive, Jun 2026).
  • Texas went furthest on how connections work day to day: the state utility commission's 24 July order confirms that a large user sited next to a power plant must be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes, whatever that plant is producing (White & Case, Aug 2026).
  • Britain went after the queue rather than the connection. Ofgem, the energy regulator, is clearing speculative projects from a queue that has grown from 41 GW to 125 GW, roughly 80 GW of it data centres, and is introducing a fee to show commitment (Ofgem, Jul 2026), with deposits, fees to stay in the queue and deadlines after an offer is made all proposed for new users (Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer, Jul 2026).
  • Japan regulated the data centres themselves rather than their connection: from April 2026 operators must report their electricity use and their efficiency rating (PUE, where 1.0 is perfect), publish targets every year, and reach 1.4 or better by fiscal 2030, or 1.3 for centres built from fiscal 2029 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Losing the connection as the penalty. Spain's draft decree would cut a data centre off from the grid if it repeatedly fails to get 80% of its power from new renewable sources. The Spanish is unambiguous: “El incumplimiento reiterado podrá acarrear la pérdida del acceso a la red.” (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026) It gains weight if a second EU country copies the threat of disconnection rather than a fine.
  • Weak signal Efficiency standards as a condition of entry. Japan's requirement that centres built from fiscal 2029 reach an efficiency rating of 1.3 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026) is a hard number aimed at the data centre rather than the grid. It gains weight if a US state or the European Commission writes a minimum efficiency into a tariff.

Outlook to 2030

The IEA expects data centres worldwide to roughly double their electricity use, from 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030 (IEA, Apr 2026). Japan's data centres must reach an efficiency rating of 1.4 or better by fiscal 2030 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026).

What it means: Connection terms written now will govern a much larger load by 2030. Developers who take part in writing them now face fewer surprises later.

Counter-argument

The reforms may change the price of connecting without limiting who connects. FERC explicitly left states in charge of retail sales, who qualifies and where plants are built (Utility Dive, Jun 2026), and its own rulemaking file describes the aim as connection that is “timely, orderly, reliable, and non-discriminatory” (FERC Docket RM26-4, accessed Sep 2026), which is a push to connect faster as much as a restriction. NERC, which sets reliability standards for the North American grid, records 58.5 GW of new supply against 11 GW of extra peak demand (NERC 2026 Summer Reliability Assessment, May 2026), so the case that there is not enough power is not the same everywhere. The test is whether any of the six grid operators' responses after 17 August actually reduces how much new demand can connect, rather than changing who connects first.

3. Who pays for the grid became the live political question

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: State regulators and governments, often in an election year · Room to act: Medium: firms can shape terms in consultations; households have little say

Who pays for the grid stopped being a technical detail of utility pricing and became a real policy tool, decided in public and often in an election year. For anyone building, financing or supplying a very large user, the tool each jurisdiction chooses now changes the project's costs materially, and the tools differ more than the problem does. The same data centre carries different costs in Oregon, Madrid and Munich.

Where Theme 1 meets Theme 3

Drawn from the cross-impact grid in the Energy, Infrastructure and Climate Resilience Change Tracker (27 August 2026 cycle), re-sourced against this cycle's evidence base.

  • Twenty-three US states have at least one special tariff for very large users and seven more have proposals pending, with Virginia's taking effect on 1 January 2027 (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026); regulators approved 29 such tariffs in 2025 alone, against 14 in the whole of 2018 to 2024 (Smart Electric Power Alliance, Apr 2026).
  • Oregon shows what a settled tariff looks like: contracts of 10 to 30 years depending on size, the customer paying 100% of the cost of upgrading the local network, a minimum charge of 90% of the capacity it reserves, and a 1 cent/kWh surcharge above 100 MW passed back to households and low-income customers (Utility Dive, May 2026).
  • The federal government answered with a voluntary promise rather than a tariff: the expanded Ratepayer Protection Pledge claims to cover 80% of all power delivered to US homes and businesses (the White House, Jul 2026), a claim that depends on those who signed it keeping their word.
  • Europe is using two different levers at once: Germany has directed a federal subsidy at transmission charges and reformed network-cost sharing (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026), while the Commission proposed in July to cut network charges for specific consumer groups (European Commission, updated Jul 2026) and Spain would attach a renewable-sourcing condition to access itself (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Pennsylvania projects the price cap will be worth roughly $45bn to 67 million people over four years (Governor of Pennsylvania, Jul 2026). But the IEA sees 15 to 27 GW of gas plants built on site at data centres by 2030 (IEA, Apr 2026), which takes load, and the customers who pay for the network, off the shared grid.

What it means: The more large loads supply themselves, the fewer customers are left to pay for the grid. By 2030 the argument may move from the cost of connecting to the cost of leaving.

Counter-argument

A tariff written for a boom is tested by a bust. If the expected demand does not arrive, households still pay for the network built for it, and terms that make the developer pay are only as good as the developer's finances in 2031. The clearest warning is in how the building is paid for rather than in the tariff: the share of the largest cloud companies' spending funded by new debt rose from 9% in financial year 2024 to 32% in the year to June 2026 (FactSet, Jul 2026). On the available evidence the tariffs move the cost but not the risk that demand falls short, and whatever is left over returns to household bills by default.

4. You cannot buy a turbine before 2030

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The three large turbine makers, and whoever already holds a delivery slot · Room to act: High for buyers who can reserve a slot early or switch to solar, storage and flexible demand

New gas plants are not just expensive, they are unavailable, and that upsets every investment plan built on them over the next twelve months. The three makers of large gas turbines have sold their output to the end of the decade, other equipment such as transformers takes as long, and prices have risen faster than utilities planned for. Whoever holds a delivery slot holds the scarce asset; everyone else must choose between waiting and building something else.

  • GE Vernova's own quarterly results are the clearest measure of the gap: orders and reserved production slots for its gas turbines grew from 100 GW to 116 GW in one quarter, and it expects at least 125 GW by the end of 2026 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). It plans to make 30 GW a year by 2030, later than most of today's plans need it.
  • Mitsubishi's order book for large turbines reached 35 GW against 23 GW a year earlier, and orders placed in the first quarter of 2026 are due for delivery between 2028 and 2030 (Utility Dive, Aug 2026); Siemens Energy's stands near 69 GW with waiting times of more than three years (Utility Dive, Aug 2026) and Ansaldo is expanding to fill the same gap (POWER, Sep 2026).
  • Other equipment is no better: large transformers now take four years to deliver, and demand for the transformers that connect power plants to the grid rose 274% between 2019 and 2025 (pv magazine USA, May 2026).
  • Prices have risen with scarcity. IEEFA puts the newest proposed gas plants at $2,500 per kW installed or more, or $2.5bn for a 1,000 MW plant (IEEFA, Apr 2026), while utilities told investors in the second quarter that they are focused on delivering projects and protecting customers rather than negotiating on price (Utility Dive, Aug 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Wood Mackenzie expects turbine prices to reach $600/kW by the end of 2027, up 195% since 2019, as developers chase 63 GW of gas capacity for 2026 to 2030 (Wood Mackenzie, reported by Utility Dive, Apr 2026). GE Vernova plans to raise its annual output to 30 GW only by 2030 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026).

What it means: Gas ordered today mostly arrives around 2030. Until then, new supply comes from solar, batteries and demand that can flex, and plans should be built around them.

Counter-argument

A shortage of one technology is not a shortage of supply. In the first half of 2026 the United States brought online 11,458 MW of utility-scale solar and 8,207 MW of batteries against 2,707 MW of natural gas across 46 projects (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026), so the shortage limits the gas route, not new capacity as a whole, and the manufacturers are doubling their output against 2024 levels (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). The limit of that argument is the season: solar at 32% of a Texan evening peak (US Energy Information Administration, Aug 2026) is not the same as power that is guaranteed on a still winter night, which is the hour the capacity auction is actually buying for.

5. Heat and low water stopped being a weather story

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Nobody: the weather decides, and plant operators and river authorities manage the consequences · Room to act: High for asset owners, lenders and insurers, who can price the risk and adapt the assets

How much water a river carries, and how warm it is, moved from a footnote in outage reports to a factor in which plants run, how fuel moves and what plants are worth, across two river basins in one season. If you own, finance or buy from a coal, gas or nuclear plant that takes cooling water from a river, your assumption about how often it can run is now the number most likely to be wrong. Heat also pushes demand up, which is why it belongs in the discussion about capacity rather than beside it.

  • Romania's Cernavoda plant, normally a fifth of national electricity, was fully offline in August for only the second time in its history, the first being 2003 (Euronews, Aug 2026); the water authority put Danube flow at Romania's border at 1,650 cubic metres per second against a July average of 4,750, approaching the 1985 minimum of 1,400 (Mediafax, Jul 2026).
  • France lost output through the summer: around 3% in June and around 6% in July, with a record 15.6% of capacity missing (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026), and 13 of 57 reactors affected at the peak of the episode (Selectra, Aug 2026).
  • Fuel could not move either. The Rhine at Kaub fell to 28 centimetres, the lowest since 2018, with coal inventories at Amsterdam, Rotterdam and Antwerp up about 75% since spring while barge loadings inland were cut by three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026): the coal existed, it was simply in the wrong place.
  • Alpine hydro ran at 51% of normal in Austria, 48% in Switzerland, 63% in Germany and 68% in France in July (Euronews, Aug 2026), and gas filled the gap: EU gas generation rose 8% last year as hydro fell 12%.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Sea life as a cause of outages. Jellyfish blockages at Gravelines sat alongside heat as a cause of lost French output this summer (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026). It gains weight if a second operator reports sea or river life blocking its cooling-water intake in another region within a year.
  • Weak signal A broader but shallower drought. The Joint Research Centre records half of the EU and the UK in drought but only 9% of territory at alert level in July, against 18% at the 2022 peak (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026). It points the other way if the 2027 season is again widespread but mild rather than intense.

Outlook to 2030

EDF's losses to heat and low water, around 0.3% of output a year, could reach 1.4% by 2035 (Selectra, Aug 2026), and energy used for cooling worldwide is set to double by 2050 against 2023 (MIT Technology Review, Jun 2026), so hot summers raise demand at the moment they cut supply.

What it means: By 2030 a dry summer is a planning case, not an exception. Plants and fuel contracts tested against one now are worth more than those that are not.

Counter-argument

2026 may have been widespread rather than severe. The Joint Research Centre's own comparison puts 9% of EU and UK territory at alert level in July against 18% at the 2022 peak and 6% in late August 2018 (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026), which is a different shape of event rather than an unambiguously worse one. France's system operator stated before the summer that security of supply required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026), and it was right: no shortfall materialised. So the theme claims a risk to how often plants can run and what that costs, not a risk of blackouts, and it is wrong if 2027 passes with French outages back within their normal range and no repeat of this summer's river transport problems.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Re-assess large connections as long contracts, not places in a queue

Whoever owns site strategy should re-cost every existing and planned large connection against the terms now being approved, before the next investment approval rather than after it. Oregon's terms run 10 to 30 years depending on size, make the customer pay 100% of local network upgrades, set minimum charges at 90% of the capacity it reserves and add a 1 cent/kWh surcharge above 100 MW (Utility Dive, May 2026); twenty-three states now have a similar tariff, with seven more pending (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026); and Texas requires a large user next to a power plant to be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes (White & Case, Aug 2026). These are balance-sheet commitments with exit costs, and they are being decided now, which is why this is a Decide rather than a Prepare. The reason to move before the next approval is that the six regional grid operators' responses to FERC, due by 17 August (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026) will set the terms in the remaining markets while the approval is under way.

Action: by 31 October 2026, the site or business development lead produces a one-page summary of the terms of each live connection (contract length, minimum charge, share of upgrade costs and cost of leaving) and names the person who owns each.

Decide Draws on Themes 1, 2 and 3.

Implication 2: Buy the delivery slot, not the technology

Capital planning should treat a place in the factory schedule as the scarce asset and decide whether to reserve one before the 2027 budget closes. GE Vernova's orders and reserved slots grew from 100 GW to 116 GW in a single quarter, and it expects at least 125 GW under contract by year end (GE Vernova, Jul 2026), Mitsubishi is delivering first-quarter 2026 orders in 2028 to 2030 (Utility Dive, Aug 2026), and transformers run to four years (pv magazine USA, May 2026). The posture is Prepare rather than Decide because the right answer depends on how much guaranteed capacity the organisation needs, which it may not yet have worked out: what actually came online in the first half of 2026 was 11,458 MW of solar and 8,207 MW of batteries against 2,707 MW of gas (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026), and for many patterns of demand that is the better stopgap. The decision to avoid is waiting for turbine prices to fall, at $2,500 per kW and rising (IEEFA, Apr 2026).

Action: by 15 December 2026, capital planning states how much guaranteed capacity it needs, by season and hour, for 2029 to 2032, and whether to reserve a delivery slot against it.

Prepare Draws on Themes 4 and 1.

Implication 3: Put a water and river-temperature test into valuation and fuel logistics

Asset owners, lenders and insurers should add a low-water case to the valuation of coal, gas and nuclear plants, and to any fuel contract that moves by barge, ahead of the next revaluation cycle rather than after the next summer. This season produced a full Cernavoda shutdown for only the second time in the plant's history (Euronews, Aug 2026), a record 15.6% of French capacity missing (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026), Alpine hydro at roughly half of normal (Euronews, Aug 2026) and a three-quarters cut in inland coal barge loadings with the fuel itself in surplus at the coast (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026). The posture is Prepare because no blackout followed and France's system operator was right that supply security required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026): the risk is to how often plants can run, what that costs and what contracts say, not a failure of the whole system. That is exactly the kind of risk a valuation model absorbs quietly and a lending committee never sees.

Action: before the next annual revaluation, the risk team adds a six-week low-water case to every river-cooled plant and barge-supplied asset it holds, and reports the revenue at risk to the audit or risk committee.

Prepare Draws on Themes 5 and 3.

Implication 4: Track how each jurisdiction chooses to share the cost of the grid

Government affairs and regulatory teams should keep a current view of the tool used in each market, because the tools are diverging and each leaves the leftover cost with someone different. The United States is using state tariffs (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026) alongside a voluntary federal pledge claimed to cover 80% of power delivered to homes and businesses (the White House, Jul 2026); Germany is subsidising transmission charges (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026); the Commission proposed cutting network charges for specific consumer groups (European Commission, updated Jul 2026); Spain would make access revocable (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026). Monitor is the right posture because none has yet been tested by demand falling short, and what matters is not the announcement but the first tool that survives that test.

Action: from Q4 2026, the regulatory lead keeps a one-line-per-jurisdiction list of these tools, reviewed at each quarterly risk meeting, and flags any move from a voluntary promise to a binding tariff, or from a fine to the threat of disconnection.

Build and Bill

Large loads connect to the shared grid on long contracts that make them pay for what they trigger, on the Oregon model (Utility Dive, May 2026). The network grows on their money, and capacity auctions start clearing below their caps again as solar, storage and flexible demand arrive. Equipment stays scarce until the turbine makers' new factories open, so the first years are slow. Heat and low water remain the main surprise for the existing fleet.

Early indicators: Second and third states adopt Oregon-style terms; a capacity auction clears below its cap without a rule change; a large-load tariff survives a cancelled project without the cost passing to households.

Power Islands

Large loads build or contract their own generation, often gas on site, and pay their own way: the IEA sees 15 to 27 GW of on-site gas at data centres by 2030 (IEA, Apr 2026). The grid is spared the cost but loses the flexibility and the customers it was counting on, and regulators respond with standby and exit charges. Turbine slots become the prize, and whoever reserved early wins.

Early indicators: Co-location approvals speed up after the Texas order; on-site gas announcements outpace grid connection requests; standby-charge filings rise.

The Blame Auction

The closest to today. Large loads connect to the shared grid, but the cost lands on household bills, because tariffs are announced and not tested and price caps are set politically (Governor of Maryland, Jul 2026). The shortfall grows under the cap, emergency powers stand in for buying new supply, and the cost surfaces in bills nobody has agreed to defend.

Early indicators: A fourth consecutive capped auction; a state leaving a negotiated price ceiling; electricity bills becoming an election issue in more states.

Behind the Meter

Large loads supply themselves, but the network they leave behind is still paid for by everyone else. Household bills rise for reasons that have nothing to do with household use, the base that pays for the grid shrinks, and the politics sharpens. The contest moves from the terms of connecting to the terms of leaving, and the reputational risk for operators arrives before the regulatory one.

Early indicators: A utility reports falling regulated load while regional consumption rises; exit-fee proposals appear; states debate charging departing loads for network costs left behind.

Options: Connecting a large load (developers and data-centre operators)

Option A · Least change

Take the standard terms

Accept the tariff and the queue on offer, and connect when the grid is ready.

Trade-off: No extra cost now. The slowest route, and the terms can change while you wait.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Build and Bill; The Blame Auction

Option B · Middle course

Offer flexibility

Accept a connection that can be cut back when the grid is under stress, in return for connecting sooner.

Trade-off: Faster connection. Operations must cope with being curtailed.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Bring your own power

Build or contract generation and storage on site, and use the grid as backup.

Trade-off: Most control, most capital. Exposed to standby and exit charges.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Power Islands; Behind the Meter

Options: Firm capacity (utilities, generators and large buyers)

Option A · Least change

Wait for prices to fall

Hold off on gas orders until the turbine makers' new factories open.

Trade-off: No capital at risk now. The supply gap stays open to the end of the decade.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Blame Auction

Option B · Middle course

Bridge with solar and storage

Build what can be delivered now and decide on firm capacity later.

Trade-off: Fast and available. Does not cover a still winter night.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Reserve a delivery slot

Pay now to secure a turbine or transformer slot in the late 2020s.

Trade-off: Locks in scarce equipment. Commits capital before the need is certain.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Build and Bill; Power Islands

Options: Who pays for the grid (regulators and policymakers)

Option A · Least change

Cap the bill

Hold prices down with caps and subsidies, as Maryland, Pennsylvania and Germany have done.

Trade-off: Protects households now. Hides a shortfall that keeps growing underneath.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Blame Auction

Option B · Middle course

Make the large load pay

Long contracts with minimum charges and exit costs, on the Oregon model.

Trade-off: Protects households while developers stay solvent. Untested when demand disappoints.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Build and Bill

Option C · Biggest shift

Tie access to new supply

Connect large loads only if they bring new clean supply, and withdraw access if they do not, as Spain's draft decree proposes.

Trade-off: Adds supply alongside demand. The strongest tool and the hardest to enforce.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Build and Bill; Power Islands

Options: Water and heat (asset owners, lenders and insurers)

Option A · Least change

Treat it as weather

Keep current availability assumptions and handle outages as they come.

Trade-off: No cost now. The next dry summer surprises the model.

⊞ Fits scenarios: None for long

Option B · Middle course

Price it in

Add a six-week water case to valuations and write low-water clauses into fuel contracts.

Trade-off: Moderate effort. Shows the risk before a lender or insurer does.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Adapt the assets

Invest in cooling, fuel stocks on site and supply routes that do not depend on one river.

Trade-off: Costly. Keeps plants running when rivers run low.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

What We Are Not Planning For

Systemic reliability

A drought-driven European supply failure this winter

The 2026 season took generation offline without taking the system down, and the operator best placed to judge said so in advance: France's system operator stated that security of electricity supply required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026), and no shortfall followed. The Joint Research Centre's own comparison puts 9% of EU and UK territory at alert level in July against 18% at the 2022 peak (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026), so this was a broader but less intense event than the one Europe already absorbed. The risk this briefing covers is how often plants can run and what that costs, which is why it sits inside Theme 5 rather than here.

↺ Reinstated if a member state issues a winter supply warning attributable to hydrology, or French nuclear outages exceed their 2022 peak.

Jurisdiction

A single US federal rule on who pays for the grid, overriding the states

It would resolve the messiest question in this cycle at a stroke, and there is no evidence it is coming. FERC explicitly left states in charge of retail sales, who qualifies and where plants are built when it issued its orders to the grid operators (Utility Dive, Jun 2026), and chose regional rules over a national standard (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026). The federal instrument actually deployed was a voluntary pledge (the White House, Jul 2026), which is the opposite of overriding the states. Planning for a single national rule would mean ignoring the twenty-three different instruments that already exist (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026).

↺ Reinstated if FERC starts writing rules on how retail customers share grid costs, or a federal bill on pricing for very large users gains committee support.

Demand

A collapse in data-centre electricity demand inside the planning horizon

The demand correction that would relieve every constraint in this briefing is not visible in the spending. Spending by the five largest cloud companies reached roughly $490bn in the twelve months to May 2026 (FactSet, Jul 2026), and ERCOT is planning against roughly 175,000 MW by 2032 (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026). What did change is how it is paid for, with new debt rising from 9% to 32% of that spending (FactSet, Jul 2026). That is a real risk and it is tracked in Theme 3's counter-argument, but it is a risk to returns and to who pays for network built for demand that never came, not a near-term collapse in demand.

↺ Reinstated if the largest cloud companies cut their spending plans for two quarters running, or a regional grid operator reports a net fall in requests to connect very large users.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If a capacity price at the ceiling the rules allow procured 317.1 MW against a 6,831.3 MW shortfall, what evidence would persuade us that the next auction will actually buy new supply rather than just decide who pays, and what do we do differently in the meantime?
  2. Oregon's approved terms run to thirty years with minimum charges at 90% of contracted capacity. Which of our live connection plans would we still approve on those terms, and who signs that off?
  3. We can reserve a turbine slot or keep our options open on technology, and with transformers taking four years we probably cannot do both. Which do we choose, and how much guaranteed capacity do we need?
  4. If a large user that signed a tariff making it pay for its network goes bust in 2031, does the cost fall on its administrator or on household bills, and have we read the tariff in every jurisdiction where we are exposed?
  5. Jellyfish and a 34-year low on the Danube took capacity off two European systems in the same season. How much revenue do we lose if low water limits a plant for six weeks rather than six days, and is that number anywhere in our current model?
