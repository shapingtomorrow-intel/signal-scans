Why This Matters Now

Electricity buyers spent most of 2026 discovering that money is no longer the binding constraint. Prices hit their ceilings, bills went up, and almost no new power arrived, because what is actually scarce is the equipment, the permission and the water to run a plant, not the willingness to pay for one.

Three responses are under way at once. Regulators on three continents have rewritten what a large new customer must sign before it can connect. Governments have started deciding in public who carries the cost of the network that customer triggers. And the manufacturers have sold out their factories to 2030.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: treat a connection as a long contract with obligations, not as a place in a queue, and check whether anything you own or finance depends on a river behaving the way it used to. The early warning is the terms sheet, not the price.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week assume that a high enough price will bring the power?

The evidence in this report says the highest price the rules allow bought 317 megawatts.

The defining fact of this cycle is that the electricity system reached the top of its price signal and got almost nothing back. PJM's 2028/2029 capacity auction cleared at the administrative cap of $325.00/MW-day, finished 6,831.3 MW below its own reliability requirement, and brought forward 317.1 MW of genuinely new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). That is under 5% of the shortfall, procured at the highest price the rules allow. The auction is not failing to work; it is doing exactly what its designers say it does. PJM's own market-design paper calls this a credibility trap and states plainly that "The market is generating the price signal it was designed to generate during a shortage." (PJM, Powering Reliability Through Market Design, May 2026) The signal is intact. What the signal is supposed to summon is not available at any price inside the delivery window.

Three mechanisms explain why, and they are the spine of the cycle. Equipment: the three large-frame turbine makers have sold the decade. GE Vernova's gas backlog plus slot reservation agreements moved from 100 GW to 116 GW in a single quarter, with at least 125 GW expected under contract by year end (GE Vernova, Jul 2026); Mitsubishi's large-frame backlog reached 35 GW against 23 GW a year earlier, with orders booked in the first quarter of 2026 scheduled for delivery between 2028 and 2030 (Utility Dive, Aug 2026); Siemens Energy's stands near 69 GW with lead times beyond three years (Utility Dive, Aug 2026). Large power transformers now run to four-year lead times (pv magazine USA, May 2026). Permission: between June and August, FERC opened show-cause proceedings against all six organised US markets (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026), the Public Utility Commission of Texas ordered that a co-located large load must be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes regardless of its paired generation (White & Case, Aug 2026), Ofgem moved to clear speculative data-centre projects out of a queue that has run from 41 GW to 125 GW (Ofgem, Jul 2026), and Japan placed a binding efficiency floor on data-centre operators from April 2026 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026). Payment: twenty-three US states now hold at least one large-load tariff, with seven more pending (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026), while Spain has drafted a decree that would make continued network access conditional on the load bringing its own new renewable supply (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026).

The signal is intact. What the signal is supposed to summon is not available at any price inside the delivery window.

What should surprise leadership is the contrast case. In the same summer, ERCOT met an hourly peak of 91.1 GW, 6% above its previous record, primarily with natural gas at 48% and solar at 32%, and did so with more than 20,000 MW of committed headroom above demand (US Energy Information Administration, Aug 2026) (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026). Nothing about Texan physics is different. What differs is that the capacity arrived before it was needed rather than being bid for afterwards. Meanwhile the politics has detached from the engineering: Maryland and Pennsylvania both announced the same auction result as a $13.3bn saving delivered by a price cap (Governor of Maryland, Jul 2026) (Governor of Pennsylvania, Jul 2026), and New Jersey households are carrying bills roughly 20% higher with capacity charges making up 15% to 20% of the bill (New Jersey Board of Public Utilities, Aug 2026). A cap that holds the price down while the shortfall widens is a bill-management instrument, not a procurement one, and on this briefing's reading the gap between those two things is the exposure that most balance sheets have not yet priced.

The second surprise sits in the water. Romania's Cernavoda plant, normally a fifth of national supply, was fully offline in August for only the second time in its history (Euronews, Aug 2026); French output fell around 3% in June and around 6% in July with a record 15.6% of capacity missing (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026); Alpine hydro ran at 48% to 68% of normal (Euronews, Aug 2026); and the Rhine at Kaub fell to 28 centimetres, cutting inland coal barge loadings by roughly three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026). Taken together, the sources suggest river flow and river temperature have become scheduling inputs rather than tail risks, which changes how a thermal or nuclear asset should be valued and how a fuel contract should be written.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The load-bearing assumption in this synthesis is that the shortfall is a delivery problem rather than a demand-forecast problem. If the large loads being planned for do not arrive, the auctions are pricing a phantom and the correct posture is patience, not procurement. That case is not weak: ERCOT's own queue holds around 460,000 MW of interest against a system that peaked at 91.3 GW (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026), which is a ratio no forecaster treats as literal. The evidence that would force a material revision is a hyperscaler capex guidance cut sustained across two consecutive quarters, or the first RTO to report a net decline in active large-load interconnection requests. Neither has happened: aggregate hyperscaler capex reached roughly $490bn in the twelve months to May 2026, though the share funded by incremental debt rose from 9% in FY24 to 32% (FactSet, Jul 2026). If that debt share keeps climbing while revenue does not, the demand correction arrives through the funding market rather than through the forecast, and it arrives faster.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Re-underwrite every large-load connection you hold or plan as a long-dated contract. Oregon's approved framework runs to thirty years with 100% of distribution upgrade costs and minimum demand charges at 90% of contracted capacity; a queue position is no longer the asset.
  2. Secure a delivery slot rather than a technology choice. With the three major backlogs between 35 GW and 116 GW and transformers at four years, the scarce item is the manufacturing window, and it is being sold forward now.
  3. Put a river test into asset valuation and fuel logistics before the next planning round. The Rhine and the Danube took generation and fuel deliveries offline this summer, and the same hazard arrived on the demand side as record peaks.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Themes

The cycle's signals are organised into five themes, ranked by impact on near-term decisions. Immediate: changes the near-term plan, the corporate structure or the proposition. Near-Term: changes competitive position over the next twelve months. Longer-Range: a multi-year structural factor to track and revisit each cycle.

1. Paying more did not buy more power

Immediate

The price signal your capital plan relies on has stopped clearing supply, and that changes what a capacity charge tells you. PJM's 2028/2029 auction hit its administrative ceiling, closed 6,831.3 MW below the reliability requirement it exists to meet, and drew forward 317.1 MW of new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). Any plan that treats a rising capacity price as a signal that supply is coming is reading the wrong instrument: it is a signal that supply is absent. The immediate consequence is that scarcity cost now lands on the bill and on the political calendar rather than on the build programme.

PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction: what the ceiling price procured

Source: PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, July 2026. Bars are to scale against each other.

  • The auction cleared at its own ceiling for the third consecutive time, which means the price no longer carries information about how short the system is (Utility Dive, Jul 2026).
  • PJM says the mechanism is working as designed, not failing: its market-design paper names a credibility trap and states that “The market is generating the price signal it was designed to generate during a shortage.” (PJM, Powering Reliability Through Market Design, May 2026)
  • The cost is already in household bills. New Jersey regulators put the increase at around 20%, with capacity charges accounting for 15% to 20% of a residential bill (New Jersey Board of Public Utilities, Aug 2026).
  • ERCOT is the control case: an hourly record of 91.1 GW on 22 July, 6% above the 2023 record, met primarily with gas at 48% and solar at 32% (US Energy Information Administration, Aug 2026), with over 20,000 MW of committed headroom at the moment the record was set (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026).

Counter-argument

The most credible challenge is that a capped auction cannot be judged on what it procured, because the cap itself suppressed entry: a price allowed to rise further might have cleared more supply, and the states that negotiated the ceiling own that trade-off. Sierra Club argues the opposite, that annual customer costs in PJM have risen by $12.5bn since June 2025 and the cap is insufficient rather than restrictive (Sierra Club, Jul 2026). Both readings can hold, and they converge on the same test: whether the 2029/2030 auction, run under whatever cap then applies, brings forward materially more than 317.1 MW. If it does, the constraint was the ceiling. If it does not, the constraint is delivery, and this theme holds.

2. The regulator rewrote the rules for every large load

Immediate

Within four months, four jurisdictions independently converted “connect a large customer” from an engineering request into a conditional contract, and they did it without coordinating. If your growth plan assumes the terms you signed under, or expected to sign under, it is already out of date in at least one market you operate in. The practical consequence is that the binding variable has moved from whether power is available to what you must accept in order to take it.

  • FERC issued section 206 show-cause orders on 18 June to all six organised US markets, requiring each to justify or reform how it integrates large loads, with a 17 August response deadline (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026); the agency chose regional rules over a national standard and kept intervention in reserve (Utility Dive, Jun 2026).
  • Texas went furthest on operational terms: the Public Utility Commission's 24 July order affirms that a co-located large load must be capable of full disconnection within thirty minutes, regardless of the generation paired with it (White & Case, Aug 2026).
  • Britain attacked the queue rather than the connection. Ofgem is clearing speculative projects from a pipeline that has grown from 41 GW to 125 GW, of which roughly 80 GW is data centres, and is introducing a commitment fee (Ofgem, Jul 2026), with deposits, progression fees and post-offer milestones proposed for demand connections (Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer, Jul 2026).
  • Japan regulated the load itself rather than its connection: from April 2026 data-centre operators must report electricity use and PUE, publish targets annually, and reach 1.4 or below by fiscal 2030 and 1.3 for centres built from fiscal 2029 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Access as the sanction. Spain's draft decree would make repeated failure to source 80% of supply from new renewables cost a data centre its network access outright. The Spanish is unambiguous: “El incumplimiento reiterado podrá acarrear la pérdida del acceso a la red.” (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026) It gains weight if a second member state copies revocable access rather than a financial penalty.
  • Weak signal Efficiency floors as entry control. Japan's 1.3 PUE requirement for centres built from fiscal 2029 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026) is a hard performance number aimed at the load rather than the grid. It gains weight if a US state or the Commission adopts a numeric floor in a tariff.

Counter-argument

The reforms may reprice access without restricting it. FERC explicitly preserved state authority over retail sales, eligibility and siting (Utility Dive, Jun 2026), and its own docket frames the objective as connection that is “timely, orderly, reliable, and non-discriminatory” (FERC Docket RM26-4, accessed Sep 2026), which is an acceleration mandate as much as a restriction. NERC's assessment records 58.5 GW of resource additions against 11 GW of demand growth (NERC 2026 Summer Reliability Assessment, May 2026), so the adequacy case for tightening is not uniform. The test is whether any of the six RTO filings made after 17 August actually reduces the volume of load that can connect, rather than reordering who connects first.

3. Who pays for the grid became the live political question

Immediate

Cost allocation stopped being a rate-case technicality and became an instrument with teeth, decided in public and often in an election year. For anyone building, financing or supplying a large load, the jurisdiction's chosen instrument now materially changes the project's cost base, and the instruments differ more than the underlying problem does. The consequence is that the same data centre carries a different balance sheet in Oregon, Madrid and Munich.

Where Theme 1 meets Theme 3

Drawn from the cross-impact grid in the Energy, Infrastructure and Climate Resilience Change Tracker (27 August 2026 cycle), re-sourced against this cycle's evidence base.

  • Twenty-three US states hold at least one large-load tariff and seven more have proposals pending, with Virginia's effective from 1 January 2027 (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026); new tariff filings ran at 29 approvals in 2025 against 14 across the whole of 2018 to 2024 (Smart Electric Power Alliance, Apr 2026).
  • Oregon shows what a settled instrument looks like: contracts of 10 to 30 years by project size, 100% of distribution upgrade costs on the customer, minimum demand charges at 90% of contracted capacity, and a 1 cent/kWh surcharge above 100 MW recycled to residential and low-income customers (Utility Dive, May 2026).
  • Washington answered with voluntary commitment rather than tariff: the expanded Ratepayer Protection Pledge claims coverage of 80% of all power delivered to US homes and businesses (the White House, Jul 2026), a scale claim that rests on the signatories honouring it.
  • Europe is using two different levers at once: Germany has directed a federal subsidy at transmission charges and reformed network-cost sharing (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026), while the Commission proposed in July to cut network charges for specific consumer groups (European Commission, updated Jul 2026) and Spain would attach a renewable-sourcing condition to access itself (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026).

Counter-argument

A tariff written for a boom is tested by a bust. If the forecast load does not arrive, the network built against it is still in the rate base, and the developer-pays terms that look protective today are only as good as the counterparty's balance sheet in 2031. The clearest warning sits in the funding mix rather than the tariff: hyperscaler incremental debt as a share of capex rose from 9% in FY24 to 32% in the year to June 2026 (FactSet, Jul 2026). On the available evidence the instruments transfer cost but not the underlying demand risk, and that residual returns to the household bill by default.

4. You cannot buy a turbine before 2030

Near-Term

The gas option is not expensive, it is unavailable, and that reorders every capital plan built on it over the next twelve months. The three large-frame manufacturers have sold their output to the end of the decade, the supporting equipment is on comparable lead times, and the cost of the units that can be delivered has risen faster than utility planning assumptions. The competitive consequence is that whoever holds a delivery slot holds the scarce asset, and whoever does not is choosing between waiting and building something else.

  • GE Vernova's own quarterly statement is the clearest measure of the gap: “Gas Power equipment backlog and slot reservation agreements grew from 100 to 116 GW; now anticipate reaching at least 125 GW by year-end 2026” (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). Annual output is planned to reach 30 GW by 2030, which is after the window most 2026 plans need it in.
  • Mitsubishi's large-frame backlog reached 35 GW against 23 GW a year earlier, and orders booked in the first quarter of 2026 are scheduled for delivery between 2028 and 2030 (Utility Dive, Aug 2026); Siemens Energy's stands near 69 GW with lead times beyond three years (Utility Dive, Aug 2026) and Ansaldo is expanding into the same gap (POWER, Sep 2026).
  • The supporting equipment is no better: large power transformer lead times have stretched to four years, with generator step-up transformer demand up 274% between 2019 and 2025 (pv magazine USA, May 2026).
  • Price has moved with scarcity. IEEFA puts the newest combined-cycle proposals at least $2,500 per kW installed, or $2.5bn for a 1,000 MW unit (IEEFA, Apr 2026), while utilities told second-quarter investors they are defending execution and ratepayer protection rather than negotiating on price (Utility Dive, Aug 2026).

Counter-argument

Scarcity in one technology is not scarcity in supply. In the first half of 2026 the United States brought online 11,458 MW of utility-scale solar and 8,207 MW of batteries against 2,707 MW of natural gas across 46 projects (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026), so the constraint binds the gas route rather than capacity additions as a whole, and the manufacturers are doubling capacity against 2024 levels (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). The honest limit of that rebuttal is seasonal: solar at 32% of a Texan evening peak (US Energy Information Administration, Aug 2026) is not the same resource as firm capacity on a still winter night, which is the hour the capacity auction is actually procuring for.

5. Heat and low water stopped being a weather story

Near-Term

River flow and river temperature moved from a footnote in an outage report to an input in dispatch, fuel logistics and asset valuation, and they did so across two river basins in one season. If you own, finance or buy from a thermal or nuclear plant that takes cooling water from a river, the availability assumption in your model is now the thing most likely to be wrong. The same hazard also arrives on the demand side, which is why it belongs in the capacity conversation rather than beside it.

  • Romania's Cernavoda plant, normally a fifth of national electricity, was fully offline in August for only the second time in its history, the first being 2003 (Euronews, Aug 2026); the water authority put Danube flow at Romania's border at 1,650 cubic metres per second against a July average of 4,750, approaching the 1985 minimum of 1,400 (Mediafax, Jul 2026).
  • France lost output through the summer: around 3% in June and around 6% in July, with a record 15.6% of capacity missing (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026), and 13 of 57 reactors affected at the peak of the episode (Selectra, Aug 2026).
  • Fuel could not move either. The Rhine at Kaub fell to 28 centimetres, the lowest since 2018, with coal inventories at Amsterdam, Rotterdam and Antwerp up about 75% since spring while barge loadings inland were cut by three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026): the coal existed, it was simply in the wrong place.
  • Alpine hydro ran at 51% of normal in Austria, 48% in Switzerland, 63% in Germany and 68% in France in July (Euronews, Aug 2026), with the substitute bought at the margin: EU gas generation rose 8% last year as hydro fell 12%.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Biology as an outage cause. Jellyfish blockages at Gravelines sat alongside heat as a cause of lost French output this summer (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026). It gains weight if a second operator reports a biological intake event in a different basin within a year.
  • Weak signal A broader but shallower drought. The Joint Research Centre records half of the EU and the UK in drought but only 9% of territory at alert level in July, against 18% at the 2022 peak (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026). It gains weight, as a counter-indicator, if the 2027 season repeats the wide-and-shallow pattern rather than the intense one.

Counter-argument

2026 may be wide rather than deep. The Joint Research Centre's own comparison puts 9% of EU and UK territory at alert level in July against 18% at the 2022 peak and 6% in late August 2018 (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026), which is a different shape of event rather than an unambiguously worse one. France's system operator stated before the summer that security of supply required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026), and it was right: no shortfall materialised. The theme therefore claims availability risk and cost, not reliability failure, and it is falsified if the 2027 season passes with French unavailability back inside its historic band and no repeat river-logistics event.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's evidence into the next planning round. Each names the move, the horizon and a decision posture.

SI 1: Re-underwrite large-load connections as long-dated contracts, not queue positions

Whoever owns site strategy should re-price every live and planned large-load connection against the terms now being approved, before the next capital approval rather than after it. Oregon's framework runs 10 to 30 years by project size, puts 100% of distribution upgrade costs on the customer, sets minimum generation and transmission demand charges at 90% of contracted capacity and adds a 1 cent/kWh surcharge above 100 MW (Utility Dive, May 2026); twenty-three states now hold a comparable instrument with seven more pending (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026); and Texas requires full disconnection within thirty minutes regardless of paired generation (White & Case, Aug 2026). These are balance-sheet commitments with exit costs, and they are being decided now, which is why this is a Decide rather than a Prepare. The reason to move before the next approval is that the six RTO responses filed after FERC's 17 August deadline (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026) will set the terms in the remaining markets while the approval is in flight.

Action: by 31 October 2026, the site or origination lead produces a one-page term exposure sheet per live connection covering contract length, minimum charge floor, upgrade-cost share and exit liability, and names the internal owner of each.

Decide Draws on Themes 1, 2 and 3.

SI 2: Buy the delivery slot, not the technology

Capital planning should treat the manufacturing window as the scarce asset and decide its position on slot reservation before the 2027 budget closes. GE Vernova moved from 100 GW to 116 GW of backlog plus slot reservation agreements in a single quarter and expects at least 125 GW under contract by year end (GE Vernova, Jul 2026), Mitsubishi is delivering first-quarter 2026 orders in 2028 to 2030 (Utility Dive, Aug 2026), and transformers run to four years (pv magazine USA, May 2026). The posture is Prepare rather than Decide because the right answer depends on a firm-capacity requirement the organisation may not yet have quantified: what actually came online in the first half of 2026 was 11,458 MW of solar and 8,207 MW of batteries against 2,707 MW of gas (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026), and for many load shapes that is the better bridge. The decision to avoid is waiting for turbine prices to fall, at $2,500 per kW and rising (IEEFA, Apr 2026).

Action: by 15 December 2026, capital planning states a firm-capacity requirement by season and hour for the 2029 to 2032 window, and a reserve-or-decline position on slot reservation against it.

Prepare Draws on Themes 4 and 1.

SI 3: Put a water and river-temperature test into valuation and fuel logistics

Asset owners, lenders and insurers should add a water-availability case to thermal and nuclear valuation, and to any fuel contract that moves by barge, ahead of the next revaluation cycle rather than after the next summer. This season produced a full Cernavoda shutdown for only the second time in the plant's history (Euronews, Aug 2026), a record 15.6% of French capacity missing (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026), Alpine hydro at roughly half of normal (Euronews, Aug 2026) and a three-quarters cut in inland coal barge loadings with the fuel itself in surplus at the coast (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026). The posture is Prepare because no reliability event followed and France's system operator was right that supply security required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026): the exposure is availability, cost and contractual, not systemic failure. That is precisely the kind of exposure a valuation model absorbs quietly and a credit committee never sees.

Action: before the next annual revaluation, risk adds a six-week water-constraint case to every river-cooled and barge-supplied asset in the book, and reports the revenue-at-risk figure to the audit or risk committee.

Prepare Draws on Themes 5 and 3.

SI 4: Track which cost-allocation instrument each jurisdiction actually chooses

Government affairs and regulatory teams should maintain a live view of the instrument in use in each market, because the instruments are diverging and each allocates the residual differently. The United States is using state tariffs (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026) alongside a voluntary federal pledge claimed to cover 80% of power delivered to homes and businesses (the White House, Jul 2026); Germany is subsidising transmission charges (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026); the Commission proposed cutting network charges for specific consumer groups (European Commission, updated Jul 2026); Spain would make access revocable (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026). Monitor is the right posture because none of these is yet stress-tested against a demand disappointment, and the signal that matters is not the announcement but the first instrument that survives one.

Action: from Q4 2026, the regulatory lead maintains a one-line-per-jurisdiction instrument register, reviewed at each quarterly risk meeting, flagging any move from voluntary pledge to enforceable tariff or from financial penalty to access conditionality.

Monitor Draws on Themes 3, 2 and 1.

Scenario Matrix

The two uncertainties that will shape this system more than any others over the next two to five years are whether the cost-allocation question settles into durable instruments, and how fast deliverable supply actually arrives. Cost allocation is the vertical axis, settled at the top and contested at the bottom. Supply delivery is the horizontal axis, slow on the left and fast on the right. The Priced Queue

Tariffs and conditional connection terms hold. A large load knows what it will pay and for how long, and the terms survive a downturn because the developer, not the household, carries the network commitment. But turbines, transformers and connection dates stay scarce, so the settled rules mostly ration a queue rather than shorten it. Capacity prices stay at or near their ceilings and stop carrying information; procurement moves to bilateral contracts and slot reservation. Advantage goes to incumbents with existing interconnection rights and to anyone who reserved manufacturing capacity early. The political temperature falls because the allocation question has an answer, even though the physical shortfall persists.

Early indicators: Second and third states adopting Oregon-style term-and-collateral tariffs; a capacity auction clearing below its cap without a rule change; secondary trading in turbine slots becoming visible.

Build and Bill

The instruments hold and deliverable supply arrives, mostly as solar, storage and flexible load rather than as new gas. Manufacturing expansion lands, connection reform releases stalled projects onto non-firm terms, and the shortfall narrows without the household bill absorbing it. Capacity prices fall away from their ceilings and resume signalling. The competitive question shifts from access to operating cost, and the winners are those who can run flexibly against a volatile but adequate system. Water and heat remain the residual constraint, because they bind the existing thermal fleet regardless of how much new capacity is added, and they become the main source of surprise in an otherwise comfortable environment.

Early indicators: A large-load tariff surviving a cancelled project without a rate-base transfer; storage additions continuing above 8 GW per half-year; an RTO reporting a fall in firm-capacity shortfall.

The Blame Auction

The worst combination and the closest to the present. Instruments are announced but not tested, supply stays scarce, and the argument moves from how to build to who is at fault. Price caps are negotiated politically rather than set analytically, so the auction result becomes a communications exercise and the shortfall compounds quietly underneath it. Emergency authorities substitute for procurement. Large loads respond by going behind the meter where they can, which removes the flexibility the grid was counting on. Litigation and state-versus-federal jurisdiction fights consume the time that planning needs, and the cost eventually surfaces in bills that nobody has agreed to defend.

Early indicators: A fourth consecutive capped auction; a state withdrawing from a negotiated ceiling; growth in off-grid or prime-power data-centre announcements.

Behind the Meter

Supply arrives quickly but nobody agrees who should pay for the network that carries it. Large loads self-supply, co-locate and contract directly with generators, and the regulated system is left with the fixed costs and a shrinking base to spread them over. Household bills rise for reasons that have nothing to do with household consumption, which makes the politics worse rather than better. Regulators respond with exit fees and standby charges, and the contest moves from connection terms to departure terms. For an operator this is the environment with the widest gap between a good commercial outcome and a defensible public position, and the reputational exposure arrives before the regulatory one.

Early indicators: Rising standby and exit-fee filings; co-location approvals accelerating after the Texas order; a utility reporting falling regulated load against rising regional consumption.

What We Are Not Planning For

Systemic reliability

A drought-driven European supply failure this winter

The 2026 season took generation offline without taking the system down, and the operator best placed to judge said so in advance: France's system operator stated that security of electricity supply required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026), and no shortfall followed. The Joint Research Centre's own comparison puts 9% of EU and UK territory at alert level in July against 18% at the 2022 peak (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026), so this was a broader but less intense event than the one Europe already absorbed. The exposure this briefing carries is availability and cost, which is why it sits inside Theme 5 rather than here.

↺ Reinstated if a member state issues a winter supply warning attributable to hydrology, or French unavailability exceeds its 2022 peak.

Jurisdiction

Federal preemption of state cost allocation in the United States

It would resolve the messiest question in this cycle at a stroke, and there is no evidence it is coming. FERC explicitly preserved state authority over retail sales, customer eligibility and siting when it issued the show-cause orders (Utility Dive, Jun 2026), and chose regional rules over a national standard (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026). The federal instrument actually deployed was a voluntary pledge (the White House, Jul 2026), which is the opposite of preemption. Planning for a single national rule would mean ignoring the twenty-three different instruments that already exist (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026).

↺ Reinstated if FERC opens a rulemaking that reaches retail cost allocation, or federal legislation on large-load ratemaking is introduced with committee support.

Demand

A collapse in data-centre electricity demand inside the planning horizon

The demand correction that would relieve every constraint in this briefing is not visible in the spending. Aggregate capex across the five largest hyperscalers reached roughly $490bn in the twelve months to May 2026 (FactSet, Jul 2026), and ERCOT is planning against roughly 175,000 MW by 2032 (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026). What did change is the funding mix, with incremental debt rising from 9% to 32% of capex (FactSet, Jul 2026). That is a real risk and it is tracked in Theme 3's counter-argument, but it is a risk to returns and to who absorbs stranded network cost, not a near-term demand collapse.

↺ Reinstated if two consecutive quarters of aggregate hyperscaler capex guidance cuts, or the first RTO to report a net fall in active large-load interconnection requests.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If a capacity price at the ceiling the rules allow procured 317.1 MW against a 6,831.3 MW shortfall, what evidence would persuade us that the next auction is a procurement mechanism rather than a cost-allocation one, and what do we do differently in the meantime?
  2. Oregon's approved terms run to thirty years with minimum charges at 90% of contracted capacity. Which of our live connection plans would we still approve on those terms, and who signs that off?
  3. We can have a turbine slot or we can have optionality on technology, and at four-year transformer lead times we probably cannot have both. Which do we buy, and what firm-capacity number are we buying it against?
  4. If a load that has signed a developer-pays tariff fails in 2031, does the network cost sit with the developer's administrator or with the household bill, and have we read the instrument in every jurisdiction where we hold exposure?
  5. Jellyfish and a 34-year low on the Danube took capacity off two European systems in the same season. What is our revenue-at-risk if a water constraint binds for six weeks rather than six days, and is that number anywhere in our current model?

Claim-fidelity self-disclosure

This appendix records every claim in the briefing that goes beyond what a cited source literally states, so a reader can see where the analyst's judgment enters.

Cycle-over-cycle continuity claims

Every statement in this briefing about what a previous edition said is checked against that edition's own record, not against memory or paraphrase. Eight appear, all inside the Continuity ledger. Seven grade the seven milestones the 16 July edition set for this cycle: the Q2 capex prints, the Atlantic season against the soft-pricing bet, whether any grid executed data-centre curtailment, the California FAIR Plan trajectory, data-centre water-permit denials, the Bank of England stress-test results, and the PJM 2028/2029 clearing price. The eighth is a correction rather than a grade. The July edition recorded the 2 July PJM peak as roughly 162.7 GW and concluded that the 2006 record had held; PJM's own account puts the peak at 168,158 MW, above the 165,563 MW record. That reading is withdrawn here rather than quietly restated, because it was load-bearing for July's argument that the grid passed the summer without breaking anything.

Superlatives used

Every "first", "only", "largest" and "record" in the briefing was checked against the source behind it. Most turn out to be calendar or counting words rather than claims ("first quarter", "first half of 2026", "only 9% of territory"). Five are genuine superlatives and each is carried by a source using the same word for the same referent: "only the second time in its history" for the Cernavoda shutdown, and "just once before, in 2003", are Euronews's characterisation; "record 15.6%" of French capacity missing is AFP's; "all-time peak" for the 2 July PJM record is PJM's own; "lowest since 2018" for the Rhine at Kaub is Insurance Journal's. Three are analyst characterisation with no source behind them, kept because they are framing rather than claims, and disclosed here: "the defining fact of this cycle", "the three large-frame turbine makers have sold the decade", and "the clearest measure of the gap" for GE Vernova's own statement. Three further superlatives were removed at draft rather than defended: an earlier sit-up line claiming the auction cleared at "the highest price on record" was factually wrong, because the 2027/2028 auction cleared higher at $333.44/MW-day, and now reads "the highest price the rules allow"; "two of Europe's largest rivers" is now "the Rhine and the Danube"; and Japan's PUE floor is described as "a hard performance number" rather than "the first".

Analytical synthesis claims

Four judgments read across sources and none is stated by any single source. First, that the binding constraint is delivery rather than willingness to pay: this draws on the PJM auction report, PJM's market-design paper, the three OEM order books and the EIA and Texas Tribune ERCOT figures, and is marked in the text as this briefing's reading. Second, that a price cap which holds the bill down while the shortfall widens is a bill-management instrument rather than a procurement one: this reads the Maryland and Pennsylvania releases against the auction report, and both governments would contest the characterisation. Third, that cost-allocation instruments transfer cost without transferring demand risk: this combines the Sabin Center's state count, the Oregon terms and the FactSet funding data, and is framed in the text as "on the available evidence". Fourth, that river flow and temperature have become scheduling inputs rather than tail risks: this reads the Romanian, French, Alpine and Rhine evidence together and is framed as "taken together, the sources suggest". The counter-argument in each theme is the disconfirming case for that judgment, not a rival frame.
