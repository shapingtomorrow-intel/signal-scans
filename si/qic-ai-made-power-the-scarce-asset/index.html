Why This Matters Now

The money for AI data centres is there; the power to run them, and the permission to build them, are not, so the value in the AI buildout is moving to whoever can deliver an energised, approved site on time.

Grid planners have started to discount the pipeline of speculative projects before they plan for it. Capacity markets in the United States have hit the ceilings regulators set for them. Australian governments have agreed to make new data centres bring their own power and pay for their own water and connections, while arguing over whether coal and gas count.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: treat delivered power and local licence as the underwriting variables for every AI-linked infrastructure position, and treat the headline data-centre pipeline as a list of applications, not of loads. The cue to watch is the text of the Commonwealth standards due in early 2027: if they make data centres net generators, every firmed power project in the portfolio has a new class of buyer.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week assume capital is the scarce input in AI infrastructure?

The evidence in this report says it is delivered power.

This cycle sets the baseline for QIC on AI, digital infrastructure and power demand, and its central claim is that capital has stopped being the binding constraint on the AI buildout. At the time of publication of the December 2025 FSR, Bloomberg consensus estimates were for AI hyperscalers' capital expenditure for the year 2028 to be less than $600 billion; the same statistic is now over $1 trillion, and the funding has moved to debt: the five hyperscalers were 3% of outstanding US investment-grade debt at the end of 2025 but over 15% of year-to-date issuance by early May, AI issuers were 41% of non-refinancing US high-yield issuance, and private credit's share of AI investment rose from 9% in 2024 to 34% in 2025 (Bank of England, July 2026). What that capital cannot buy on demand is an energised, approved site. In the United States, electricity demand is projected to grow by nearly 2% annually through 2030 with around half of the increase driven by data centres, while more than 2 500 gigawatts of projects, large loads included, remain stalled in grid connection queues worldwide (IEA, February 2026). PJM's auction for 2028/2029 cleared at the FERC-approved cap of $325/MW-day across its whole footprint, cleared only 525 MW of new generation and uprates, and still came in short of the reliability requirement by 6,831 MW (PJM Interconnection, July 2026).

Australia has arrived at the same place with its own mechanisms. Demand: AEMO forecasts data centre electricity consumption in the NEM rising from approximately 5 TWh in 2025-26 to 34 TWh by 2035-36, inside a system whose consumption rises by more than 40% while around 15 GW of coal and gas retires (AEMO, August 2026). Pipeline: 11 large projects representing 5.4 GW of maximum demand were in the NEM connection process at the end of March, around 60% in New South Wales and 40% in Victoria, on two-year connection timelines and 5-10 year ramps (AEMO, June 2026); across all large loads, 225 projects with a combined 67 GW had made connection requests without moving to construction, and 31% of data centre projects awarded connection agreements have not gone ahead (The Energy, August 2026). Licence: National Cabinet agreed that large data centres bring material energy, water and land-use impacts and that the Commonwealth will develop consistent mandatory standards, to be legislated in early 2027 (Prime Minister of Australia, August 2026); the Energy Minister says the standards come with no exceptions and no carve-outs and require new supply, while Queensland claims a win that lets data centres draw on its gas and surplus coal (ABC News, August 2026). Delivery: the 2026 ISP's optimal path needs $106 billion to 2050 and grid-scale wind and solar rising from 23 GW to 61 GW by 2030, and AEMO's own reading is that the current connections pipeline is encouraging, but on its own would not reach the ODP's projected level of generation capacity for 2030 (AEMO, June 2026).

Whoever can energise a site on time sets the price of the AI buildout.

Taken together, the sources suggest a reversal an infrastructure investor should act on: the asset that carries the AI premium is no longer the data centre but the power and the permission behind it. The surprise for leadership is the counterparty flip. Once a data centre must bring its own supply, it stops being a tenant and becomes a buyer of exactly what QIC's portfolio owns: firmed generation, storage, network capacity and approved industrial land. Whoever can energise a site on time sets the price of the AI buildout.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The synthesis rests on one assumption: that the data-centre load now in Australian and US queues is large enough, after probability-weighting, to keep delivered power scarce through 2028. If AEMO's weighted forecasts and PJM's next auction show the pipeline collapsing faster than supply is added, capacity prices would fall from their caps, the 40 GW Australian pipeline would arrive into a softer market, and the premium on energised sites would narrow to a normal locational spread. The evidence that would force revision is specific: a 2027 PJM clearing price materially below the cap with reserve margins restored, or an AEMO update that cuts the 2035-36 data-centre forecast while the 9.1 GW annual connection pace holds. Either would mean the buildout is power-rich, not power-constrained, and the report's returns argument would invert.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Re-underwrite every AI-linked position on delivered power and licence, not on the tenant. The evidence says the queue, the connection date and the approval are where value and risk concentrate.
  2. Position the portfolio's power, storage and network assets as counterparties to bring-your-own-supply data centres before the 2027 standards land. The Commonwealth is about to define a new buyer class; the first contracts will set the terms.
  3. Adopt a probability-weighted view of the data-centre pipeline in every forecast QIC relies on. A third of connected projects did not proceed; sizing to headline enquiries overbuilds.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

1. Power delivery and local licence, not capital, now pace the AI buildout

Immediate

QIC's AI exposure is a power exposure, and the evidence this cycle says the power is the scarce part. The grid operators on both sides of the Pacific now describe the same shape: demand that is real and concentrated, a pipeline that must be discounted before it is planned for, and a supply build that is large but late. For an owner of generation, storage, networks and industrial land the consequence is a repricing of what is already held, and a new class of buyer that regulation is about to create. The risk sits in the same place: a portfolio sized to the headline queue rather than to the load that energises.

  • Australia's data-centre load is forecast to grow roughly sevenfold in a decade, from approximately 5 TWh in 2025-26 to 34 TWh by 2035-36, inside a system that must add a 40 GW pipeline by the early 2030s while around 15 GW of coal and gas retires, and AEMO sees no forecast reliability gaps before 2030 only if that pipeline is delivered on time (AEMO, August 2026).
  • The queue is not the load: 11 data-centre projects representing 5.4 GW of maximum demand were in the NEM connection process at the end of March 2026, most at early stages and concentrated in New South Wales and Victoria (AEMO, June 2026), while AEMO's forecaster now applies a weighted probabilities method because 31% of data-centre projects awarded connection agreements have not gone ahead (The Energy, August 2026).
  • US capacity markets have hit the ceiling regulators set for them: PJM's 2028/2029 auction cleared at the FERC-approved cap of $325/MW-day, fell 6,831 MW short of the reliability requirement and cleared 525 MW of new generation (PJM Interconnection, July 2026), and the market monitor attributes $6.3 billion, or 38%, of the $16.4 billion in charges to data centres (Utility Dive, July 2026).
  • Licence has become a siting gate in law: National Cabinet agreed that large data centres bring material energy, water and land-use impacts and that mandatory standards will be legislated in early 2027 (Prime Minister of Australia, August 2026), with the Energy Minister insisting on no exceptions and no carve-outs and warning that an open-slather approach in Queensland would lift wholesale bills by 13% (ABC News, August 2026).

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalBring-your-own-supply as the licence condition. If the 2027 legislation makes large data centres underwrite new generation, every firmed renewable and storage project in the NEM gains a creditworthy offtaker class; it gains weight if the exposure draft carries a net-supply obligation rather than a certificate purchase.
  • Weak signalSeparate procurement for data-centre load. PJM's market monitor wants data centres procured in their own auction (Utility Dive, July 2026); it gains weight if PJM or FERC opens a docket on it, because a separate price for computational load would travel to other markets, Australia included.
  • Weak signalQueensland as the low-cost firm-power location. The Premier is pitching the state's gas and surplus coal-fired generation to data centres (ABC News, August 2026); it gains weight if the Commonwealth standards allow firming from the existing fleet, which would move siting north and put Queensland network and land assets in the path of the load.
  • Weak signalLoad growth that lowers bills. Columbia's review finds hyperscale data centres can leave retail rates neutral or better where interconnection costs are ring-fenced (Columbia CGEP, June 2026); it gains weight if an Australian regulator adopts beneficiary-pays connection charging, which would blunt the political case against the buildout.

Counter-argument

The most credible challenge is that the AI load is smaller and softer than the headlines, so the scarcity is temporary and building power for it strands assets. Grattan puts data centres at just 17% of future power demand growth and argues the real problem is a slow build-out for every source of demand (Grattan Institute, September 2026); a third of connected Australian projects did not proceed (The Energy, August 2026); and Columbia's review shows that where costs are ring-fenced, data centres pay more than the marginal cost of serving them, so the political constraint may ease (Columbia CGEP, June 2026). On that reading the premium on energised sites is a 2026-2028 spike, not a durable feature, and the scarce asset reverts to being the cheapest generation.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's signals into the FY27 deployment plan. Each names the move, an owner, a date and a decision posture.

SI 1: Re-underwrite AI-linked positions on delivered power and licence

The Global Infrastructure investment committee should adopt, for the FY27 round, a rule that no AI-linked commitment is priced on tenant demand alone. Each case should carry a dated connection path, an energisation date the network has confirmed, a planning consent status, and a probability-weighted load that applies the attrition AEMO's forecaster now applies, rather than the headline queue. The reason is in the evidence: the queue holds 5.4 GW of data-centre demand and 67 GW of large-load requests, a third of connected projects never proceeded, and the assets that cleared PJM's auction were the ones already built. A position underwritten on the tenant carries the phantom; one underwritten on the connection carries the premium.

Action: The Head of Global Infrastructure tables the power-and-licence underwriting standard for approval at the December 2026 investment committee, applied to every AI-linked paper from January 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1.

SI 2: Make the portfolio's power and network assets contract-ready for bring-your-own-supply data centres

The Head of Asset Management should have three portfolio assets, chosen for firm capacity, storage or network headroom, holding contract-ready offers for data-centre counterparties before the Commonwealth standards are legislated in early 2027. The offers should price firming and grid support separately from energy, because ride-through and grid-stability obligations are moving onto the load. The reason is the counterparty flip: a data centre that must underwrite new supply is a buyer of what QIC owns, and the first contracts written under the new standards will set the market's terms.

Action: Head of Asset Management to deliver three contract-ready power, storage or network offers to the investment committee by 31 March 2027, with firming and grid-support priced as separate lines.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1.

SI 3: Turn resilience and community credentials into a consent and connection asset

The Sustainability and Resilience lead should convert QIC's resilience, water and community practice into a documented consent-and-connection case that bid teams can use, measured against the energy, water and land-use standards National Cabinet has agreed to legislate. The reason is that licence has joined power as a siting gate: a site that clears water, land and community tests earlier reaches energisation earlier, and the two-year connection timeline AEMO reports starts only once the application is complete.

Action: Sustainability and Resilience lead to publish the consent-and-connection playbook, mapped to the exposure draft of the Commonwealth standards, by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1.

SI 4: Watch the financing of the buildout for the correction that would reprice the tenants

The Clients and Capital team, with the risk function, should track the debt-funded financing of the buildout as the cycle risk that could reach QIC's counterparties. The Bank of England's July report shows capex consensus for 2028 above $1 trillion, hyperscalers at over 15% of investment-grade issuance, AI at 41% of high-yield issuance and private credit at 34% of AI investment; a repricing there would not cancel the load, but it would sort tenants by balance sheet. No capital commitment follows yet; the trigger is a named one.

Action: Clients and Capital to add a quarterly AI-financing indicator to client reporting from the December 2026 quarter, with a named trigger: a hyperscaler cutting 2027 capex guidance, or a widening in AI high-yield spreads against the index, moves this to Prepare.

Contracted and Connected

Supply arrives on the ISP's schedule and most of the queued load energises. Data centres sign as net-supply buyers under the 2027 standards, firmed renewables and storage find long-dated offtake, and capacity prices ease from their caps as new generation clears. The premium on energised sites narrows to a locational spread, but the volume of contracts is high and the counterparties are creditworthy. Returns come from scale and contract quality rather than scarcity; network assets earn on throughput; the political argument over bills fades because beneficiary-pays connection charging holds.

Early indicators: New generation clearing at scale in PJM's 2027 auction; AEMO's weighted forecast converging on the headline queue; Australian standards adopted with net-supply obligations and no state carve-outs.

Overbuilt Grid

Supply arrives but the load does not: the phantom share of the pipeline proves larger than the forecaster assumed, duplicate applications collapse, and generation and firming built for data centres serves a smaller market. Capacity prices fall well below their caps, merchant firming assets in weak regions face stranding, and ratepayer politics turns from bills to unused build. Energised sites lose their premium; the opportunity moves to buying distressed power and shell assets from developers who financed on debt.

Early indicators: AEMO cutting its data-centre forecast while connection pace holds; capacity clearing prices falling from the cap; developers reporting cancellations of connection agreements above the historical 31%.

Rationed Power

Load arrives faster than supply: queues lengthen, the ISP's 2030 build slips, and capacity markets stay at their caps with reserve margins below requirement. Governments ration through standards, tariffs and moratoria; states compete on their existing fleets; behind-the-meter generation bypasses the queue. This is the scenario in which energised, approved capacity carries its highest premium and in which QIC's existing power, storage and network assets are repriced hardest, alongside the sharpest political exposure over bills and water.

Early indicators: PJM 2027 auction at the cap with the shortfall widening; AEMO reliability gaps brought forward before 2030; a state moratorium bill on data centres in Australia.

Stalled on Both Sides

Neither the load nor the supply arrives on plan: phantom applications clog queues that slow build-out further, standards are legislated but contested state by state, and the AI financing cycle corrects, sorting tenants by balance sheet. Activity is high but conversion is low; returns on new power and digital assets are thin and delayed, while existing connected assets hold value by default. The premium is on patience and on counterparties that survive the correction.

Early indicators: A hyperscaler cutting 2027 capex guidance; AI high-yield spreads widening against the index; Commonwealth standards delayed past mid-2027 by state disputes.

What We Are Not Planning For

Demand discontinuity

A collapse in AI demand that empties the queue

The four scenarios already carry the phantom-load case, and the evidence this cycle is of demand that is real but overstated, not absent: AEMO forecasts sevenfold growth on a probability-weighted basis and PJM cleared at its cap. A demand collapse would be a financing event first, which Strategic Implication 4 monitors with a named trigger.

↺ Reinstated if two of the four largest hyperscalers cut 2027 capital expenditure guidance in the same quarter.

Technology rescue

New nuclear or long-duration storage arriving in time to relieve scarcity before 2030

Small modular reactors and multi-day storage are not in the 2026 ISP's optimal path before 2030 at scale, and the constraint this cycle identifies is connection and consent, which new generation technology does not shorten. They belong in the 2030s planning horizon, not the FY27 plan.

↺ Reinstated if an Australian regulator issues a construction licence for a grid-connected SMR, or a multi-day storage project wins a firming contract in the NEM.

Statutory prohibition

A statewide data-centre moratorium in Australia

US-style moratorium bills have not surfaced in Australian parliaments, and National Cabinet has chosen mandatory standards over prohibition; the political contest is over which fuels count, not whether to build. A moratorium would change the licence gate from conditional to closed and is tracked, not planned for.

↺ Reinstated if a state government introduces a moratorium bill, or a capital-city council adopts a blanket refusal policy for data-centre development applications.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. Should QIC underwrite AI-linked assets on the tenant's covenant or on the connection date, when the two now point to different prices?
  2. Which of the portfolio's power, storage and network assets should be offered to data-centre counterparties first, and at what firming premium?
  3. If the Commonwealth standards let data centres firm from the existing fleet, does QIC pursue Queensland siting or stay with New South Wales and Victoria where the queue is?
  4. At what phantom share of the pipeline does QIC stop treating AEMO's headline forecast as a demand case and start treating it as an applications list?
  5. Would QIC rather own the scarce energised site at today's premium, or the distressed shell in an overbuilt grid two years from now?

Analytical synthesis claims

The organising thesis, that capital is abundant and delivered power and local licence are the binding constraints, reads across the Bank of England Financial Stability Report, the IEA Electricity 2026 summary, the PJM auction release and the AEMO publications; no single source states it. The "counterparty flip" (a data centre required to bring its own supply becomes a buyer of power, storage and network capacity) is the analyst's inference from the National Cabinet communique and the ABC report of the Energy Minister's statements; the communique names mandatory energy standards without specifying a net-supply obligation, and the requirement to bring new supply is reported speech. "A third of connected projects never proceeded" and "a third of the connected projects never got built" restate The Energy's 31% figure, which applies to data-centre projects awarded connection agreements. "The assets that cleared PJM's auction were the ones already built" is analyst characterisation of the 525 MW new-generation figure against 138,318 MW procured. The two-year connection timeline is AEMO's reported current experience, not a rule. The claim that data centres in Australia are "about to become net-supply obligors" is conditional on the legislated text and is marked as such in the weak signals. The scenario matrix, the exclusions, the Futures Wheel and the two figures are analyst constructions; the wheel's three-ring expansion was authored to the generator's schema from the cycle's evidence because the expansion model was unreachable from the composition environment. The funnel figure mixes units by design and says so in its caption. At the analyst's instruction this cycle carries one theme rather than the format's five or six, so both figures and the weak-signals block sit inside that theme; the structural audit's theme-count rule is recorded as a documented exception in memory.json.
