Executive Synthesis

At a glance

Europe's late-stage capital question is one of deployment, not supply. Roughly five to six trillion euros of European long-only capital sits in pension funds, insurers, sovereign wealth funds and EU and national innovation vehicles. The Draghi report (September 2024) named the constraints: pension allocation rules, Solvency II capital charges on equity, and a fragmented listing architecture. Eighteen months on, the policy envelope has been drawn (EU Listing Act in force December 2024; Savings and Investments Union Communication COM(2025) 124 of March 2025; Solvency II amending Directive 2025/2 transposing by 29 January 2027) and the UK has gone first on deployment via the Mansion House Compact and the Pension Schemes Act 2026.

The binding question for Revolut, as the load-bearing European fintech test asset, is which of these reforms actually moves capital before the IPO window opens. Revolut's November 2025 secondary tender at a $75 billion valuation, led by Coatue, Greenoaks, Dragoneer and Fidelity with NVIDIA's NVentures, substituted for an IPO. The question is whether the next funding moment (primary capital, listing or both) lands inside a European demand pool that has actually deepened, or whether friend-shored sovereign capital and a Nasdaq listing remain the path of least resistance.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: the European policy stack on scale-up capital is now substantially defined and the deadlines fall inside Revolut's IPO-window arithmetic. The strategic posture decision is whether to treat the next 18 months as a window for active engagement with the European deployment-side reforms (Mansion House Accord progress, IORP II review, the EuVECA Q3 2026 review, the Council's December 2025 securitisation position) or to underwrite the IPO destination on the assumption that the pipes will not be built in time. Both are defensible. Cluster 4 stays open on which closes the gap by 2027-28.

The first six months of the cycle have produced four data points that, read together, reframe Revolut's late-stage capital question. Per the Draghi report (September 2024), the European competitiveness shortfall sits at a documented investment gap of roughly €750-800 billion per year, with pension-fund allocation rules, Solvency II capital charges and capital-market fragmentation named as the binding constraints. Per the Atomico State of European Tech 2025 report, European tech has been underfunded by $375 billion over the past ten years; nearly half of late-stage funding now comes from US and Asian investors, and European pension allocations to VC rose 55% in 2024 but still lag US allocations by a factor of three. Per the CFA Institute (March 2026), 72% of US venture capital originates from pension and insurer sources; in Europe the figure is 30%. Per the ECB Financial Stability Review (May 2026), the ECB now treats accelerating the Savings and Investments Union as a financial-stability priority, not a competitiveness one alone.

Read together, these four points describe one connected change. The policy and capital architecture for late-stage European capital is now substantially defined on paper: the EU Listing Act has staggered application dates from December 2024 to June 2026; the Savings and Investments Union Communication COM(2025) 124 of March 2025 named supervisory convergence, securitisation revival, retail savings accounts, supplementary pensions and an EU 28th regime for innovative companies as the legislative spine; the Solvency II amending Directive 2025/2 contains a long-term equity treatment Member States must transpose by 29 January 2027; the IORP II review and the PEPP review were proposed by the Commission in November 2025; and the Council agreed its securitisation general approach in December 2025. The UK has gone further on deployment: the Mansion House Accord of May 2025 expanded the Compact to 17 providers covering ~90% of active DC savers, committing 10% to private markets and 5% to UK assets by 2030, and the Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026) enacts the £25 billion megafund threshold and the value-for-money regime that operationalises the consolidation.

The strategic question for Revolut is the gap between the policy stack and the deployment timeline. The earliest material EU pension-allocation shift sits in 2027-28 at best; the UK Mansion House Compact has moved DC unlisted-equity allocation from £0.8 billion to £1.6 billion year-on-year, against a 2030 target consistent with £25-50 billion. Meanwhile the sovereign-wealth substitute is operational: Singapore's GIC was the largest sovereign-wealth investor into European tech in 2024 ($1.9 billion across two deals); Mubadala, ADQ, NBIM and the Saudi PIF have all been live in European late-stage rounds; and Revolut's own November 2025 $75 billion secondary tender drew US private-markets capital, NVIDIA's NVentures and Coatue rather than a European pension pool. Two paths are visible: friend-shored sovereign capital plus a Nasdaq listing, the route Klarna chose in September 2025 and Flutter and CRH chose before, or active engagement with the European deployment-side reforms. Which closes the depth gap by 2027-28 is the cluster-4 question this cycle leaves open.

The European capital pools are not the question. The European pipes are. The board decision in front of Revolut is whether the deployment-side reforms in train shift enough pension and insurer allocation to growth equity inside the IPO-window arithmetic, or whether to treat the friend-shored sovereign-and-Nasdaq path as the working substitute and budget the next 18 months around it.

If the European policy stack is substantially defined and the UK has the only running deployment experiment, what changes in Revolut's late-stage strategy between now and the 2027 IPO-window opening?

The evidence in this cycle says the answer is not "wait and see". Revolut's November 2025 $75 billion tender already substituted for an IPO; the next funding decision lands inside the EU Listing Act application window, the UK Pension Schemes Act 2026 implementation, the Solvency II Directive 2025/2 transposition deadline and the Council's securitisation legislative process. Each of these has an investor-relations and public-affairs leverage point Revolut can use, or not.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The cycle's central assumption is that European deployment-side reforms will not move enough European pension and insurer capital before the IPO-window opens to change the destination calculus. The analysis is wrong if any of three things happens. The Mansion House Accord cohort hits the £25 billion implied compliance number by end-2027 rather than slipping to 2030, and the FCA primary-market reforms attract a flagship fintech IPO that demonstrates depth. The EU 28th regime proposal (COM(2026) 321 final) ships through Parliament inside one year and creates a credible cross-border vehicle for late-stage European companies. The Solvency II long-term equity treatment is transposed early in Member States covering >50% of insurer AUM, and insurer allocation to growth equity moves above 2%. Watch the EBA's first FY27 reporting and the Bank of England's December 2026 Financial Stability Report.

Themes

Five themes, ordered by impact tier. Each names one leg of the European scale-up capital architecture and the underlying condition that holds it in place.

1. The deployment gap: capital exists, plumbing does not

Immediate

The deployment gap, not the supply gap, is the binding question. Roughly five to six trillion euros of European long-only capital sits in pension funds, insurers and sovereign vehicles; the Draghi report quantifies the investment shortfall at €750-800 billion a year. The constraints are documented and named: pension-system design, Solvency II capital charges on equity, fragmented capital-market plumbing. Bruegel argues the binding obstacle is unreformed pension systems specifically, not the market layer on top of them. The CFA records the pension-and-insurer VC origin share at 30% in Europe against 72% in the US. Whether the deployment-layer reforms in train can shift more than one percentage point of pension and insurer allocation to growth equity inside the next two budget cycles.

  • The load-bearing diagnostic: the Draghi report quantifies the EU's competitiveness shortfall versus the US and names the underdevelopment of funded pension systems, fragmented capital markets and Solvency II capital charges as binding constraints on European late-stage capital deployment. European Commission (Draghi) (September 2024, anchor).
  • The corroborating frame: the Letta report reframes the single market as the precondition for retaining European scale-ups and household savings inside the bloc, and proposes the "fifth freedom" of knowledge, a 28th regime for innovative companies and a savings and investments market. Council of the European Union (Letta) (April 2024, anchor).
  • The quantification: Atomico's State of European Tech 2025 records Europe underfunding its tech companies by $375 billion over ten years; nearly half of late-stage funding now comes from US and Asian investors; European pension allocations to VC rose 55% in 2024 but still lag US levels by a factor of three. Atomico with Invest Europe (November 2025).
  • The pension-and-insurer-origin gap: in the US, 72% of venture capital originates from pension and insurer sources; in Europe the figure is 30%, and Europe's venture landscape is crowded with small early-stage funds but lacks mid-sized vehicles able to write $25-100 million cheques. CFA Institute Market Integrity Insights (March 2026).
  • The mechanism diagnosis: Bruegel argues the real obstacle to EU capital market integration is unreformed pension systems, not market plumbing: private pension savings are limited by national rules, so far less money than there could be flows to capital markets. A separate Bruegel working paper disentangles size versus allocation in capital market development. Bruegel (November 2025).
  • The macro corroboration: the OECD Pension Markets in Focus 2025 records pension assets at $69.8 trillion at end-2024; bonds and equities dominate allocations in 34 of 38 OECD countries; alternatives, real estate and private investment funds remain a small share. OECD (November 2025).
  • The regulator-on-record: the ECB Financial Stability Review states that accelerating progress on the SIU is essential to support growth and competitiveness while safeguarding financial stability, and flags persistent liquidity and leverage vulnerabilities in non-bank financial intermediation. European Central Bank (May 2026).

The case against this framing

The deployment-gap framing may understate the supply problem. European pension assets are smaller per head than US assets to begin with: the Bruegel size-versus-allocation paper argues size is a real and binding factor, and pension consolidation alone cannot close the gap if the underlying funded pension system remains under-built (Germany's contributory pillar continues to dominate). This counter would gain weight if the Mansion House Accord cohort moved aggressively on allocation while the underlying private-pension AUM growth stalled below 5% per year, or if the EU IORP II review produced softer-than-expected reforms and the supplementary-pensions Recommendation failed to gain Council traction.

2. The EU policy stack: Listing Act to Savings and Investments Union

Immediate

The EU policy stack has moved from announcement to enforcement. The EU Listing Act (Regulation 2024/2809) entered into force on 4 December 2024 with staggered application through June 2026; the Savings and Investments Union Communication COM(2025) 124 of 19 March 2025 set out the legislative spine across supplementary pensions, retail savings accounts, securitisation revival, supervisory convergence and the EU 28th regime; the Solvency II amending Directive 2025/2 of January 2025 contains a long-term equity treatment that Member States must transpose by 29 January 2027; the IORP II and PEPP reviews and an auto-enrolment Recommendation were proposed by the Commission in November 2025; the Council agreed its securitisation general approach in December 2025; and EU Inc. (COM(2026) 321 final) of March 2026 operationalises the Letta 28th regime. Which subset of the policy stack actually changes capital-flow architecture by 2027 rather than slipping to a 2030 horizon, and what that means for Revolut's IPO-destination window, sits at the centre of this cycle.

EU policy stack: in force, in train, in proposal

Five EU policy-stack measures by status: live (green), legislative process underway (orange and blue), proposed (red and purple). Compiled from EUR-Lex, Commission and Council documents 2024-26.

  • Regulation (EU) 2024/2809 (Listing Act) amends the Prospectus, MAR and MiFIR regimes to simplify EU public capital market access, with staggered application from 4 December 2024 to June 2026; ESMA's Listing Act implementation page sets out the Level 2/3 work programme. EUR-Lex (November 2024); ESMA (June 2026).
  • The Savings and Investments Union Communication COM(2025) 124 final sets out the Commission's strategy to channel EU savings into productive investment via supplementary pensions, retail savings accounts, securitisation revival, supervisory convergence and an EU 28th regime for innovative companies. European Commission (March 2025).
  • Directive (EU) 2025/2 recalibrates Solvency II to enable long-term equity allocations by insurers, including a dedicated treatment designed to unlock insurer capital for European firms and venture/growth equity; Member State transposition by 29 January 2027. EUR-Lex (January 2025).
  • The Commission's October 2025 announcement of a Solvency II delegated act and a Communication on bank equity investments is explicitly designed to free up insurer and bank balance-sheet capacity for European firms, private equity and venture capital. European Commission (DG FISMA) (October 2025).
  • The November 2025 supplementary pensions package proposes an IORP II review (lifting undue investment constraints), a PEPP review and a Recommendation on auto-enrolment, pension tracking and dashboards: the harmonisation lever absent from CMU 1.0. European Commission (DG FISMA) (November 2025).
  • The Council's general approach on securitisation agreed in December 2025 is one of the SIU's three concrete legislative deliverables and a key route to releasing bank balance-sheet capacity for European credit. Council of the European Union (December 2025).
  • EU Inc. (COM(2026) 321 final) proposes an optional EU-wide corporate form: founded in 48 hours for under €100, no minimum share capital, single-window filing, operationalising the Letta 28th regime recommendation as a fix for the fragmentation that drives scale-ups to the US. European Commission (March 2026).
  • The European Parliament Research Service tracks SIU progress against the March 2025 strategy: supervisory convergence proposals delivered Q4 2025; EuVECA review scheduled Q3 2026; shareholders' rights review Q4 2026. European Parliament Research Service (November 2025).

The case against this framing

The policy stack may be substantially defined on paper but the implementation lag is the binding variable, and the analysis risks treating the proposal stage as if it were the in-force stage. The IORP II review proposal of November 2025 has to clear Council and Parliament before any Member State transposition, plausibly a 2028 effective date at the earliest. The 28th regime has historically been the most politically sensitive Commission proposal (it touches national company law) and may not ship in this Commission's term. This counter would gain weight if Council Working Party progress on either IORP II or the 28th regime stalls inside H2 2026, or if Member State transposition of Solvency II 2025/2 is slower than scheduled across the major insurance jurisdictions.

3. The Mansion House experiment: UK pension consolidation as the live test case

Immediate

The UK has the only European deployment-side experiment running at scale. The Mansion House Compact of July 2023 committed nine UK DC providers to 5% of default-fund AUM in unlisted equities by 2030. The Mansion House Accord of May 2025 expanded that commitment to 17 providers covering roughly 90% of active DC savers, with 10% in private markets and 5% in UK assets by 2030, estimated to unlock up to £50 billion. The Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026) enacts the £25 billion megafund threshold for multi-employer DC schemes and the LGPS pool consolidation, legislating the value-for-money regime and the DC mandation power. The FCA's PS24/6 listing reforms (in force July 2024) sit on top as the listing-venue counterpart. Whether consolidated DC schemes and aligned LSEG reforms create the closed loop the EU is still building, or whether trustee fiduciary anxiety holds the allocation below the 5%/10% commitments in practice. The first-year ABI data point is directional: DC unlisted-equity investment doubled year-on-year to £1.6 billion, against a 2030 target consistent with £25-50 billion.

  • The original commitment: the Mansion House Compact of July 2023 committed nine (later eleven) UK DC providers to 5% allocation to unlisted equities by 2030, the prototype voluntary-allocation lever the EU is now considering replicating. HM Treasury / City of London Corporation (July 2023, anchor).
  • The 2025 expansion: the Mansion House Accord announcement of May 2025 brought 17 providers (~90% of active DC savers) to a 10% private-markets and 5% UK-asset commitment by 2030, estimated to unlock up to £50 billion of investment. HM Treasury (May 2025).
  • The legislative spine: Pension Schemes Act 2026 (Royal Assent 29 April 2026) enacts the £25 billion megafund threshold for multi-employer DC schemes, the LGPS pool consolidation (86 administering authorities into 6 pools), the value-for-money regime and the DC mandation power, operationalising Mansion House. UK Parliament (April 2026).
  • The listing-venue counterpart: FCA PS24/6 created a single ESCC commercial-companies category replacing the Premium/Standard split, established a disclosure-based regime, and permitted 10-year sunset dual-class share structures: the parallel UK lever to attract scale-up listings. Financial Conduct Authority (July 2024, anchor).
  • The Bank of England endorsement: the December 2025 Financial Stability Report records the FPC welcoming the Pension Schemes Bill's DC consolidation measures; UK DC schemes hold >80% in listed equities and bonds (vs <33% of UK assets) and are smaller and lower-risk than international peers, validating the productive-investment case for consolidation. Bank of England (December 2025).
  • The VfM mechanism: CP26/1: Value for Money Framework sets a joint FCA/DWP/TPR regime under which schemes must publish performance, costs and service-quality data, targeting 2028 for first assessments. The mechanism is designed to force consolidation by exposing poor-value sub-scale schemes. Financial Conduct Authority (January 2026).
  • The first-year delivery data: ABI's October 2025 update records DC default-fund investment in unlisted equities doubling year-on-year from £0.8 billion to £1.6 billion: directional progress towards the 2030 target, still far from the £25-50 billion implied by full compliance. Association of British Insurers (October 2025).

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal A flagship UK fintech IPO under the new FCA primary-market regime: would gain weight if Revolut, Monzo or a comparable scale-up announces a London primary listing inside H2 2026 and the LSEG IPO pipeline rebound the Bloomberg / Goldman / EY commentary projects converts into completed deals at scale.
  • Weak signal A DC reallocation event under the Pension Schemes Act 2026 megafund threshold: would gain weight if a Mansion House Accord cohort member announces a specific Revolut-class late-stage allocation, or if NEST, USS or another major scheme publishes a DC unlisted-equity book that includes a named European fintech.

The case against this framing

The Mansion House lever may be more political than fiduciary, and the allocation commitments may not survive a market correction or a change of UK government. Trustees retain fiduciary duty, and the Pension Schemes Act 2026 mandation power has not yet been exercised. The October 2025 ABI data of £1.6 billion in DC unlisted-equity AUM is small against the £25-50 billion required for full compliance, and the path to compliance assumes a five-year ramp that the value-for-money mechanism alone may not deliver. This counter would gain weight if the May 2026 Mansion House Accord progress update reports below £3 billion in DC unlisted-equity AUM, or if the FCA Value for Money assessments due in 2028 are delayed.

4. The listing-destination question: London, Amsterdam, Frankfurt or Nasdaq

Near-Term

The listing-destination decision is now a board-level strategic variable. Klarna chose NYSE in September 2025 at a $40 IPO price for a ~$15 billion valuation, closing 15% higher; Flutter shifted its primary listing to NYSE on 31 May 2024 with full LSE delisting set for August 2026; CRH completed its NYSE primary listing on 25 September 2023 and finalised LSE delisting in April 2026. The pattern is documented and the depth-of-demand premium at New York is real for tech and consumer-brand IPOs in the $10-20 billion size class. Against this, the UK has the new FCA Listing Rules in effect from 19 January 2026, the LSEG H1 2026 pipeline rebound that Goldman, Bloomberg and EY have flagged, and the Euronext European Common Prospectus launch of February 2026 that targets full Listing Act implementation by June 2026. Whether the UK and EU demand-side reforms close enough of the depth gap by 2027-28 to make a European primary listing viable for a $75 billion fintech, or whether dual-listing becomes the default European optionality structure.

  • The canonical 2025 European-to-NYSE move: Klarna's NYSE debut on 10 September 2025 priced at $40/share for a ~$15 billion valuation and closed 15% higher at $45.82 with implied market cap ~$19.65 billion: the Swedish BNPL leader chose NYSE over a European exchange. CNBC (September 2025, anchor).
  • The structural-anchor case: Flutter Entertainment shifted its primary listing to NYSE on 31 May 2024 following shareholder approval, with eventual full LSE delisting set for August 2026: emblematic of European champions migrating their primary capital base to the US. U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (May 2024, anchor).
  • The earlier anchor: CRH completed its primary listing transition to NYSE on 25 September 2023 citing ~75% of earnings from North America; LSE delisting was finalised in April 2026, removing the cement major from London entirely. CRH plc (September 2023, anchor).
  • The UK rebound case: Bloomberg's January 2026 piece records London's reformed listing regime facing its first market test after years of losing IPOs to New York, with the new UK Listing Rules in effect from 19 January 2026. Bloomberg (January 2026).
  • The Goldman call: Goldman Sachs forecasts double-digit European IPOs in 2026, with January 2026 deal volume already exceeding the entirety of Q1 2024; Anthony Gutman predicts UK listings will return as the reforms bite. Bloomberg (March 2026).
  • The Euronext play: Euronext's European Common Prospectus of February 2026 is framed by CEO Stephane Boujnah as a "race against time" to keep Europe competitive with US and Asian listing venues; full Listing Act implementation expected June 2026. Euronext (February 2026).
  • The market data: EY IPO Eye for Q4 (January 2026) records London IPO market rebound in Q4 2025, US capital markets raising $45.5 billion in IPO proceeds in 2025, and the UK preparing for a 2026 rebound as the new listing regime takes effect. EY (January 2026).
  • The market commentary: CityAM reports LSEG sees a strong IPO pipeline for H1 2026 after the new listing regime came into force on 19 January 2026, with momentum building across defence, financials and industrials. CityAM (January 2026).

The case against this framing

The dual-listing default may be the wrong frame for a $75 billion fintech: the UK FCA reforms have not yet been tested by a flagship tech IPO, and the LSEG pipeline rebound is sector-tilted to defence and industrials rather than financials. Klarna and Flutter may be the wrong precedents for Revolut, whose business model is European retail-banking and whose customer concentration is European; the optimal listing venue for a primarily-European customer-base fintech may differ from the optimal venue for a US-earnings cement major or a Swedish BNPL. This counter would gain weight if a comparable European fintech announced a London-primary IPO inside H2 2026 and the listing demonstrated a depth-premium narrower than the documented 30-40% New York uplift.

5. Sovereign wealth as the working substitute

Near-Term

Friend-shored sovereign-wealth and non-European institutional capital is the working substitute for the European deployment layer the policy stack is still building, but the substitution pattern is not what the policy debate assumes. The lead investors in the canonical late-stage European tech rounds (Revolut, Klarna, Helsing, Wayve, Mistral) are mostly US growth-equity, US public-market institutional and US-VC capital, not Gulf or Singapore sovereign wealth. Sovereign-wealth capital is the structural co-investor that enables those rounds at scale: GIC at $1.9 billion into Sunfire and Storegga was the largest sovereign-wealth deployment in 2024, Mubadala and ADQ have taken positions in the syndicate stack rather than the lead, and NBIM's CEO has gone on record explaining why the world's largest sovereign fund is shifting its equity book toward US Big Tech and away from European tech. The substitution is real, but it is US private-markets capital plus sovereign-wealth co-investment, not sovereign wealth alone. What this means for the late-stage equity terms available to a Revolut-class European fintech between now and an IPO window is the cycle question.

  • The Revolut canonical case: the November 2025 secondary share sale at a $75 billion valuation (up 67% from $45 billion) was led by Coatue, Greenoaks, Dragoneer and Fidelity, with NVIDIA's NVentures joining the cap table, Andreessen Horowitz, Franklin Templeton and T. Rowe Price participating: a fundraise structure that substituted for an IPO. Revolut (November 2025, anchor); TechCrunch (November 2025).
  • The 2024 SWF baseline: GIC deployed $1.9 billion into just two European startups (Sunfire, Storegga) in 2024, making it the largest sovereign-wealth fund investor into European tech that year; Canada Pension Plan followed at $1.6 billion; Temasek did five deals at $622 million. Sifted (December 2025).
  • The public-institution share: Atomico's State of European Tech 2025 records public institutions accounting for ~38% of European VC commitments in 2025 (vs 6-8x lower share in the US); Atomico proposes a "European Capital Compact" to channel pension, insurance and sovereign assets to European innovation. Atomico (November 2025).
  • The defence-tech case: Helsing's $1.2 billion raise at an $18 billion valuation, led by Dragoneer and Lightspeed in May 2026, follows the June 2025 €600m round at €12 billion led by Prima Materia; the company sits ~80% European-owned despite US lead investors. TechCrunch (May 2026).
  • The European-AI counter-example: Mistral AI's €1.7 billion Series C in September 2025 was led by ASML (€1.3 billion, ~11% stake), with participation from DST, a16z, Bpifrance, General Catalyst, Index, Lightspeed and NVIDIA: notably no Gulf sovereign capital at this round despite earlier talks. Mistral AI (September 2025, anchor).
  • The autonomous-vehicle case: Wayve's February 2026 $1.2 billion raise at $8.6 billion (potentially $1.5 billion with Uber's contingent commitment) is funded by SoftBank Vision Fund 2, Eclipse, Balderton, Ontario Teachers, Baillie Gifford, British Business Bank and Schroders: a textbook IPO-substitute round and a vector for sovereign and pension capital into European AI. TechCrunch (February 2026).
  • The NBIM verdict: NBIM CEO Nicolai Tangen says Europe's lag in tech and AI explains an "extraordinary shift" in NBIM's equity book toward US Big Tech; NBIM holds stakes in 2.3% of all listed European companies but is choosing US over European tech listings. CNBC (March 2026).

The case against this framing

The "working substitute" reading may overstate the sovereign-wealth role and understate the US-VC and US private-markets contribution that actually does most of the late-stage work in European tech rounds. The Revolut tender was led by Coatue, Greenoaks, Dragoneer and Fidelity; the Helsing round by Dragoneer and Lightspeed; the Wayve round by SoftBank, Eclipse and Balderton with Ontario Teachers and Schroders. Friend-shored sovereign capital is part of the picture but not the marginal backer in most named rounds. This counter would gain weight if a 2026 European late-stage round at the $1 billion+ scale closed with sovereign capital as the lead and US VCs as the syndicate, or if a NBIM, GIC or Mubadala direct allocation to European late-stage equity exceeded $5 billion in a calendar year.

Strategic Implications

Four strategic implications, each pointing to a decision Revolut's board cannot defer past the 2027 IPO-window opening. Each carries a decision posture (Decide / Prepare / Monitor) and a theme reference.

SI 1: Treat the European deployment-side reforms as an active engagement target, not a passive backdrop

Decide

The Mansion House Accord, the IORP II review, the Solvency II Directive 2025/2 transposition and the EU 28th regime are now legislative artefacts with deadlines that fall inside Revolut's IPO-window arithmetic. The CFO and Public Affairs functions should map which specific consultations, transposition exercises and Member State implementations have a credible path to allocating capital to a Revolut-class asset, and pick three to engage actively across the next 18 months. The default of passive observation produces a 2030 demand-side architecture; active engagement may produce a 2028 one. By Q4 2026 the board should review which of the three engaged tracks have materially advanced and which have not. Draws on Theme 1 and Theme 2.

SI 2: Run the listing-destination decision as a portfolio choice, not a binary

Prepare

The Klarna NYSE, Flutter NYSE-primary and CRH NYSE-primary precedents are documented and the Goldman / Bloomberg / EY commentary for H1 2026 is real on a UK rebound. The dual-listing default is one option; a UK-primary listing with secondary US ADRs is another; a Euronext / Amsterdam primary with parallel ADRs is a third. The Treasury function should run all three scenarios against the Atomico depth-premium evidence and against a counterfactual that the FCA / LSEG reforms deliver, and present the board with a quantified ranking by Q1 2027 ahead of any formal IPO announcement. The current path of least resistance is Nasdaq; the path that maximises European optionality may be different. Draws on Theme 4.

SI 3: Build an investor-relations programme aimed at the Mansion House Accord cohort

Prepare

Seventeen UK DC providers covering ~90% of active DC savers have committed to 10% private-markets and 5% UK-asset allocation by 2030. The May 2025 announcement, the Pension Schemes Act 2026 and the ABI's October 2025 progress data put the cohort on the record. A targeted IR programme aimed at the named providers (Aviva, Aegon, L&G, Phoenix, Royal London, NEST, Smart Pension, USS and the other Accord signatories) should be in place by Q3 2026, focused on the unlisted-equity allocation and the trustee fiduciary path to a Revolut-class asset. The mechanism for engagement exists; the named cohort is small; the question is whether Revolut chooses to use the lever. Draws on Theme 3.

SI 4: Disclose the sovereign-wealth composition of the cap table as a strategic asset, not a passive fact

Monitor

The November 2025 $75 billion tender drew US private-markets capital plus NVIDIA's NVentures; the cap table is light on European sovereign capital and material on US private-markets and US-VC capital. The board should decide whether the next primary round (or pre-IPO secondary) actively targets European pension, insurer or sovereign capital as a deliberate diversification, and whether the cap-table composition becomes a disclosed asset in the IPO prospectus or remains a private fact. Watch for an NBIM, GIC or Mubadala direct allocation announcement; watch for a UK DC scheme purchasing Revolut secondary; track the cap table evolution against the Atomico European Capital Compact framing. Draws on Theme 5.

What We Are Not Planning For

Three scenarios deliberately excluded from this cycle's planning envelope. Each is named with the indicator that would force a reinstatement.

A Brexit-style reversal of UK pension consolidation

This briefing assumes the UK Pension Schemes Act 2026 and the Mansion House Accord cohort hold. We are not planning for a policy reversal under a different UK government, a Treasury de-prioritisation of the productive-investment agenda, or a trustee revolt that re-fragments DC consolidation.

Reinstatement trigger: a UK General Election outcome producing a Labour-Treasury or Conservative-Treasury position that materially walks back the Mansion House framework; or a documented Court of Appeal decision constraining the DC mandation power on fiduciary-duty grounds.

A US capital-controls regime that closes the Nasdaq destination

This briefing assumes the US capital market remains open for European-domiciled tech and fintech IPOs at current trajectory. We are not planning for a US tariffs-style extension to capital markets that imposes inbound-listing restrictions, an SEC rulemaking that materially changes the foreign-private-issuer regime, or a Treasury action that blocks specific European fintech listings on national-security grounds.

Reinstatement trigger: a US SEC consultation on foreign-private-issuer eligibility constraints; or a documented Treasury or CFIUS position that materially affects European fintech access to US public markets.

A Eurozone sovereign-debt episode that reprices the SIU envelope

This briefing assumes the eurozone sovereign-debt market remains broadly stable and the SIU envelope remains the active policy track. We are not planning for a France, Italy or Belgium sovereign-debt episode that diverts Commission and ECB attention from the SIU implementation, or for an ECB monetary tightening cycle that materially raises the cost of capital across European late-stage equity.

Reinstatement trigger: a 10-year sovereign spread for France or Italy above 200 bp against Bunds for more than one quarter; or a documented ECB monetary policy pivot that materially affects euro-area equity-risk premia.
