Executive Synthesis

Why this matters now

The May cycle, "Fixed Costs, Falling Tide", read the Club's near-term squeeze: falling participation, an ageing model, a rising estate cost base. This edition lengthens the horizon to 2040 and finds three of those pressures hardening into the horizon and two new ones arriving. The ground under the estate is physically moving as the Solent rises and its saltmarsh buffer retreats; decarbonisation has already reached the fleet, with red diesel gone and the marina pulled toward energy provision; the estate grows harder and dearer to insure as Flood Re heads for its 2039 exit; and the membership base ages and thins beneath all of it. The through-line: to 2040 the estate, the fuel and the members all move at once.

Four shifts this cycle records, and the pattern that names them:

  • The estate is on moving ground. Sea-level rise and saltmarsh loss reshape the Hamble frontage to 2040, turning flood risk and defences into a dated capital question.
  • The zero-carbon wake reaches the fleet. Red diesel has ended for propulsion and the EU is steering a 90 percent cut by 2040, so fuel and charging become a Club decision, not a distant one.
  • The ground is becoming uninsurable at the edges. Flood Re exits in 2039, coastal erosion has no cover, and premiums and build costs climb together.
  • The membership thins and ages. Participation is in decline and discretionary spend is under pressure, so the 2040 funnel has to be rebuilt for a different demographic.

This cycle names the variable that will decide the Club's position in 2040: not whether these forces are real, the evidence settles that, but whether the Club adapts the estate, the energy offer and the membership funnel on a planned timetable, or lets a moving seascape set the timetable for it. Four developments carry the pattern.

First, the estate sits on moving ground. The Environment Agency's climate-change allowance puts sea level around the UK about 20cm higher than in 1901 and accelerating, and the Climate Change Committee projects nearly 7 million UK properties in flood-risk areas today, rising up to 40 percent by 2050. The saltmarsh that buffers the Hamble frontage has lost more than half its extent to erosion, with a dredged-mud restoration trial now under way in Lymington Harbour on the western Solent. Along comparable western-Solent shoreline the Agency projects properties at risk rising from 35 today to 360 within a century. For the Club this is a dated adaptation task, not a distant risk.

Second, the zero-carbon wake has reached the recreational fleet. Diesel used to propel private pleasure craft now attracts the full 52.95 pence-a-litre rate, some 46 pence a litre above the old rebated rate, and suppliers must collect a propulsion declaration. Europe's boating industry ties the sector to the EU's 90 percent emissions-cut target for 2040, and the European Boating Industry's 2026 roadmap sets a technology-neutral pathway for Europe's marinas. Charging is arriving, even as marinas are barred from profiting on resold electricity, so the marina is being pulled toward becoming an energy provider even as the fleet turns over only about 2 percent a year.

Third, the ground grows harder to insure at its edges. Flood Re is legislated to end in 2039, and it excludes commercial property; a Commons committee confirms there is no specific insurance for coastal erosion at all. UK property insurers paid a record 6.1 billion pounds in 2025 while average buildings cover eased to 306 pounds, down 6 percent, even as quarterly property claims hit a record 846 million pounds, while the EU insurance protection gap sits near 75 percent. The estate is still insurable today, but the direction of travel to 2039 and beyond is clear.

Fourth, the membership base thins and ages beneath all of it. The RYA's 2026 Club Membership Census flags ageing and retention pressures, British Marine finds 77 percent of adults did a watersport in 2025 but only 13 percent sailed, and four in five say they want to take part more than they do, and Deloitte records a second straight quarter of falling leisure spend. Activity falls sharply with age, with just 44 percent of over-75s active. The 2040 funnel cannot be the 2010 one.

The estate, the fuel and the membership are all moving at once, and only one of the three keeps its own timetable.

The historical rhyme worth holding is the Club's own estate history: the Hamble frontage, pontoons and slipways were built for a stable waterline and a growing, younger membership. To 2040 both assumptions weaken together, and the prudent posture is to treat estate adaptation, the energy transition and membership renewal as one connected capital and strategy programme rather than three separate committee items.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The most consequential assumption is that these forces compound to 2040 rather than resolving on their own. If national flood-defence investment reaches the Hamble frontage, if drop-in fuels such as HVO scale and spare the Club a charging build-out, and if the post-pandemic dip in participation reverses as the RYA's pathways mature, then the picture is a manageable set of upgrades rather than a wholesale re-set, and a lighter, staged response is right. The falsifiable tests: whether a funded shoreline scheme covers the Club's frontage before 2035; whether HVO reaches price parity and marina availability; and whether Club membership and the junior pipeline stabilise over two seasons. Signals to watch: a local coastal-strategy funding decision, an HVO parity milestone, and two seasons of membership and junior-intake data.

  3. Uninsurable ground, recorded. Flood Re exits in 2039 and erosion has no cover. The open variable is when risk-reflective pricing starts to bite the estate policy.

  5. Name the pattern: the deck is shifting. The estate, the fuel and the members move together to 2040, so the planning question shifts from whether to how fast and how funded.

1. The Moving Estate: sea-level rise and saltmarsh loss reshape the Hamble shoreline to 2040

Immediate

The ground under the estate is physically moving. The Environment Agency's climate-change allowance puts UK sea level about 20cm higher than in 1901 and accelerating, and the Climate Change Committee projects nearly 7 million UK properties in flood-risk areas today, with the number at risk rising up to 40 percent by 2050. The saltmarsh that buffers the Hamble frontage has lost more than half its extent to erosion, and on comparable western-Solent shoreline the Agency projects properties at risk rising from 35 today to 360 within a century. For the Club this is a dated adaptation task on a fixed frontage, not a distant risk.

The moving estate, in four numbers

The moving-estate signal in four numbers. Source: Met Office, University of Reading, Environment Agency, Climate Change Committee.

  • UK sea levels will be 20 to 45 cm higher around UK coasts by 2050; nearly 7 million UK properties sit in flood-risk areas today and the number at risk could rise by up to 40 percent by 2050, with annual flood damage rising toward 4.5 billion pounds. Climate Change Committee (20 May 2026).
  • Along the western Solent from Hurst Spit to Lymington, properties at risk are projected to rise from 35 today to 360 within 100 years without further adaptation. Environment Agency (9 June 2026).
  • Sea level around the UK has risen about 20.1 cm since 1901 and is accelerating, with two-thirds of that rise occurring over the last three decades. Met Office (14 July 2026).
  • A 2026 dredged-mud saltmarsh restoration trial in Lymington Harbour on the western Solent aims to offset losses, noting that over 50 percent of the Solent's saltmarsh has been lost to erosion. University of Reading (23 February 2026).

Counter-argument

A funded shoreline scheme could reach the Hamble frontage before the risk bites. The Agency's record flood-defence investment and the Hurst-to-Lymington strategy show national money is moving, and a scheme covering the Club's frontage would convert a capital liability into a maintained asset. The falsifiable test is whether a funded coastal-defence scheme covers the Club's frontage before 2035. Actors to watch: the Environment Agency, New Forest and Eastleigh coastal partnerships, and the Solent Forum.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal A single scheme could cover both saltmarsh restoration and hard defence at the frontage. Would gain weight if the Lymington mud trial scales to a funded Hamble programme.
  • Weak signal Managed realignment could be proposed for stretches of the Hamble shoreline. Would gain weight if the next SMP review reclassifies frontage policy from hold-the-line.

2. The Zero-Carbon Wake: decarbonisation mandates reach the recreational fleet and the marina

Near-Term

The zero-carbon wake has reached the recreational fleet. Diesel used to propel private pleasure craft now attracts the full 52.95 pence-a-litre rate, some 46 pence above the old rebated rate, and suppliers must collect a propulsion declaration. Europe's boating industry ties the sector to the EU's 90 percent emissions-cut target for 2040, and the EU Sustainable Transport Investment Plan now includes recreational craft for the first time. Charging is arriving on the Solent, so the marina is being pulled toward becoming an energy provider even as the fleet itself turns over only slowly.

The zero-carbon wake, in four numbers

The decarbonisation signal in four numbers. Source: HMRC, European Boating Industry, British Marine, Fortune Business Insights.

  • Diesel to propel private pleasure craft can no longer benefit from a reduced rate of duty and is charged at the full rate, and using red diesel for propulsion in Great Britain is unlawful absent a propulsion declaration. HM Revenue & Customs (7 July 2026).
  • The EBI roadmap ties Europe's roughly 6.5 million recreational vessels and 10,000 to 20,000 marinas to the EU's 90 percent GHG-reduction target for 2040, noting HVO can cut lifecycle CO2 up to 90 percent as a drop-in fuel. European Boating Industry (1 May 2026).
  • Fuel suppliers to private pleasure craft must collect a propulsion declaration and charge the white-diesel rate of 52.95 pence per litre, some 46.47 pence above the rebated red-diesel rate. British Marine (23 June 2026).
  • France's Nautic Forum, on 28 and 29 May 2026, frames electrification of propulsion, eco-design and composite recycling as priorities, asking what recreational boating looks like at the 2035 horizon. BoatIndustry.fr (16 May 2026).
  • The European Boating Industry's 2026 roadmap sets a technology-neutral energy transition for Europe's 6.5 million recreational vessels and marinas, with HVO a drop-in fuel cutting lifecycle CO2 up to 90 percent but constrained by cost and limited marina availability. Marine Industry News (1 June 2026).
  • UK marinas are barred from profiting on resold electricity, which British Marine argues stifles investment in shore power and electric-vessel charging even as marina electricity demand rises; Ofgem is reviewing the resale framework. Marina World (2 July 2026).
  • The global electric-boat market is projected to grow from about 8.9 billion dollars in 2026 to 24.94 billion by 2034, a 13.7 percent CAGR. Fortune Business Insights (13 July 2026).

Counter-argument

Drop-in fuels such as HVO could spare the Club a charging build-out. If HVO reaches price parity and marina availability, the fleet decarbonises through the existing bowser rather than a capital charging programme, and the Club's role stays that of a fuel reseller. The falsifiable test is whether HVO reaches price parity and reliable Solent supply before 2030. Actors to watch: the RYA, British Marine, and Solent marina fuel suppliers.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal The propulsion-fuel declaration regime could tighten into per-berth reporting. Would gain weight if HMRC extends record-keeping duties to marina berth-holders.
  • Weak signal A charging build-out could become a members' expectation, not an option. Would gain weight if a neighbouring Solent marina markets fast charging as a berth benefit.
Decision link: Strategic Implication 2.

3. Uninsurable Ground: coastal-flood insurance retreats as the estate's capital bill climbs to 2040

Near-Term

The ground grows harder and dearer to insure at its edges. Flood Re is legislated to end in 2039 and excludes commercial property, and a Commons committee confirms there is no specific insurance for coastal erosion at all. UK property insurers paid a record 6.1 billion pounds in 2025, and while average buildings cover has eased slightly of late, claims stay at record levels and building costs are forecast up 13.1 percent to 2031, so the estate's capital and insurance bill still climbs toward the 2039 transition. The estate is still insurable today, but the direction of travel to 2039 and beyond is a board-level financial question.

Uninsurable ground, in four numbers

The insurance-retreat signal in four numbers. Source: House of Commons Library, ABI, BCIS, EIOPA.

  • There is no specific insurance for coastal erosion because it is treated as a virtual certainty, and 10,100 properties are at risk from coastal erosion over the next 80 years with no compensation route. House of Commons EFRA Committee (20 March 2026).
  • Only about 25 percent of natural-catastrophe losses in the EU have been insured over recent decades, leaving a roughly 75 percent protection gap as climate risk rises. EIOPA (16 April 2026).
  • Flood Re is a time-limited scheme with a planned transition to risk-reflective pricing by 2039; its Levy 1 rose from 135 million to 160 million pounds for the year to March 2026, and the average quote for a home with a flood claim was about 1,316 pounds versus roughly 4,400 before the scheme. Flood Re Limited (30 June 2026).
  • A record 2.65 billion pounds was invested in flood and coastal erosion risk management to March 2026, within a 10.5 billion programme to 2036 intended to benefit 840,000 properties. Environment Agency (30 June 2026).
  • UK property insurers paid a record 6.1 billion pounds in claims in 2025, with domestic flood claims up 38 percent and the average flood payout to homeowners up 60 percent to 30,000 pounds. Association of British Insurers (17 February 2026).
  • The average buildings-only home insurance premium eased to 306 pounds in Q1 2026, down 6 percent year on year, even as insurers paid a record 846 million pounds in property claims in the quarter. Association of British Insurers (6 May 2026).
  • BCIS forecasts UK building costs rising 13.1 percent and tender prices 15.5 percent over the five years to mid-2031. BCIS (6 July 2026).
  • Flood Re has a fixed legislative exit in 2039 after which the market must move to risk-reflective pricing, with around eight million English properties projected to face some flood risk by 2050. Insurance Business UK (24 February 2026).
  • MDL Marinas is pursuing a multi-million-pound 2026/27 growth strategy across five marinas, following 6.35 million pounds of capital investment in the 2025/26 season, underlining the rising cost base of the marine estate. Marine Industry News (23 March 2026).

Counter-argument

Risk-reduction works could keep the estate insurable and affordable past 2039. Flood Re's own transition guidance ties affordability to faster risk reduction, so defences and property-level resilience at the frontage could hold premiums down even after the scheme ends. The falsifiable test is whether the Club's buildings and estate policy renews without a material exclusion or excess step-change before 2030. Actors to watch: the ABI, Flood Re, and the Club's insurer and broker.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal A risk-reflective repricing could arrive before 2039 as insurers pre-position. Would gain weight if a renewal adds a flood excess or coastal exclusion to the estate policy.
  • Weak signal Commercial-property flood cover could tighten faster than domestic. Would gain weight if a Solent marina reports a declined or loaded commercial renewal.

4. The Thinning Membership: ageing demographics and the discretionary squeeze on the club model

Longer-Range

The membership base ages and thins beneath all of it. The RYA's 2026 Club Membership Census flags ageing and retention pressures, and British Marine finds 77 percent of adults did a watersport but only 13 percent sailed, and Deloitte records a second straight quarter of falling leisure spend. Activity drops sharply with age, and the Club's model rests on an older, discretionary-spend membership. The pathways to renew the base exist, but to 2040 the funnel has to be rebuilt for a different demographic rather than assumed to refill itself.

The thinning membership, in four numbers

The membership signal in four numbers. Source: RYA, British Marine, Deloitte, Sport England.

  • In 2024-25 activity fell sharply with age, with just 44.3 percent of over-75s active versus higher rates among younger adults, underscoring the ageing-participation squeeze. Sport England (23 April 2026).
  • The RYA's 2026 Club Membership Census tracks club membership trends, age demographics, retention rates and why members leave, as a barometer of the health of UK clubs and the sport. Royal Yachting Association (11 May 2026).
  • The RYA Female Futures Group, linked to the Athena Pathway, targets a 50 percent increase in female coaching and management roles and more active female racers by 2028. Royal Yachting Association (11 June 2026).
  • The RYA British Sailing Youth Team fields about 75 young sailors across eight classes, running up to 40 programme days a year to feed the racing pathway. Royal Yachting Association (27 July 2026).
  • UK net spending on leisure fell for a second consecutive quarter to minus 10.6 percent in Q4 2025, with holidays cut sharpest as discretionary caution persists. Deloitte UK (13 February 2026).
  • British Marine's 2025 Watersports Participation Survey found 77 percent of UK adults did at least one watersport or coastal activity in 2025, with sailing participation at 13 percent and the highest participation, 82 percent, among 16-to-34-year-olds, while four in five said they would like to take part more often. All At Sea (24 July 2026).

Counter-argument

The post-pandemic dip could reverse as the RYA pathways mature. If the youth team, the Female Futures Group and adult-returner routes lift conversion, and the discretionary squeeze eases with the wider economy, the base stabilises and the members' model needs refreshing rather than rebuilding. The falsifiable test is whether Club membership and junior intake stabilise over two consecutive seasons. Actors to watch: the RYA, British Marine, and the Club's membership and sailing committees.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal The adult-returner segment could outgrow the junior pipeline as the main funnel. Would gain weight if returner conversion outpaces junior intake for two seasons.
  • Weak signal The discretionary squeeze could push members from ownership to shared or club-owned boats. Would gain weight if berth demand softens while sailing-school demand holds.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions this cycle puts to the Club, each with a posture, an owner and the themes it draws on.

SI 1: Commission a to-2040 estate adaptation and capital plan

The 20-45cm sea-level rise by 2050, over-half saltmarsh loss and a western-Solent risk rising from 35 to 360 properties make the Hamble frontage a dated capital task, not a maintenance line, and building costs are forecast up 13.1 percent to 2031. Plan pontoons, slipways, defences and property-level resilience against a 2040 waterline, phased and costed, rather than renewing to the old line. If a funded shoreline scheme reaches the frontage, the plan simply sequences around it.

Action: within this cycle, commission a to-2040 estate adaptation and capital plan with a phased cost profile and a defence-funding watch on the local coastal strategy.

Who gains: the Club that adapts the estate on a planned timetable, and the members who inherit a maintained frontage rather than an emergency bill.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 3. Owner: Rear Commodore (House & Estate) / General Committee.

SI 2: Stand up a marina charging and low-carbon fuel pathway

Red diesel for propulsion now carries the full 52.95 pence rate, the sector is tied to a 90 percent cut by 2040, and charging is arriving even as electricity-resale rules face Ofgem review. Set a staged path: secure low-carbon fuel such as HVO through the existing bowser now, and scope marina fast charging for the point when the fleet turns over. If HVO reaches parity, the charging build-out can wait; if it does not, the groundwork is already done.

Action: this cycle, adopt a low-carbon fuel and charging roadmap with a decision gate tied to HVO price parity and local charging demand.

Who gains: the Club that sequences the energy transition deliberately, and the members who avoid a rushed, dearer retrofit later.

Prepare Draws on Theme 2. Owner: Marina & Facilities lead / House Committee.

SI 3: Re-underwrite the estate insurance and reserves against the 2039 Flood Re exit

Flood Re exits in 2039 and excludes commercial property, coastal erosion has no specific cover, and UK claims hit a record 6.1 billion pounds. Review the estate policy for flood and coastal exposure now, model a risk-reflective premium path to 2039, and align the reserves and sinking-fund policy to it. If faster risk reduction holds premiums down, the reserve build is prudent headroom rather than a cost.

Action: within two cycles, obtain a forward view of the estate's flood and erosion insurability to 2039 and set a matching reserves and resilience-investment policy.

Who gains: the Treasurer who prices the 2039 transition early, and the members who are not surprised by a step-change in premium or excess.

Prepare Draws on Theme 3. Owner: Hon. Treasurer / Finance Committee.

SI 4: Rebuild the membership funnel for the 2040 demographic

The RYA's 2026 membership census flags ageing pressures, four in five say they want to sail more than they do, and leisure spend fell 10.6 percent. Tie the Club's junior, adult-returner and female pathways to the RYA's youth team and Female Futures Group, and track recruitment-to-retention as a board metric. If participation stabilises, the funnel is refreshed rather than rebuilt, but the work is the same either way.

Action: by the next cycle, publish a recruitment-to-retention funnel with named pathway owners and a season-on-season membership and junior-intake metric.

Who gains: the Club that builds its 2040 base deliberately, and the sailing programme that keeps its pipeline as the national one thins.

Monitor Draws on Theme 4. Owner: Vice Commodore / Membership & Sailing Committees.

The Adapted Club

The best pairing: the estate is adapted on a planned, funded timetable and the membership is renewed and broadened. Defences, charging and a re-underwritten policy are sequenced ahead of need, and a wider 2040 base of members, families and returners carries them. Change absorbed, not survived.

Indicators: a funded frontage scheme lands; HVO or charging in place before the fleet turns; two seasons of stable-to-growing membership and junior intake.

Full Club, Fragile Ground

The membership renews and broadens, but the estate is adapted only reactively. A vibrant, younger club sits on a frontage that floods and reprices unpredictably, so member energy and subscription income are steadily absorbed by emergency estate and insurance bills rather than the sailing offer.

Indicators: membership and pathways strengthen while capital planning lags; rising insurance excess; ad hoc defence spend after weather events.

A Fine Club, Ageing

The estate is adapted on a funded, proactive plan, but the membership ages and thins. A well-maintained, resilient frontage serves a shrinking base, so the fixed cost of a first-class estate falls on fewer, older members and the subscription-per-head arithmetic tightens toward 2040.

Indicators: the capital plan and defences advance on schedule; membership drifts older; junior and returner conversion stays flat.

The Stranded Estate

The worst pairing: the estate is adapted late and the membership thins. A moving waterline sets the Club's timetable, defences and pontoons are renewed reactively at record build costs, insurance reprices as Flood Re exits, and a shrinking, ageing base carries a rising fixed-cost estate. The Club spends more to run less.

Indicators: no funded frontage scheme by 2035; an estate renewal declined or heavily loaded; two seasons of falling membership and junior intake.

What We Are Not Planning For

Four scenarios held out of the plan because the evidence does not yet justify resourcing against them. Each carries the reinstatement trigger that would change that judgement.

Exclusion 1: Managed retreat or relocation of the Club estate

The Environment Agency holds the line on the Hamble frontage with record defence investment; the evidence points to adaptation in place, not abandonment, within the horizon. We are not planning for managed retreat or relocation of the estate to 2040.

Reinstatement trigger: an SMP review reclassifying the Club's frontage to no active intervention, or the loss of a primary defence without a funded replacement.

Exclusion 2: A collapse of the members'-club model

Participation is in decline but the base and its broad participation remain substantial, and renewal pathways are in train. We are not planning for a collapse of the members' model to 2040; we are planning to rebuild its funnel.

Reinstatement trigger: two consecutive years of double-digit membership loss alongside a failure of the junior and returner pipeline to convert.

Exclusion 3: A mandated all-electric fleet by 2040

The sector path is technology-neutral, with drop-in fuels and slow fleet turnover; no measure mandates all-electric recreational craft. We are not planning for a compulsory all-electric fleet by 2040, while treating charging demand as a live prepare-item.

Reinstatement trigger: a UK ban on new combustion leisure engines, or withdrawal of marina red-diesel and drop-in fuel supply.

Exclusion 4: The estate becoming uninsurable today

The estate is still insurable now, with Flood Re running to 2039; the retreat is a direction of travel, not a present fact. We are not planning for immediate uninsurability, while re-underwriting against the 2039 exit.

Reinstatement trigger: an estate renewal declined outright, a coastal-flood exclusion added, or the Flood Re exit brought forward before 2039.

Discussion Points

  2. For the House Committee and marina: do we secure low-carbon fuel and scope charging this cycle, or hold until HVO reaches price parity and the fleet turns over?
