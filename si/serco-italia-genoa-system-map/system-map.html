System Map · Port of Genoa Resilience

How Weather, Inland Links, Shipping Routes and New Technology Act on the Port of Genoa

22 October 2026 · Companion to the Decision Intelligence briefing of 22 October 2026, Preparing the Port of Genoa for Storms, Shocks and Supply-Chain Shifts · Horizon: to 2040 Built from the briefing’s 36 verified sources. Links drafted by analysis.

The Decision Intelligence briefing says what is changing for the Port of Genoa to 2040. This System Map shows why the pieces move together: which changes feed each other, which push back, and where the port’s operators and partners can change how the whole system behaves rather than one part of it.

Read it by following the arrows. A + means the two move in the same direction; a − means they move in opposite directions. Where the arrows close into a circle, they form a loop: an R loop reinforces itself, so change feeds on change, and a B loop balances, pushing back against change. 2. The four loops this quarter

Reinforcing loops run in both directions: the same circle that compounds decline would compound recovery. The status on each card is this cycle’s reading, and is what the monthly Change Tracker updates. Priming means the parts are in place but the circle has not yet closed at scale. Hover over a status for its meaning.

Priming

B1 Congestion brake

More cargo moving inland raises congestion at the gates and on rail, and persistent congestion pushes cargo to rival ports. It is priming: 2025 was a record year, but trains fell 14.2% and a main gate closed for two months in 2026, while the Gronda bypass is stalled.

Watch: full-year 2026 rail share; gate closures overlapping with rail works; any Gronda decision.

Priming

R1 Automation squeeze

Automation cuts the shifts offered to the dockworker pool, thinner crews struggle to cover peaks, and peak delays strengthen the case for more automation. It is priming: the workforce plan cuts CULMV shifts from 122,138 to 112,834, and PSA Pra’ already reports gaps at peaks.

Watch: CULMV’s authorisation renewal in 2027; the Pra’ automation talks.

Priming

R2 Grid-dependent yard

Electric equipment and shore power raise the port’s dependence on grid power; a power cut becomes an operating stop, stops become backlogs, and backlogs push more automation. It is priming: the substation is sized for up to 132 MW and Pra’ is going fully electric, but no outage has yet been documented.

Watch: the first ferry-berth shore-power connection (2028 at the earliest); backup-power contracts at the terminals.

Priming

B2 Seeing the risk

Stops and losses raise demand for evidence of exposure from insurers, lenders and regulators; that demand funds a live picture of risk, and a live picture cuts unplanned stops. It is priming: IRIDE has 31 satellites with ground-motion and coastal services, but the port’s digital model serves planning, not live operations.

Watch: a live risk pilot on the breakwater or quays; insurers asking for monitoring data.

What the map shows that the briefing alone cannot
  1. Most weak points sit on links the port does not own. Rail service, road and gate access and grid power all act on congestion and stops, and none of them is run by the Port Authority or the terminals. That is why the first leverage point is a shared register rather than a port project.
  2. Two reinforcing loops run through automation. R1 Automation squeeze and R2 Grid-dependent yard both pass through automated, electric terminals: automation can relieve peak shortages and still add a dependence on power and software that nobody owns end to end.
  3. The balancing loops are not yet working. B1 Congestion brake works by losing cargo, which is the outcome the port wants to avoid, and B2 Seeing the risk has its parts in place but no live picture joining them. Building the live picture is the cheapest way to add a brake that does not cost traffic.

3. Four leverage points

Each point is numbered on the map, and each ties to a Strategic Implication already in the briefing, so the map changes the reasoning behind the plan rather than adding a second plan.

  1. 1

    One dependency register

    Rail service and road access are where B1 turns. A shared register with owners and fallbacks keeps congestion from building into lost cargo.

    DecideBriefing Implication 1: a shared dependency register and a first joint outage exercise by 30 June 2027

  2. 2

    Common weather rules

    Operating stops are where weather enters the map. Common limits and shared sensors cut uneven stoppages and the backlogs that feed R2.

    PrepareBriefing Implication 2: a common weather-operations protocol and sensors before the 2027-28 storm season

  3. 3

    A live picture of risk

    The live picture of risk is where B2 closes. Feeding the model with satellite, forecast and sensor data turns evidence into fewer stops.

    PrepareBriefing Implication 3: a twelve-month live risk pilot by the end of 2027

  4. 4

    Watch transhipment and cover

    Transhipment calls decide how much cargo moves inland and so how much load B1 carries. Quarterly readings show which way it is moving.

    MonitorBriefing Implication 4: four readings to the management committee each quarter from January 2027

4. How the map explains the scenarios

The two axes of the briefing’s Scenario Matrix 2040 are the map’s two dominant uncertainties: how much inland load B1 Congestion brake must carry, that is whether Genoa stays a transhipment hub or becomes a gateway, and whether B2 Seeing the risk is built for the whole port or terminal by terminal. The Scenario Stress Test tracks which of the four is gaining momentum.

Risk run: by each terminal → as one system
One system · Hub

The Shared Harbour

B1 carries little inland load and B2 works port-wide: storms are managed in sequence and most cargo never leaves the quay.
One system · Gateway

The Corridor Port

B1 carries heavy load, but partners share the register and B2 reaches up the corridors, so failures are seen and routed round.
Each terminal · Hub

The Sea Wall

B1 is light and B2 is fragmented: each terminal copes alone until a compound storm and outage hits several at once.
Each terminal · Gateway

The Gridlocked Gateway

B1 carries heavy load and B2 is fragmented: congestion brakes the port by pushing cargo away.
Main role: transhipment hub → inland gateway

Read this way, the scenarios stop being four stories and become four states of the same system, each with early indicators already on the map.

6. How the map stays live

A map drawn once goes stale as fast as a port’s works calendar. Inside a continuous intelligence cycle, every format updates it:

FormatWhat it does to the map
Weekly Signal ScanAttaches new evidence to existing links, such as a gate closure or a gale, and proposes new links when something does not fit.
Monthly Change TrackerScores each loop as strengthening, stable or weakening, which is the status shown in section 2.
Quarterly Decision IntelligenceRedraws the map, re-weights the loops and tests the leverage points against the scenarios.
Action TriggersFire when a loop changes state: for example, a full-year fall in rail share would mark B1 turning.
