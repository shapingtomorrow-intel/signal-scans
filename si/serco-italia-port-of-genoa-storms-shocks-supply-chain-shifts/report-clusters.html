Why This Matters Now

The Port of Genoa is busier than ever, but what will decide whether it keeps working to 2040 sits mostly outside its gates: the railways, motorways, power lines, data links and people it depends on.

Several forces press on those links at once. Weather is turning more violent in short bursts, and the sea around Liguria is warmer than on record. Shipping through the Red Sea and the Gulf remains unsettled, and Europe’s carbon rules are moving ship-to-ship cargo to ports outside the EU, so more of Genoa’s cargo travels inland. The terminals are automating and electrifying, which makes them more dependent on power and software.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: treat the port and its outside links as one system, with one shared picture of where they could fail. Watch rail traffic, gate access and how the terminals respond in the next gale.

Executive Synthesis

Which plans on your desk this week assume the Port of Genoa’s risks stop at the quay?

The evidence in this briefing says the biggest ones start beyond it.

The Port of Genoa is setting records while the systems that keep it working fall behind. Genoa and Savona-Vado handled almost 3 million twenty-foot container units (TEU) in 2025, up 6.3%, yet trains fell 14.2% as national network works interrupted services (Western Ligurian Sea Port System Authority, February 2026, Italian). In the first half of 2026 transhipment, containers passing ship to ship, fell 21.3% while inland cargo grew (Port Authority, August 2026), so more business leans on rail, roads and gates. The motorway operator says it has no intention of starting the Gronda bypass (L’Espresso, October 2026, Italian).

Three questions stay open. Whether shipping returns through Suez: September transits were 21% up on a year earlier but 39% below their level before the Red Sea attacks (Lloyd’s List Intelligence, October 2026). Whether Brussels’ plan to bring more hubs outside the EU under its carbon price wins traffic back (European Commission, July 2026). And whether defences keep pace with the weather: the new breakwater’s design study raised extreme wave values off Genoa by about 16% (Breakwater design study, June 2021, Italian).

Looking ahead to 2040, the Mediterranean Experts on Climate and Environmental Change (MedECC) expect sea level likely to rise 0.15 to 0.33 m by mid-century and today’s 100-year extreme sea level to become 10 to 30% more frequent (Mediterranean Experts on Climate and Environmental Change, November 2024); SRM forecasts Mediterranean container traffic up 15% by 2030 (SRM, Intesa Sanpaolo, July 2026, Italian). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

This briefing assumes the limits on Genoa to 2040 lie in its outside links rather than at the quay. That is wrong if rail traffic climbs back above its 2024 level once the network works end and the Terzo Valico line to Milan opens, or if a storm damages the quays on the scale of October 2018 before the new breakwater is finished.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Draw up one register of the port’s outside dependencies, with an owner for each. No one holds the whole picture today.
  2. Agree common wind and heat rules across the terminals. In the March 2026 gale each terminal decided alone.
  3. Turn the port’s digital model into a live picture of risk. It is not yet fed by satellite, sensor or forecast data.
  4. Find out who is covered when the links fail. Few businesses hold the catastrophe cover the law requires.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Genoa's port is setting records while the links that keep it working fall behind

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The Port Authority and terminals; rail, road, grid and cable operators; Brussels and shipping lines · Room to act: Wide on seeing and managing risk; narrow on routes, rules and national works

For the port’s operators and partners, the test to 2040 is less whether the quays survive the next storm than whether the rail lines, roads, power, data links and people behind them keep working when it comes. The port has never moved more containers, yet each outside link is strained or late, and the shift from transhipment to inland cargo puts more weight on exactly those links. The weather adds stress in short, violent bursts. The tools to see the whole system now exist, from the Port Authority’s digital model to Italy’s new satellites, but they are not yet joined up.

Genoa and Savona-Vado: more containers, fewer trains and less transhipment, 2025 and first half of 2026

Change on the same period a year earlier. Sources: Western Ligurian Sea Port System Authority, February 2026 (full year 2025) and August 2026 (first half of 2026).

  • Physical exposure. Short, violent bursts and heat are the hazards growing fastest: 110 mm of rain fell in six hours above Genoa on 18 October 2026 (Mentelocale, October 2026, Italian), river flooding ranks as the city’s top climate risk (Euro-Mediterranean climate centre, March 2026), and Italy’s seas were 1.18°C above the 1991-2020 average in 2025 (Italy’s environment institute, July 2026, Italian), after three years of strong Mediterranean marine heatwaves (Copernicus, April 2026).
  • Critical dependencies. The outside links are strained or late: trains fell 14.2% in 2025 (Port Authority, February 2026, Italian); the Gronda bypass for a node carrying about 40,000 vehicles a day is stalled (L’Espresso, October 2026, Italian); and CULMV, the dockworker pool, loses shifts as terminals automate, from 122,138 to 112,834 (Shipping Italy, July 2026, Italian), while PSA Pra’ struggles to cover peaks (Shipping Italy, June 2026, Italian).
  • External disruption. The port is turning from a hub into a gateway: transhipment fell 21.3% in the first half of 2026 (Port Authority, August 2026), Middle East container trade fell 9% amid the Gulf crisis (Il Sole 24 Ore, October 2026), and relay traffic, ships swapping containers with no local cargo, halved at western Mediterranean EU ports between 2023 and 2026 while Tanger Med held (inforMARE, July 2026).
  • Technology and adaptation. Tools to see risk arrive faster than the arrangements to use them: the Port Authority’s digital model, from a survey of about 75,000 square metres, serves planning, not live operations (Port Authority, May 2026, Italian); Italy’s IRIDE constellation reached 31 satellites, with ground-motion and coastal services (European Space Agency, May 2026); and ransomware halted the Port of Ancona through its cloud logistics accounts, without touching a crane (Cybersecurity360, July 2026, Italian).

Weak signal to watch

  • Weak signal The March 2026 gale exposed a gap in who decides: the Port Authority said common operating procedures for all terminals were neither legally nor technically feasible, and the dockworkers’ union said it had asked for years for wind gauges on the ground to add to those on the cranes (Shipping Italy, March 2026, Italian). It gains weight if a further gale brings an accident or a stoppage at one terminal while its neighbour keeps working.

Outlook to 2040

Physical stress keeps rising: Mediterranean sea level is likely to be 0.15 to 0.33 m higher by mid-century, and conditions for the region’s ports are projected to significantly worsen (MedECC, November 2024). Investment needs keep growing, with a study for the energy industry putting Italian ports’ energy-transition need at €36bn by 2050 (Ports Europe, September 2026). The outside links lag: a smaller Gronda would take six to eight years to build, and the motorway concession runs to 2038 (L’Espresso, October 2026, Italian).

What it means: plan for the outside links, not the quays, to be the binding limit into the mid-2030s, and invest now in seeing them as one system.

Counter-argument

The official view rates this risk lower. Italy’s national strategy for critical entities rates natural risks to maritime transport as generally low, extreme weather as medium, and sees terrorist or hybrid attack as the main threat (Italian Government, April 2026, Italian); 61% of port authorities worldwide rate cyber attacks a high risk, ahead of climate (PortEconomics, April 2026). The Bank of Italy finds Ligurian container traffic up 3.9% in 2025 despite the works (Bank of Italy, June 2026, Italian), and climate models project lower wave heights in the north-western Mediterranean by 2100 (Frontiers in Marine Science, June 2026). Yet the same strategy flags high vulnerability through dependence on energy and telecommunications.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Map and manage the port’s outside dependencies as one system

The Port Authority should convene the rail, motorway, grid and cable operators and the dockworker pool to build one register of the links that can stop the port, each with an owner, a warning arrangement and a fallback. A record year with fewer trains and a closed gate is the case for it.

Action: the Port Authority publishes the register and runs a first joint outage exercise by 30 June 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1 On the System Map: leverage point 1, rail and road access, which stops the congestion brake (B1) turning cargo away.

Implication 2: Agree port-wide wind and heat rules, backed by shared ground sensors

Terminal operators and the Port Authority should agree common limits for stopping and restarting work in wind, heat and heavy rain, read from ground sensors every terminal sees. March showed each terminal deciding alone, and heat illness in ports is rising while regional heat rules cover mainly farms and building sites (Worklimate, July 2026, Italian).

Action: sign a common weather-operations protocol and commission the sensors before the 2027-28 storm season.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1 On the System Map: leverage point 2, operating stops, which cuts the backlogs that drive the grid-dependent yard loop (R2).

Implication 3: Turn the digital model into a live picture of risk

The Port Authority, with Earth-observation and data partners, should feed its digital model with satellite measurements of ground and coastal movement, weather forecasts and terminal sensors, starting with the breakwater, quays and access viaducts. Joining them up is cheaper than finding movement after a failure.

Action: start a twelve-month pilot of a live risk layer on the breakwater, quays and access routes by the end of 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 1 On the System Map: leverage point 3, the live picture of risk, which closes the seeing-the-risk loop (B2).

Implication 4: Watch where transhipment goes, and whether cover follows the risk

Port leadership, shipping lines and insurers should track four readings each quarter: Suez transits, relay traffic at hubs outside the EU, the port’s rail share and catastrophe-cover take-up among port businesses. They show whether Genoa heads back to a hub or on to a gateway.

Action: the Port Authority’s strategy team reports the four readings to its management committee quarterly from January 2027.

Monitor Draws on Theme 1 On the System Map: leverage point 4, transhipment calls, which set the inland load on the congestion brake (B1).

The Shared Harbour

↗ Shift from todayTranshipment, down 21.3% in the first half of 2026 (Port Authority, August 2026), recovers, and the terminals run storms and heat to one shared protocol.

Ships again call to swap containers, helped by a reopened Suez and carbon rules that no longer favour hubs outside the EU. The breakwater admits the largest ships, and a joint operations centre closes berths in sequence. Inland links matter less.

Early indicators: Suez transits back to their pre-attack level; more hubs outside the EU under the carbon price; a port-wide weather protocol.

The Corridor Port

↗ Shift from todayRail’s share of traffic, 13.2% in 2025 (Port Authority, February 2026, Italian), rises well above it as the Terzo Valico opens and works are planned with the port.

Genoa is mainly a gateway for northern Italy and the Alpine countries. Port, rail, motorway and grid operators share a dependency register and plan works together. Inland weather exposure grows, but failures are seen early and cargo is routed round them.

Early indicators: rail share rising two years running; a decision on a smaller Gronda; partners joining a shared register.

The Sea Wall

↗ Shift from todayTranshipment comes back, but the March 2026 pattern, some terminals stopping in a gale while others work, becomes the rule.

Hub volumes return behind the breakwater, but each terminal runs its own wind limits, sensors and backup power, and insurers price each separately. The system holds until a storm during a power or cyber outage hits several terminals at once.

Early indicators: transhipment growing again; no common protocol after the next gale; terminal-by-terminal backup-power contracts.

The Gridlocked Gateway

↗ Shift from todayInland cargo keeps growing while the Gronda stays unbuilt, and the city node carrying about 40,000 vehicles a day becomes the port’s limit.

Genoa wins gateway cargo but each partner plans alone. Gate closures, rail outages and storm seasons coincide, cargo diverts to rival ports in bad months, and uninsured losses fall on hauliers and small logistics firms.

Early indicators: rail share flat after the Terzo Valico opens; no Gronda decision; overlapping gate and rail closures.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes at each reading. At this first reading no future leads; the evidence leans towards the gateway and terminal-by-terminal side.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

Heat, not storms, starts to set the working hours at Genoa’s terminals

Window: 2031 to 2038 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: port resilience plans are built around storms and sea level, and leave heat to each employer.

Heat-and-work monitoring names ports among sectors with rising heat illness while regional rules target farming and construction (Worklimate, July 2026, Italian), and the Mediterranean hit a record 28.6°C on 14 August 2026 (MeteoGiornale, August 2026, Italian).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2027: Liguria’s summer heat ordinance names ports or logistics.
  • By September 2028: a Genoa terminal moves summer shifts to night because of heat.

What it touches today: shift patterns and equipment specifications for the automated Pra’ yard.

Weak signal

Insurers start pricing port cover on satellite ground-motion data rather than claims history

Window: 2030 to 2037 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: cover is priced on past claims, and the protection gap is expected to close through the legal obligation alone.

IRIDE satellites now feed public ground-motion and coastal services (European Space Agency, May 2026), while the insurance regulator reports a wide protection gap for natural catastrophes (insurance regulator, August 2026, Italian).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: IRIDE ground-motion services are offered to private infrastructure owners.
  • By June 2028: an Italian insurer prices cover for a port or coastal asset on satellite monitoring.

What it touches today: the monitoring written into the new breakwater’s maintenance plan.

Emerging

Transhipment returns to Genoa because the EU’s carbon rules catch the North African hubs

Window: 2029 to 2036 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: plans treat the loss of transhipment to hubs outside the EU as permanent.

The Commission proposes lowering the threshold for listing a neighbouring hub outside the EU from 65% to 50% (European Commission, July 2026), which would add six ports to the list on current data (inforMARE, July 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: Parliament and Council agree the lower threshold.
  • By June 2029: relay traffic falls at Tanger Med and East Port Said while western Mediterranean EU hubs recover.

What it touches today: whether yard capacity at Vado and Pra’ is sized for inland cargo only.

Static model, cover as today

What We Are Not Planning For

Shipping routes

A lasting closure of the Suez route

Suez traffic is recovering: September 2026 transits were 21% higher than a year earlier (Lloyd’s List Intelligence, October 2026). A closure lasting years would strengthen the gateway reading rather than overturn it.

↺ Reinstated if monthly Suez transits fall below their year-earlier level for two quarters.

Sea level

Sea-level rise overtopping the quays before 2040

The likely mid-century rise is 0.15 to 0.33 m (MedECC, November 2024), and the breakwater’s design study assessed future waves and sea level (Breakwater design study, June 2021, Italian). Before 2040 the damage comes from storms and rain riding on that rise.

↺ Reinstated if the Genoa tide gauge shows a rise faster than MedECC’s likely range.

Labour

A long, port-wide strike over automation

The Pra’ agreement commits to keeping jobs (Port Authority, February 2026, Italian), and the union and labour pool are preparing to negotiate, not block (Shipping Italy, April 2026, Italian). Disputes are likely; weeks of port-wide stoppage are not.

↺ Reinstated if CULMV’s authorisation is not renewed in 2027.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle’s evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If rail, the motorway node and the power supply failed in the same storm, which would we restore first, and who would decide?
  2. Should the decision to stop work in a gale stay with each terminal, now that neighbours stopped and carried on at different times in March?
  3. If transhipment does not come back, which investments sized for a hub port should we scale down or put to other uses?
  4. What would a live picture of risk have to show, and how reliably, before we let it decide when berths close?
  5. Which of our current activities looks least wise in these futures?
