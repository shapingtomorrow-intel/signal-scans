Why this matters now

The career ladder is coming apart in three places at once: the rung people used to start on, the climb that used to pay, and the idea that a job title tells you what someone earns.

Three responses are underway, none coordinated with the others. Employers are quietly redesigning junior work rather than deleting it. Regulators are writing rules for who is accountable when software makes an employment decision, then postponing the deadline while older duties keep running. And organisations are finding the only route to progression still open, moving their own people sideways, needs a training capacity most never built.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: if people can no longer be paid more for leaving, every promise about progression has to be kept inside the organisation. The warning that you cannot keep it is quiet: watch how many people move sideways this year, and how many sit in the same seat for a third.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week still assume that a good performer can get paid more by leaving?

The evidence in this report says the exit has closed and nobody has rebuilt the inside.

Three separate mechanisms that used to make a career legible have stopped working at the same time, and each shows up in a different dataset that was not built to talk to the others. The bottom rung: payroll microdata through June 2026 puts employment of young workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less-exposed peers (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026), while the same authors state: "We find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement.". The climb: in June "the number and rate of quits were unchanged at 3.2 million and 2.0 percent, respectively" (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, August 2026), and the Cleveland Fed reads it as follows: "The recent confluence of low hire rates, low quit rates, and low layoff rates is likely a result of a long-term downtrend in labor market fluidity in general" (Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, August 2026). The rung itself: the premium for working with AI is being paid at the hiring gate and not to the people already there, with 66% of employers offering salary premiums for AI-skilled roles while 54% of employees whose work involves AI report pay flat or falling (the Indeed AI at Work Tracker, reported by CIOL, July 2026).

What has materially changed since the June cycle is not the volume of evidence but the quality of the counter-evidence, and it is now strong enough to change the shape of the decision rather than to caveat it. The New York Fed concludes: "We find that, while overall hiring has slowed since then, the evidence from job postings provides little indication of a distinct AI-driven decline in labor demand." It reports that the divergence "began before 2022 and does not show a clear additional break in trajectory after 2022" (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, May 2026). A randomised experiment finds AI increases performance for all participants, with substantially larger gains for lower-education individuals, closing an education-based gap from 0.548 to 0.139 standard deviations (National Bureau of Economic Research, May 2026). Brookings reports a competing reading: "They argue that the timing of the decline in job postings corresponds better to the macroeconomic shift of rising interest rates than to the launch of LLMs." (Brookings Institution, March 2026). Taken together, the sources suggest the mechanism most organisations have assumed, AI removing junior work, is the one the evidence supports least; what it supports well is that firms have stopped hiring into the junior tier and stopped reallocating anybody.

An organisation that removes the bottom rung and cannot move people sideways has not made itself leaner, only static.

The reading this briefing takes is that the two failures compound, and that is the part leadership teams are least likely to have priced. If the external market no longer pays people to move, the internal labour market has to carry progression instead, and the capacity to run one is not there. "Using this metric, the adult participation rate in education and training for the EU-27 in 2022 was 39.5%" (Cedefop, March 2026), and one in four mid-career professionals plateaus around the ten-year mark (NYU School of Professional Studies and the Burning Glass Institute, June 2026). An organisation that removes the bottom rung and cannot move people sideways has not made itself leaner, only static.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The most consequential assumption in this synthesis is that the collapse in mobility is structural rather than cyclical. If it is cyclical, the internal-labour-market argument is a two-year workaround rather than a redesign, and the correct posture is patience rather than investment. The disconfirming signal is already in the data: "For those not changing jobs, the Tracker edged up to 3.4 percent in June from 3.3 percent in May, while the Tracker for those changing jobs increased to 4.1 percent from 3.7 percent in May" (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, July 2026). If the switcher premium widens for three consecutive quarters and the quits rate follows it upward, this briefing's reading is wrong and the money belongs elsewhere.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Decide whether AI fluency is a pay-bearing criterion, and write it down if it is. Comparable work has to be assessed on objective, gender-neutral criteria, and a premium that exists in payroll and nowhere in the job architecture is precisely what that machinery is built to surface.
  2. Set the 2027 junior intake on the redesign case, not the displacement case. Employers themselves expect to reshape graduate and apprentice roles rather than remove them, and in a market where nobody is quitting, experience cut now cannot be bought back later at the price it saved.
  3. Spend the deferred AI Act window on the duties that did not move. The employment-related compliance date shifted to December 2027; the prohibition on solely automated decisions, the worker-data rights and the works-council consultation duties continue to run throughout.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Audience Snapshots

Four lenses on the same evidence base, one per audience. Each card carries the one question this cycle puts to that reader, and introduces no evidence the themes below do not already defend.

Board and CEO

If nobody can leave, what is your retention number actually telling you?

The shift: Quits were unchanged at 3.2 million and 2.0 percent in June 2026, so a strong retention figure is now at least as likely to reflect a frozen external market as a satisfied workforce.

The question to brief: Which of our retention, engagement and attrition targets would still read as a success if the external market reopened tomorrow?

People, Talent and HR leadership

Are you already paying an AI premium you have never written into a job description?

The shift: 66% of employers say they offer salary premiums for AI-skilled roles while 54% of employees whose work involves AI report pay flat or falling, so the premium is being paid at the gate rather than inside the structure.

The question to brief: If two people on the same grade asked us in writing to justify the difference between them, which objective criterion would we name?

Investors and capital allocators

Which of your holdings has removed its junior intake without saying so?

The shift: Employers expect redesign rather than removal, with 87 per cent expecting AI to reshape graduate and apprentice roles and 42 per cent expecting only 1 to 10 per cent of them to be affected.

The question to brief: Where a company reports lower headcount and flat output, can it show whether the saving came from automation or from a hiring pause it will have to reverse?

Government, regulator and trade body

Who supervises an employment decision that no person made?

The shift: The compliance deadline for employment-related high-risk AI moved from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, while duties under data protection, works-council law and the platform-work regime continue to run.

The question to brief: During the deferral window, which authority in your jurisdiction is actually receiving and acting on complaints about automated employment decisions?

Themes

Five themes, ordered by impact tier. The first three name one break each in the same ladder; the last two name the machinery being built around it. Immediate: changes the FY27 plan, the organisational structure or the proposition. Near-Term: changes competitive position over the next twelve months. Longer-Range: a multi-year factor to track and revisit each cycle.

1. The bottom rung is being redesigned, not deleted

Immediate

Your 2029 mid-level bench is being settled by hiring decisions taken this year, and the evidence base does not support the reason most organisations are giving for those decisions. The strongest measured facts are relative rather than absolute: young workers in AI-exposed occupations have fallen behind a control group, not fallen in level. The strongest disconfirming evidence comes from a central bank and a randomised trial, and both cut at the displacement mechanism specifically rather than at the observation. What employers themselves report is the most decision-relevant finding of the three, and it points at redesign. A firm that suspends graduate intake on the displacement headline will have acted on the part of this evidence base that stands up least well.

Sources: Stanford Digital Economy Lab (August 2026), Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas (February 2026), Federal Reserve Bank of New York (May 2026), Brookings Institution (March 2026) and Institute of Student Employers research reported by The Global Recruiter (May 2026).

  • Young workers aged 22 to 25 in AI-exposed occupations now stand 19% below where they would have been had they kept pace with less-exposed peers, on payroll microdata through June 2026, and the same authors report finding no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026).
  • The postings evidence contradicts the displacement timing rather than the observation: the divergence "began before 2022 and does not show a clear additional break in trajectory after 2022", and "the gap in labor demand between high- and low-exposure jobs stabilizes after 2023, at odds with AI gradually displacing exposed occupations" (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, May 2026).
  • More broadly, employment has declined 1 percent since late 2022 in the 10 percent of sectors most exposed to AI, on the Felten, Raj and Seamans exposure index, while wages in computer systems design rose: a split that pure demand weakness would not produce (Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas, February 2026).
  • Employers expect the rung to change shape rather than disappear: 87 per cent of employers expect AI adoption to reshape graduate and apprentice roles, while 42 per cent believe only a small number (1 to 10 per cent) will be affected (Institute of Student Employers research, reported by The Global Recruiter, May 2026).

Counter-argument

The relative decline is equally compatible with a labour market that has simply stopped hiring anybody. Brookings notes that results can be sensitive to which AI measure is chosen, and reports the competing finding that the timing of the decline in job postings corresponds better to the macroeconomic shift of rising interest rates than to the launch of large language models (Brookings Institution, March 2026). The European aggregate offers no confirmation: in June 2026, the youth unemployment rate was 15.5% in the EU, up from 15.4% in the previous month, and 14.8% in the euro area, down from 14.9% in May 2026 (Eurostat, July 2026). The United Kingdom is the exception rather than the pattern: there were an estimated 1,012,000 young people aged 16 to 24 years in the UK who were NEET in January to March 2026; this number increased by 89,000 when compared with January to March 2025, and that bulletin makes no attribution to AI (Office for National Statistics, May 2026).

2. Mobility stopped paying, and the inside is not ready to take the load

Immediate

The route through which your people have historically captured wage growth has closed, and the substitute you are assumed to have does not exist at the scale required. Quits and hires are both flat, and a central bank reads that as a long-run decline in fluidity rather than a rate-cycle effect, which removes waiting it out as a strategy. The counter-evidence is real and arrived in the month this briefing closed. Even so, the burden the freeze shifts onto internal redeployment lands on a training system that most of Europe's adults do not participate in, and on a mid-career population where one in four has already stopped moving.

  • The reallocation channel has stopped rather than contracted: in June, the number and rate of quits were unchanged at 3.2 million and 2.0 percent, respectively, and the number and rate of job openings were little changed at 7.4 million and 4.4 percent, respectively, in June (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, August 2026).
  • The freeze is being read as durable by the people who measure it: the recent confluence of low hire rates, low quit rates, and low layoff rates is likely a result of a long-term downtrend in labor market fluidity in general (Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, August 2026).
  • Payroll-matched data shows the premium narrowing from the switcher side rather than because stayers are bid up. For job-changers: "For this group, annualized gains slowed to 6.4 percent in January from 6.6 percent in December." January's increase tied with November 2025 as the slowest pace of pay growth for job-changers since February 2021 (ADP Research, February 2026).
  • The internal substitute is thin on both capacity and outcome: adult participation in education and training for the EU-27 was 39.5% (Cedefop, March 2026), and at the 10-year mark, those who ultimately stall out have already fallen behind, averaging just 1.5 promotions and 45% wage growth, compared to 1.9 promotions and 66% growth for peers who avoid stall (NYU School of Professional Studies and the Burning Glass Institute, June 2026).

Counter-argument

The freeze may already be thawing, and the disconfirming number is the most recent one in this briefing. For those not changing jobs, the Tracker edged up to 3.4 percent in June from 3.3 percent in May, while the Tracker for those changing jobs increased to 4.1 percent from 3.7 percent in May (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, July 2026), which is the signature of a switcher premium re-widening rather than closing. European data cannot arbitrate the question, because Eurostat publishes transitions into unemployment and inactivity rather than a job-to-job switching rate: of all those in employment in the fourth quarter of 2025, 2.5 million (1.2%) became unemployed in the first quarter of 2026 (Eurostat, June 2026). Germany's version of the same freeze is demographic rather than behavioural: "Im Jahresdurchschnitt 2026 sinkt die Zahl der Erwerbstätigen um 90.000", a projected fall of 90,000 in employment as an annual average, written in German thousands notation (IAB, the research institute of the German Federal Employment Agency, March 2026).

3. Pay moved inside the job title

Immediate

Two people on the same grade, holding the same job description, are beginning to be paid differently, and in most organisations nobody has written down why. The premium is measurable but its location is contested, and the employer-side evidence puts it at the hiring gate rather than inside the incumbent structure. That distinction decides whether you are running a temporary recruitment tactic or have quietly changed your pay structure. Either way the equal-pay machinery now requires the criterion to be named and defended, and the transposition lag across most member states is a window rather than a reprieve.

  • The premium is being paid at the gate and not to incumbents: 66% of employers said they offer salary premiums for AI-skilled roles, while more than half (54%) of employees whose work involves AI said their salaries have either remained the same or declined over the past year (the Indeed AI at Work Tracker, reported by CIOL, July 2026).
  • Advertised premiums are large and rising, though not measured with the job title held constant: the average wage premium for workers with AI skills hit 62%, up from 57% the previous year (PwC Global AI Jobs Barometer, June 2026).
  • Adoption is broad but shallow, which bounds how large a genuine within-role output gap can currently be: 43.0% of U.S. workers reported using AI for their jobs in January to February 2026, while 5.2% of all U.S. work hours were spent using AI (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, March 2026).
  • In France the divide is visible inside a single occupational status: among cadres, "la moitié d'entre eux l'utilisent désormais au moins une fois par semaine", and more than half (53 %) of large firms plan to give more weight to AI skills in recruitment (Apec, May 2026). The national statistical office puts the mechanism plainly: "son utilisation reste restreinte à une population déjà à l'aise avec les outils numériques, ce qui contribue à augmenter les inégalités", that is, use stays restricted to a population already comfortable with digital tools, which contributes to increasing inequality (INSEE, February 2026).

Counter-argument

The premium may be a scarcity rent that closes rather than a productivity fact that persists. In a randomised experiment, AI increases performance for all participants, with substantially larger gains for lower-education individuals: in the control group higher-education participants outperform lower-education participants by 0.548 standard deviations, and with AI that gap falls to 0.139 (National Bureau of Economic Research, May 2026). An employer who hard-codes an AI-fluency premium into the job architecture may be defending a differential the technology is actively eroding. The legal exposure runs the same way in either case, because the assessment of comparable work is to be based on criteria including skills, effort, responsibility and working conditions, applied objectively and gender-neutrally (Directive (EU) 2023/970, May 2023). As of June 2026, "only Slovakia, Malta, Lithuania (on a phased basis) and Italy have implemented the full directive on time" (Baker McKenzie, June 2026).

Decision link: Strategic Implication 1.

4. Rules deferred, duties not

Near-Term

The extension your legal team reported is real and considerably narrower than it sounds. The compliance date for employment-related high-risk AI moved by roughly sixteen months, but the duties that already govern an automated employment decision were untouched, and one of them prohibits the decision in principle. In Germany the practical gate on deployment is the works agreement rather than the AI Act clock; in Italy an employer has already been sanctioned this summer over worker monitoring. Organisations that read the deferral as relief will spend the window building systems they cannot lawfully switch on.

  • The deferral is law rather than proposal: Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of the European Parliament and of the Council of 8 July 2026 amends the AI Act (Official Journal of the European Union, July 2026), and the compliance deadline for these employment-related systems would move from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027 while recruitment, candidate selection, performance evaluation, task allocation, monitoring of workers and decisions on promotion or termination stay classified as high-risk (DLA Piper, June 2026).
  • The prohibition that already binds did not move: the data subject shall have the right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning him or her or similarly significantly affects him or her (Article 22, Regulation (EU) 2016/679, April 2016).
  • Germany's federal government states the position formally: "Bei der Einführung von Algorithmischem Management sind zudem die Unterrichtungspflichten des Arbeitgebers gegenüber dem Betriebsrat nach dem BetrVG zu beachten." The employer's information duties towards the works council apply on introduction, alongside data subject rights under the GDPR (the Federal Government's answer to the Bundestag, April 2026).
  • Enforcement is proceeding on the older duties. A decision of 18 June 2026 held that "la conservazione della posta elettronica deve essere strettamente legata alla possibilità di accesso solo da parte dell'intestatario": retention of corporate email must be strictly tied to the possibility of access by the account holder alone (Garante per la protezione dei dati personali, June 2026).

Counter-argument

A reasonable employer could read the direction of travel as deregulatory and plan on that basis. There is still no dedicated EU instrument on algorithmic management: the Commission has folded workplace AI into the broader Quality Jobs Act and on 20 July 2026 opened only the second-phase social-partner consultation, a pre-legislative step. Its own record of the position is warm rather than urgent: "In its reply of 10 March 2026, the Commission welcomed this resolution and its aim to improve working conditions and to promote the transparent, fair, accountable and safe deployment and use of automated monitoring and decision-making systems in the workplace." (European Commission, DG Employment, July 2026) The one instrument that does regulate it head on binds digital labour platforms rather than employers generally, and it binds them through their member state: "Member States shall require digital labour platforms to inform persons performing platform work, platform workers' representatives and, upon request, national competent authorities, of the use of automated monitoring systems or automated decision-making systems." (Directive (EU) 2024/2831, October 2024) The answer to that reading is who fills the gap: Germany's largest industrial union puts it as a condition rather than a preference: "Betriebs- und Personalräte müssen frühzeitig und verbindlich bei KI beteiligt werden", works and staff councils must be involved early and bindingly on AI. That relocates the negotiation to the plant rather than removing it (IG Metall Baden-Wurttemberg, July 2026).

5. Agents reach the system of record before they reach the workforce plan

Longer-Range

The machinery for holding an organisation accountable for what an agent did is being assembled now. The machinery for counting agents alongside people is not being assembled at all. Statute already requires a named human owner and a retained log for high-risk employment systems, standards bodies are writing the identity and delegation semantics that would make an agent enrollable, and the regulator finds acting systems already inside the hiring funnel. On the available evidence, governance is running ahead of accounting, and for most organisations the first meeting between the workforce plan and an agent will happen when somebody asks who owned its decision.

  • Statute already requires a named owner and a retained trail: deployers shall assign human oversight to natural persons who have the necessary competence, training and authority, as well as the necessary support, and shall keep the automatically generated logs for a period of at least six months (Article 26, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, June 2024).
  • Colorado turns agent activity into a documentary artefact: both developers and deployers are required to retain records necessary to demonstrate compliance with the act for at least 3 years (Colorado SB26-189, May 2026).
  • The regulator is already supervising automated decision-making inside the hiring funnel, reporting engagement with employers using it in recruitment and identifying a need for consistency in human involvement where automation is applied at the early stages (Information Commissioner's Office, March 2026). It does not find that employers are deciding without a human, and this briefing does not claim it.
  • Policy is treating the system as occupying a management function rather than as a licensed tool. On the Commission's definition, "AM involves the automation of one or several functions of workforce management covering the entire HR cycle from recruitment to termination of employment relationships, as well as the operational management of workers." (the Commission staff working document on algorithmic management, July 2026)

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signal Agent identity becomes an infrastructure question. NIST conducts fundamental research into agent authentication and identity infrastructure to enable secure human-agent and multi-agent interactions (the NIST AI Agent Standards Initiative, April 2026). Gains weight if an agent identity specification reaches draft publication and HR system vendors commit to it.
  • Weak signal Delegation semantics are being written for systems that act rather than draft: "AI agents are software systems that can act autonomously, such as browsing the web, executing transactions and calling other services, on behalf of users or organisations." (the OpenID Foundation, March 2026) Gains weight if delegation from a named employee to a named agent becomes a supported primitive in enterprise identity providers.
  • Weak signal The absence is the signal. No source in this cycle's evidence base enumerates agents alongside employees in a workforce plan, a headcount report or a disclosure. Gains weight the moment one listed company reports an agent count in a human-capital disclosure.

Counter-argument

The constraint may hold agents out of the workforce plan for longer than this theme implies. Survey work on AI trust in the agentic era reports control and governance worries, rather than capability, as what holds scaled agent deployment back (McKinsey & Company, March 2026), which is consistent with deployments still being narrow enough that a separate register would be premature furniture. The regulator's own posture supports patience over urgency: "We have engaged, on a voluntary basis, with organisations in the private and public sectors who may be using ADM as part of their recruitment processes" (Information Commissioner's Office, March 2026), which is supervision by conversation rather than by enforcement. Neither point is quantified here, because neither source's figures could be verified against its live page.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle into the FY27 plan. Each names the move, the owner, a date and a decision posture. Two are decisions rather than preparations because the evidence behind them will not improve before they have to be taken.

SI 1: Name the AI-fluency premium in the job architecture, or stop paying it

The CHRO, with the general counsel and the reward function, should settle before the end of October 2026 whether AI fluency is a pay-bearing criterion, because the alternative is a differential that exists in payroll and nowhere in the documentation. The evidence says the premium currently sits at the hiring gate rather than in the incumbent structure, which is what makes the decision cheap today: writing the criterion down costs a job-architecture revision, whereas discovering it during a pay-differential justification costs the defence. Comparable work must be assessed on objective, gender-neutral criteria (Directive (EU) 2023/970, May 2023), and the transposition lag in most member states (Baker McKenzie, June 2026) extends the window without softening the duty.

Action: By 31 October 2026, the CHRO and general counsel jointly decide whether AI fluency is a named objective criterion in the job architecture. If it is, define it, publish it in the grade descriptors, and test the resulting pay distribution for correlation with sex before the first reporting cycle under the Directive.

Decide Draws on Theme 3.

SI 2: Set the 2027 junior intake against the supervision work agents create

The CEO and CHRO should settle the 2027 graduate and apprentice number on the redesign case rather than the displacement case, because displacement is the part of the evidence base the disconfirming sources attack most directly. Employers themselves expect reshaping rather than removal (Institute of Student Employers research, May 2026), and an acting system now requires oversight by named natural persons with the competence, training and authority to exercise it (Article 26 of the AI Act, June 2024), which describes a job rather than a headcount saving. In a market where quits are unchanged at 3.2 million and 2.0 percent (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, August 2026), an organisation that pauses junior intake cannot buy the experience back later at the price it saved.

Action: Before the 2027 intake decision closes, the CEO and CHRO set the graduate and apprentice number against a named list of the supervision, review and exception-handling tasks that automated systems create, and record in the same paper the assumption that would justify cutting it instead.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 5.

SI 3: Fund internal mobility as an operating capacity with a throughput target

The CHRO and CFO should treat internal mobility as a funded capacity rather than a policy during FY27 planning, because the external channel through which people captured progression has closed and the internal substitute is thin on both capacity and outcome (Cedefop, March 2026). The constraint is not which taxonomy the organisation picks but how many people it can actually move and retrain in a year. Prepare rather than decide is the honest posture here, because the switcher premium widened in the month this briefing closed (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, July 2026) and a single month is not a trend.

Action: In FY27 planning, the CHRO and CFO set a lateral-move rate and a retraining-throughput target for the year with a named budget line, and report both to the board at the first 2027 review alongside the quits rate in the organisation's main labour markets.

Prepare Draws on Theme 2.

SI 4: Spend the deferral window on the duties that did not move

The general counsel, CHRO and CIO should use the period to December 2027 to close the duties already in force rather than to pause AI Act work, because the date that moved covers conformity obligations while the prohibition on solely automated decisions (Article 22 GDPR, April 2016), the worker-data rights and the consultation duties continue to apply throughout (the Federal Government's answer to the Bundestag, April 2026). In Germany the works agreement is the practical gate on deployment (IG Metall Baden-Wurttemberg, July 2026); in Italy monitoring enforcement produced a decision this summer (the Garante, June 2026). An organisation that builds an employment-decision system during the window and cannot evidence a lawful basis, a named human decision-maker or a completed consultation will have bought sixteen months of engineering it cannot deploy.

Action: By 31 March 2027, produce a register of every system that screens, scores, schedules, monitors or ranks a worker, naming for each the accountable person, the lawful basis, the log-retention arrangement and the consultation status, and take it through the works council or equivalent body wherever one exists.

The Rented Advantage

Churn returns and capability stays personal. People carry their AI fluency between employers, and the premium for it is set by the external market rather than by any job architecture. Recruitment becomes the dominant lever again and retention becomes expensive, because the thing being retained walks out of the building every evening. Pay structures come under pressure from two directions at once: incumbents who did not receive the premium ask why, and equal-pay machinery asks the employer to name the criterion. Organisations that never wrote the criterion down find themselves defending a differential they cannot describe. Internal development looks like a subsidy to competitors, so nobody funds it, and the junior tier stays thin.

Early indicators: A quits rate returning above its pre-freeze range; advertised AI premiums widening rather than converging; equal-pay information requests naming AI skills as the contested criterion.

The Reopened Ladder

Churn returns and capability has been absorbed into the organisation rather than the individual. Agents are registered, owned and logged, so what a person brings is judgment about them rather than private fluency with them. The junior tier rebuilds around supervision, exception handling and review, which are teachable and therefore hireable. Mobility works again in both directions: people move between employers, and employers can absorb them because the capability sits in the system they join. Pay differentiation inside a grade fades because the tool is institutional. The constraint moves to training throughput and to the quality of the audit trail, and the organisations that invested early in both are the ones that can hire at speed.

Early indicators: Agent counts appearing in workforce or human-capital reporting; entry-level postings specifying oversight rather than AI-tool proficiency; internal lateral-move rates rising alongside external quits.

The Sealed Grade

Nobody leaves and capability stays personal. This is the extension of what the current data shows, and it is the least comfortable of the four. Progression stalls because the external route is shut and the internal route was never built. Pay differences between people on the same grade harden into something everybody can see and nobody has written down, which is the exact condition equal-value law is designed to expose. Mid-career plateau spreads, junior hiring stays suppressed, and the experience the organisation will need in 2029 is not being made anywhere. Engagement scores stay high because the alternative is worse, which makes the problem invisible to the board for another two years.

Early indicators: Lateral-move rates flat while attrition falls; a rising share of pay-differential queries reaching HR; median tenure in the same role passing three years across a whole grade.

The Registered Workforce

Nobody leaves and capability has moved into the system of record. The organisation runs a single register covering people and the automated systems acting under their authority, with a named owner, a lawful basis and a retained log for each. Workforce planning becomes a portfolio question rather than a headcount one, and the compliance work done during the deferral window turns out to have built the management instrument as a side effect. Internal mobility becomes the main progression channel and is funded as one. This is the most demanding of the four to reach and the most durable once reached, because it survives both a reopening of the external market and a tightening of the rules.

Early indicators: A works agreement or equivalent covering automated management systems; log-retention arrangements audited rather than asserted; workforce plans expressed as people plus systems.

What We Are Not Planning For

Labour market

Economy-wide AI job displacement inside this planning horizon

The single most-cited source for the entry-level shock is explicit on this point: "We find no evidence of widespread, economy-wide job displacement." (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, August 2026) Job-postings evidence puts the divergence before ChatGPT and stable after 2023 (Federal Reserve Bank of New York, May 2026), and the European aggregate has barely moved: in June 2026, the youth unemployment rate was 15.5% in the EU, up from 15.4% in the previous month (Eurostat, July 2026). Planning for a general displacement event would misallocate attention away from the two things that are measurably happening: the junior tier is being redesigned and nobody is changing jobs.

↺ Reinstated if EU youth unemployment rises by more than a point and a half across two consecutive quarters while the overall rate stays flat.

Regulation

A dedicated EU directive on algorithmic management before 2028

The Commission has folded workplace AI into the broader Quality Jobs Act and on 20 July 2026 opened only the second-phase social-partner consultation under Article 154 (European Commission, DG Employment, July 2026), which is a pre-legislative step rather than a proposal. The duties that actually bind employers in the interim already exist and are covered in Theme 4, so nothing is lost by leaving the hypothetical instrument out of the plan.

↺ Reinstated if the Commission tables a legislative proposal, or the social partners open negotiations under Article 155.

Monetary policy

A mobility rebound delivered by rate cuts alone

The Cleveland Fed attributes the confluence of low hire, quit and layoff rates to a long-term downtrend in labour market fluidity in general rather than to the rate cycle (Federal Reserve Bank of Cleveland, August 2026), and notes hire rates sitting below their levels during the strong expansions of the 2000s and 2010s. A plan that waits for monetary policy to restore internal progression is therefore waiting on a mechanism the measuring institution does not think is the operative one. This exclusion is the one most likely to be wrong, and Theme 2 carries the number that would overturn it.

↺ Reinstated if the quits rate holds above 2.4 percent for two consecutive quarters alongside a widening switcher premium.

Discussion Points for the Leadership Team

  1. If our retention improved this year, can we show it was chosen rather than inherited from a market where nobody is moving? What would we do differently if we concluded it was inherited?
  2. We are being asked to fund internal mobility and to cut the junior intake in the same planning round. Which of the two do we believe more, and what evidence would we accept as settling it?
  3. If AI fluency is worth paying for, why is it not in the grade descriptors? If it is not worth paying for, why does it appear in our offers?
  4. The compliance date moved to December 2027 and the duties did not. Are we treating the next sixteen months as engineering time or as evidence-building time, and who owns the difference?
  5. The randomised evidence says AI narrows the performance gap between people rather than widening it. If that holds, which of our current talent and reward decisions is pointed the wrong way?
