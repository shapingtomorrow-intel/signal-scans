Why This Matters Now

Two pressures meet this winter on the same electricity and heating bills. War around the Strait of Hormuz has left Europe with less gas in store than usual, and prices for homes are rising again. At the same time, demand from data centres is growing faster than grids can connect it.

Governments are responding by deciding for themselves who gets connected, who is cut off first in a shortage and who pays for new power plants. A strong El Niño adds weather risk to grids that came close to their limits last winter.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: work out what your organisation would pay, and what it would lose, if energy costs stay at this winter’s level through 2027 and your power supply comes with conditions attached.

Executive Synthesis

Which of our plans for 2027 assumes energy costs fall back after this winter, and that a grid connection, once granted, cannot be cut?

The evidence in this briefing says gas stores are low going into winter, and governments in Britain, Spain and Texas are writing rules on who connects and who is cut first.

This winter, homes and data centres are competing for the same scarce energy, and governments are starting to decide who wins. The Strait of Hormuz crisis disrupted roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG trade (S&P Global Energy, Nov 2026), and EU gas stores were just over 71% full on 28 September against a five-year average of 86% (Oilprice.com, Sep 2026). Ofgem raised Britain’s cap by 4% in October because of the conflict (Ofgem, Aug 2026). Texas has 474 GW of connection requests from large users, about 90% of them data centres (Texas Public Radio, Aug 2026). Part of today’s calm rests on something that can turn: the US grid got through last winter only by using every emergency tool it had (Industrial Info Resources, on FERC and NERC, Sep 2026). September’s briefing found that paying more no longer brought more power; this edition finds the shortage moving from the price of power to who is allowed to have it.

Several things are open. Will a cold February empty German gas stores, as the storage industry warns could happen in a winter like 2010 (ZDFheute, in German, Sep 2026)? Will households carry the cost of the largest US grid’s emergency purchase, now that PJM leaves retail allocation to each state (PJM, Nov 2026)? And how much of the data-centre queue is real, when 42% of PJM’s forecast growth is already delayed or cancelled (Forbes, on PJM’s market monitor, Oct 2026)?

Looking ahead to 2030, Europe’s gas network operators expect stores to refill to only 65% by September 2027 if LNG stays tight (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026); China’s computing could use about 6% of its electricity (Yicai, in Chinese, Aug 2026); and Britain will cap data-centre connections by region after 2030 (Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, Oct 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the power shortage is real, not an artefact of speculative requests. Take PJM’s cancelled and delayed data-centre load out and its 6,831.3 MW shortfall shrinks to 3,055.0 MW (Forbes, on PJM’s market monitor, Oct 2026), and studies find data-centre growth has so far lowered average retail rates (Latitude Media, Jun 2026). If the queue is mostly phantom, the new rules are filters, not rationing.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Fix what you can of next year’s energy cost before the January cap and the 2027 refill season.
  2. Plan every new site on the assumption that its connection can be capped or cut. The rules can still be shaped.
  3. Firm up or give back equipment reservations.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. The gas shock will put winter bills back at the top of the agenda

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Gas markets and Qatar’s supply on the price; energy regulators on the caps; governments on support · Room to act: Moderate: buyers choose how much of next year’s cost to fix now, and how exposed to gas they stay

Europe went into the winter of 2022 with full gas stores and still faced a price shock. This year it goes into winter with stores well below normal and part of its LNG supply cut off by the war around the Strait of Hormuz. Bills for homes are rising again in Britain, France and Italy, and the cost of refilling the stores next summer will set bills for 2027.

Sources: (Ofgem, Aug 2026); (Oilprice.com, Sep 2026); (Connaissance des Énergies (AFP), on the CRE, in French, Nov 2026); (Cornwall Insight, Nov 2026); (FOX10 News, on NOAA, Nov 2026); (ZDFheute, in German, Sep 2026); (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, Sep 2026); (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026). The German warning applies only to a winter as cold as 2010; the January figure is a forecast, not the cap.

  • Supply has been cut at the source. The Strait of Hormuz crisis disrupted roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG trade (S&P Global Energy, Nov 2026), QatarEnergy extended force majeure on LNG deliveries through November, and 35 cargoes under one Italian buyer’s long-term contract have not arrived this year (Oilprice.com, Sep 2026).
  • The stores are low. EU gas storage was just over 71% full on 28 September, against a five-year average of 86%, with Germany below 58% (Oilprice.com, Sep 2026); Germany’s storage industry warns its stores would not last a winter as cold as 2010 (ZDFheute, in German, Sep 2026).
  • Bills are following. Ofgem raised Britain’s cap by 4% in October because of higher gas prices caused by the Middle East conflict (Ofgem, Aug 2026), and Cornwall Insight forecasts a typical bill of £1,999 a year from January (Cornwall Insight, Nov 2026). France’s regulator raises its reference gas price by 5.8% in December, taking the average 2026 bill 11.8% above 2025’s (Connaissance des Énergies (AFP), on the CRE, in French, Nov 2026), and Italy’s typical gas bill rose 9.1% in October to 1,945 euros a year (Open, on ARERA, in Italian, Nov 2026).
  • Electricity is better protected than in 2022. ECB economists find wholesale power prices have reacted far less to gas this time, partly because renewables cushion them, though wholesale rises take several months to reach bills (European Central Bank, Jun 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Refill: Europe’s gas network operators expect stores to reach 90% by the end of September 2027 if LNG supply is ample, but only 65% if it stays tight (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026).

Demand: In a cold winter with further disruption, Europe may need to cut gas demand by up to 15% (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026).

Switching: Electricity is just over 20% of the world’s final energy use; COP31’s pledge targets 35% by 2035, while S&P Global’s base case reaches about 27% (S&P Global Energy, Nov 2026).

What it means: Budget for gas-linked costs to stay at this winter’s level through the 2027 refill, not just until spring.

Counter-argument

This is not 2022. Ofgem notes prices are still well below the height of that crisis (Ofgem, Aug 2026), authorities say Europe faces no immediate gas shortage (Oilprice.com, Sep 2026), and the gas network operators find Europe copes without demand cuts in a normal winter (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026). If Qatari supply returns in the new year, refilling could be quick and the rise in bills short.

On the System Map: loop B2 (bills bring the backlash) is turning: higher gas costs are reaching bills in Britain, France and Italy, and governments are under pressure to act; leverage point 1 sits on locking in energy costs.

2. Governments, not markets, will decide which data centres get connected

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Ministers, energy regulators and grid operators in Britain, Germany, Spain, Texas and the largest US grid · Room to act: Moderate: the rules are still being written, and large users can take part or adapt their plans to them

For most of the grid’s history, a new user that paid for its connection joined the queue and was connected in turn. That is ending. Requests from data centres now far exceed what grids can connect, so governments and regulators are writing rules that decide who connects first, on what conditions, and who is cut off first in a shortage.

Sources: (Texas Public Radio, Aug 2026); (Ofgem, Jul 2026); (ITmedia, in Japanese, Sep 2026); (Energías Renovables, in Spanish, Oct 2026). The four figures count different things (requests, applications, reserved capacity and granted rights), so they show the scale of the queue in each place rather than a like-for-like comparison. About 90% of the Texas requests and 73 GW of Britain’s are data centres.

  • The queues are far larger than the grids. Texas will not connect new data centres until state regulators finish an audit of large users requesting 474 GW, more than five times record demand, about 90% of it data centres (Texas Public Radio, Aug 2026). Britain’s demand applications rose from 41 GW to 125 GW in under a year, 73 GW of them data centres (Ofgem, Jul 2026), and in Tokyo Electric’s area alone data centres have reserved 15 GW (ITmedia, in Japanese, Sep 2026).
  • Governments are choosing who goes first. Britain will let its grid operator connect government-chosen strategic projects first and will cap data-centre connections by region after 2030, with the criteria still to come (Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, Oct 2026). Spain’s draft decree makes grid access depend on matching 80% of use with new renewable power, hour by hour (Energías Renovables, in Spanish, Oct 2026).
  • Being cut first is becoming the price of getting in. Spain’s revised decree gives data centres five more years to comply if they accept that the grid operator can cut their supply without compensation, first in line (Roams, in Spanish, Oct 2026). In Germany, 91% of data-centre operators see access to power as a major challenge, and their association accepts connections that can be cut back only with a guaranteed minimum (Bitkom, in German, Nov 2026).
  • Regulators are also guarding who may do the cutting. The US federal regulator rejected PJM provisions that would dictate which retail customers are curtailed (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, Oct 2026), leaving that choice to the states.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Spain would pay heavy industry to switch off, but not data centres. Large industrial users in Spain’s power-reduction scheme are paid for taking part; under the revised decree data centres would not be, and would be the first disconnected (Roams, in Spanish, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Demand: Computing in China could use about 800 billion kWh by 2030, about 6% of its electricity, and its plan now requires computing and power projects to be planned together (Yicai, in Chinese, Aug 2026).

Japan: Peak demand from data centres and chip plants is projected to rise from 0.83 GW in 2026 to 7.62 GW by 2035 (ITmedia, in Japanese, Sep 2026).

Rules: Britain’s regional caps on data-centre connections apply after 2030 (Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, Oct 2026).

What it means: Treat a grid connection as a permission with conditions that can change, and take part while the conditions are written.

Counter-argument

Much of the queue may never be built. PJM’s market monitor counts 42% of its forecast data-centre growth as delayed or cancelled (Forbes, on PJM’s market monitor, Oct 2026), and Ofgem’s new fees target speculative projects (Ofgem, Jul 2026). If the rules mainly remove phantom demand, real projects may find connecting easier, not harder. Data centres’ offer to flex their use is limited too: only 3% of their capacity is expected to be available for load shifting in 2030 (Ember, Oct 2026).

On the System Map: loop B1 (rules filter the queue) is turning: Britain, Spain, Texas and Germany are writing filters, and 42% of PJM’s forecast data-centre growth is already delayed or cancelled; leverage point 2 sits on connection terms.

3. Households will be asked to pay for a power shortfall they did not cause

Near-Term

Weight: high · Who decides: US federal and state regulators, the largest US grid operator (PJM), and European governments on grid charges · Room to act: Low for households, moderate for large users, who can offer to pay their own way and so shape the rules

When a grid runs short, someone pays to fix it. In the largest US grid, PJM, data-centre growth has created a shortfall that an emergency purchase of new supply is meant to fill. Who pays for that purchase, and for the wider grid, is being decided now, and the default routes run through every customer’s bill.

  • The bill for the shortfall is large. PJM’s market monitor says data-centre load has added $29.4 billion to the cost of capacity, and put the 2028/29 shortfall at 6,831.3 MW (Monitoring Analytics, PJM’s market monitor, Sep 2026).
  • The emergency purchase is on hold over who pays. PJM proposed contracts of up to 15 years at up to $555/MW-day (PJM, Jul 2026); the federal regulator accepted it but suspended it to 28 February 2027 and opened a hearing on cost allocation (Federal Energy Regulatory Commission, Sep 2026). PJM’s amended filing charges utilities by each zone’s share of large-load growth and leaves retail allocation, including to data centres, to the states (PJM, Nov 2026).
  • Some states are making large users pay. California’s new laws make data centres pay for any upgrade costs, to stop them shifting costs onto everyday ratepayers (Office of the Governor of California, Sep 2026), and Pennsylvania’s regulator approved a tentative order to cut certain large new loads before other customers (Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission, Oct 2026).
  • In Europe, governments are paying less of the grid. Germany plans to cut its subsidy for transmission charges from 6.5 billion euros in 2026 to about 5.5 billion in 2027, adding about 0.25 cents per kWh to household grid charges for each billion less (Haufe, in German, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Contracts: Backstop contracts of up to 15 years would lock in today’s cost decisions into the 2040s (PJM, Jul 2026).

States: Pennsylvania’s final order on cutting large loads first is planned for 28 January 2027 (Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission, Oct 2026).

Rates: US residential rates rose 6% in 2025, more than twice inflation (Latitude Media, Jun 2026).

What it means: Expect the cost of the shortfall to be settled state by state in 2027, and assume households carry part of it unless a state says otherwise.

Counter-argument

Data centres may lower bills, not raise them. EPRI found data-centre growth associated with retail rates about 6% lower on average from 2019 to 2024, because fixed grid costs are spread over more sales (Latitude Media, Jun 2026). And with cancelled and delayed load removed, PJM’s shortfall shrinks from 6,831.3 MW to 3,055.0 MW (Forbes, on PJM’s market monitor, Oct 2026). If the shortfall shrinks and states follow California, households may pay little.

On the System Map: loop B2 (bills bring the backlash) is priming in the US: California and Pennsylvania are moving costs and cuts onto large users, while PJM leaves retail allocation to the states; leverage point 2 sits on connection terms.

4. A shortage of grid equipment, not money, will set the pace to 2030

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: A small number of turbine, transformer and cable makers; governments on which suppliers are allowed · Room to act: Moderate: buyers decide whether to firm reservations, order early and standardise designs

New power plants and grid links need turbines, transformers, switchgear and cable, and a handful of makers supply most of them. Their queues now set the pace of the build-out more than money does, and Europe’s grid operators want most of their equipment in the same few years.

Sources: (McKinsey & Company, Sep 2026); (Rystad Energy, May 2026). “More than” bars mark the lower bound each source gives; the longest wait is McKinsey’s, the others Rystad Energy’s.

  • Turbine queues keep lengthening. GE Vernova’s gas-power backlog and slot reservations reached 122 GW, of which 58 GW are reservations rather than firm orders, and it expects at least 130 GW by the end of the year (Turbomachinery International, on GE Vernova, Oct 2026).
  • Transformers are the tightest. Lead times have stretched from about one year to up to five (McKinsey & Company, Sep 2026), and exceed 12 months for most power transformers and 24 months for the largest (Rystad Energy, May 2026).
  • Europe wants everything at once. Its grid operators’ planned additions in almost every type of equipment peak at the same time, between 2028 and 2032, including about 34,000 km of new cable by 2035 (ENTSO-E, Nov 2026).
  • US rules are narrowing the supplier list. A national emergency order lets the Energy Secretary name pre-qualified vendors and set conditions on foreign equipment (The White House, Aug 2026); Wood Mackenzie says it could affect more than $22 billion of imports since 2025, on top of an estimated 15% shortage of power transformers (Wood Mackenzie, Aug 2026).
  • Makers are booming. Siemens Energy’s grid order backlog reached €51bn (Siemens Energy, Aug 2026); China’s transformer exports rose 27% in the first four months of 2026, with some US orders booked into 2028 and 2029 (Sina Finance, in Chinese, Jun 2026); and HD Hyundai Electric raised its order target by 22.8% (Digital Daily, in Korean, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Turbine makers are buying their own suppliers. GE Vernova agreed to buy Trench, a maker of high-voltage components, for $9.0 billion (Turbomachinery International, on GE Vernova, Oct 2026): securing parts is now worth paying for.

Outlook to 2030

Peak: Europe’s grid equipment needs peak in 2028 to 2032 (ENTSO-E, Nov 2026).

Boom: Chinese makers expect strong export demand to last two to three more years (Sina Finance, in Chinese, Jun 2026).

Supply: Nearly 200 GVA of new transformer factory capacity is expected online in 2026, led by the US (Rystad Energy, May 2026).

What it means: Plans that assume equipment on order arrives on time are the weak point; a firm delivery slot is worth more than the money to fill it.

Counter-argument

The squeeze may ease sooner than the queues suggest. New factories are opening (Rystad Energy, May 2026), Siemens Energy says expanded production capacity supported its growth (Siemens Energy, Aug 2026), and 58 GW of GE Vernova’s queue are reservations that can still be released (Turbomachinery International, on GE Vernova, Oct 2026). If demand forecasts fall, today’s shortage could turn into spare capacity after 2030.

On the System Map: loop R1 (reservations lengthen the queue) is turning: 58 GW of GE Vernova’s 122 GW queue are reservations held against future need; leverage point 3 sits on delivery slots.

5. A very strong El Niño winter will test grids with thinner safety margins

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The weather; grid operators and regulators on emergency measures; the US Department of Energy on emergency orders · Room to act: Low on the weather, moderate on how much a business relies on emergency measures holding

Grids are built to cope with bad weather, but their spare margin is thinner than it was. Last winter the US grid held only by using every emergency tool it had, including temporary federal orders to keep plants and backup generators running. This winter a strong El Niño adds to the uncertainty, after a summer in which heat cut record amounts of French nuclear output.

  • El Niño is strong and may be historic. NOAA gives a better than 79% chance of a strong to very strong event through January to March 2027, and about a 60% chance it reaches historic strength (FOX10 News, on NOAA, Nov 2026).
  • Last winter was a near miss. NERC’s chief said storms brought the US system close to the edge, with unplanned generator outages peaking at 68,169 MW in January (Renewable Energy World, Sep 2026); the regulators’ review found no customers were cut off, but operators used their full set of emergency tools (Industrial Info Resources, on FERC and NERC, Sep 2026).
  • Emergency orders now carry part of the load. The US Department of Energy has let PJM run backup generators at large users as a last resort, beyond their permit limits (US Department of Energy, Sep 2026); PJM lists five such orders this year (PJM, Oct 2026). But a federal appeals court ruled the first order keeping a Michigan coal plant open an unlawful use of the power (Michigan Attorney General, Sep 2026), and the department has since ordered a Colorado coal unit kept available to 25 December (US Department of Energy, Sep 2026).
  • Heat is now a winter planner’s problem too. Outlook to 2030

Demand: US electricity sales are expected to set records of 4,135 billion kWh in 2026 and 4,211 billion in 2027 (Renewable Energy World, Sep 2026).

What it means: Plan for winter emergencies to be met by temporary orders and backup generators that may not be available next time.

Counter-argument

Europe’s grid operators find an overall favourable position for the winter, with 134 GW more renewable capacity and 38 GW of batteries (ENTSO-E, Nov 2026), and the US grid got through last winter without cutting anyone off (Industrial Info Resources, on FERC and NERC, Sep 2026). El Niño shifts the odds, not the weather on any given week; a mild winter would take much of the pressure off gas stores and grids alike.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Lock in what you can of next year’s energy cost before the January cap and the refill season

EU gas stores were just over 71% full on 28 September against a usual 86% (Oilprice.com, Sep 2026), and stores may refill to only 65% by September 2027 if LNG stays tight (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026). Organisations that fix part of their 2027 cost now trade some upside for a budget they can plan against.

Action: Finance directors to fix or hedge at least part of 2027 energy costs and to set the 2027 budget against prices at this winter’s level, by 31 January 2027.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 5. On the System Map: leverage point 1, energy costs, where loop B2 (bills bring the backlash) reaches organisations before it reaches policy.

Implication 2: Plan every new site on the assumption that its connection can be capped or cut

Britain will connect government-chosen projects first and cap data-centre volumes by region after 2030 (Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, Oct 2026), and Spain’s revised decree would let its grid operator cut data centres first, unpaid (Roams, in Spanish, Oct 2026). Large users that take part in writing these terms can shape them; those that wait inherit them.

Action: Boards of power-hungry businesses to name an owner for connection terms in each market and respond to Britain’s framework, Germany’s grid package and Pennsylvania’s order, which is final on 28 January 2027, by 31 March 2027.

Prepare Draws on Themes 2 and 3. On the System Map: leverage point 2, connection terms, where loop B1 (rules filter the queue) decides who stays in it.

Implication 3: Firm up or give back equipment reservations, and order long-lead grid items now

Transformer lead times run up to five years (McKinsey & Company, Sep 2026), Europe’s grid equipment needs peak together in 2028 to 2032 (ENTSO-E, Nov 2026), and 58 GW of GE Vernova’s queue are reservations (Turbomachinery International, on GE Vernova, Oct 2026). A reservation held without a decision is a cost to the holder and a delay to everyone else.

Action: Capital planning teams to turn every equipment reservation into a firm order or a release, and place orders for transformers needed before 2030, by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 4. On the System Map: leverage point 3, delivery slots, where loop R1 (reservations lengthen the queue) can be slowed.

Implication 4: Track which emergency measures carry the grid through this winter, and whether they survive

Last winter the US grid held only with every emergency tool in use (Industrial Info Resources, on FERC and NERC, Sep 2026), and a federal appeals court has ruled one emergency order unlawful (Michigan Attorney General, Sep 2026). Whether temporary orders and backup generators are there next time decides how much spare margin is real.

Action: Risk and resilience teams to log each emergency order, backup-generator call and demand cut affecting their sites from December to March, and review the list before the 2027 to 2028 winter plan.

Monitor Draws on Themes 5 and 3. On the System Map: leverage point 4, the winter emergency tools, an outside switch that acts on every loop.

Pay to Plug In

↗ Shift from todayFrom connection queues of 474 GW in Texas (Texas Public Radio, Aug 2026) to connections sold at a price that large users pay in full.

In 2030 a grid connection is something large users buy. Fees, deposits and capacity charges sort serious projects from speculative ones, and data centres pay the full cost of the grid they need. Households are largely protected, but connections go to whoever can pay most, wherever the grid has room.

Early indicators: Connection fees and deposits spread; states follow California in making data centres pay; queues shrink as speculative projects drop out.

Licensed Power

↗ Shift from todayFrom Britain’s plan to connect government-chosen projects first (Department for Energy Security and Net Zero, Oct 2026) to connections allocated by the state on strategic grounds.

In 2030 governments decide who connects, by region and by national priority, and large users pay their own way and accept being cut off first in a shortage. Grids are orderly and households protected, but investment follows ministers’ lists, and projects that are not on them go elsewhere.

Early indicators: Britain publishes its criteria; Spain approves its decree with unpaid cut-offs; more countries cap data-centre connections by region.

The Bill Shock Years

↗ Shift from todayFrom a $29.4 billion addition to capacity costs (Monitoring Analytics, PJM’s market monitor, Sep 2026) to a decade in which the market rations power and households pay for the shortfall.

In 2030 power still goes to whoever bids most, and the cost of new plants and grids lands on every customer’s bill. Data centres connect where they can pay, energy-intensive industry struggles, and electricity prices become a central political issue in elections on both sides of the Atlantic.

Early indicators: Capacity prices stay at their caps; emergency purchases are charged across all customers; household bills keep rising faster than inflation.

Strategic Subsidy

↗ Shift from todayFrom a Chinese plan that sites computing and power together (Yicai, in Chinese, Aug 2026) to governments that choose which projects connect and spread their cost across all bills.

In 2030 governments treat data centres and new industry as national strategy. They choose who connects and fund the grid for them through everyone’s bills or taxes. Large projects get built quickly where the state wants them, and households pay for a policy rather than a price.

Early indicators: Governments name priority projects and fund their grid links; grid charges rise for all customers; subsidies for grid costs grow rather than shrink.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes. In November the reading leans firmly towards the state deciding who connects, and towards households paying.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Weak signal

A cold winter forces Europe to ration gas to industry

Window: 2027 to 2032 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Since 2022, Europe has treated gas shortages as a price problem it can buy its way out of.

German stores would not last a winter as cold as 2010 (ZDFheute, in German, Sep 2026), and a cold winter with further disruption could require cutting gas demand by up to 15% (S&P Global Energy, on ENTSOG, Oct 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By March 2028: an EU member state orders mandatory cuts in industrial gas use during a winter; evidence: the government’s published order.
  • By March 2029: EU gas storage falls below 30% full on 1 February; evidence: Gas Infrastructure Europe’s published storage data.

What it touches today: Energy-intensive production in Europe, and contracts that assume gas is always available at a price.

Emerging

Data centres become the grid’s shock absorber, switched off first in every shortage

Window: 2028 to 2034 · Probability band: Medium (40 to 60%)

Why it would surprise: Data centres are designed and sold as always on, with backup power for the rare outage.

Spain’s revised decree would cut data centres first, unpaid (Roams, in Spanish, Oct 2026), Pennsylvania’s tentative order cuts certain large new loads first (Pennsylvania Public Utility Commission, Oct 2026), and PJM can call on data-centre backup generators in emergencies (US Department of Energy, Sep 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: Spain, or another EU country, adopts a rule letting its grid operator cut data centres first without payment; evidence: the official gazette.
  • By March 2029: a grid operator in Europe or the US cuts supply to a data centre under such a rule during a shortage; evidence: the operator’s event report.

What it touches today: Data-centre service promises, backup-power budgets and the value of sites in grids that ration.

Weak signal

The data-centre boom stalls and households pay for grids built for it

Window: 2029 to 2035 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Plans treat forecast data-centre demand as near certain, and the grid built for it as sure to be used.

42% of PJM’s forecast data-centre growth is already delayed or cancelled (Forbes, on PJM’s market monitor, Oct 2026), and studies warn remaining customers carry fixed grid costs if demand falls away (Latitude Media, Jun 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: PJM’s market monitor reports more than half of forecast data-centre growth delayed or cancelled; evidence: its published reports.
  • By December 2029: a US utility asks to recover the cost of grid upgrades built for a cancelled large load from all customers; evidence: the state regulator’s docket.

What it touches today: Long-term contracts and grid charges sized for demand that may not arrive.
