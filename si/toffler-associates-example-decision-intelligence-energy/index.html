Key risks

  • Plans that read a high capacity price as a sign that supply is coming have it the wrong way round. PJM's auction cleared at its cap yet brought forward only 317.1 MW of new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). See Theme 1 →
  • Large users that connect now sign long contracts whose minimum charges can outlast their business case. Oregon's approved terms set minimum charges at 90% of the capacity a customer reserves (Utility Dive, May 2026). See Theme 3 →
  • Owners and lenders of river-cooled plants rely on availability assumptions that a dry summer breaks. A record 15.6% of French nuclear capacity was missing in July (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026). See Theme 5 →

Key opportunities

  • Developers who help write the new connection terms face fewer surprises when the larger load arrives. The IEA expects data centres to use about 950 TWh of electricity in 2030, roughly double 2025 (IEA, Apr 2026). See Theme 2 →
  • Buyers who secure an early delivery slot for turbines or transformers hold the scarcest asset in the system. GE Vernova expects at least 125 GW of gas turbine orders and reserved slots by the end of 2026 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). See Theme 4 →
  • Asset owners who price a low-water case first can show the risk before lenders and insurers do. With the Rhine at Kaub down to 28 centimetres, inland coal barge loadings were cut by three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026). See Theme 5 →

Why This Matters Now

Electricity buyers spent 2026 discovering that money is no longer the binding constraint. Prices hit their ceilings and almost no new power arrived, because what is scarce is the equipment, the permission and the water to run a plant.

Regulators on three continents have rewritten what a large new customer must sign to connect, governments are deciding in public who pays for the network, and the manufacturers have sold out their factories to 2030.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: treat a connection as a long contract with obligations, not as a place in a queue, and check whether anything you own or finance depends on a river behaving the way it used to. The early warning is in the terms of the contract, not the price.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week assume that a high enough price will bring the power?

The evidence in this briefing says the highest price the rules allow bought 317 megawatts.

This cycle the electricity system reached the top of its price signal and got almost nothing back. PJM, which runs the grid for 13 US states and Washington DC, cleared its 2028/2029 auction at the highest price its rules allow, 6,831.3 MW short of what reliability requires, and brought forward 317.1 MW of new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). Money is no longer what is scarce. Equipment, permission to connect and cooling water are.

Three terms are being rewritten at once. Regulators in the United States, Britain and Japan have turned a request to connect a large new customer, usually a data centre, into a long contract with conditions (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026); twenty-three US states now have a special tariff for large loads (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026); and the turbine makers have sold their output to the end of the decade (GE Vernova, Jul 2026). A dry summer added its own warning, as low and warm rivers took nuclear and coal plants offline in Romania, France and along the Rhine (Euronews, Aug 2026) (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026).

Looking ahead to 2030, the IEA expects data centres to use about 950 TWh of electricity, roughly double 2025, with 15 to 27 GW possibly supplied by gas plants built on site (IEA, Apr 2026), while developers chase 63 GW of new gas capacity (Wood Mackenzie, reported by Utility Dive, Apr 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The analysis assumes a delivery problem, not a forecasting one: if the large loads do not arrive, the auctions price demand that never comes, and the Texas queue holds around 460,000 MW of interest against a 91.3 GW peak (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026). The warning sign is the largest cloud companies cutting spending plans two quarters running; debt now funds 32% of that spending, against 9% two years earlier (FactSet, Jul 2026).

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Treat every large new connection as a long contract, not a place in a queue. Oregon's terms run up to thirty years, with minimum charges at 90% of contracted capacity.
  2. Secure a delivery slot for equipment rather than a choice of technology. Turbine makers are sold out to 2030 and large transformers take four years.
  3. Test every river-cooled plant and barge-supplied fuel contract against a dry summer. The Rhine and the Danube took generation and fuel deliveries offline this summer.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

Audience Snapshots

One card per reader group, each with one question a team can take to its next meeting.

If you are trying to connect a large new load

Connecting to the grid now means signing a long contract with conditions

What is changing: Oregon's approved terms run 10 to 30 years (Utility Dive, May 2026), and Texas requires a large load to be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes (White & Case, Aug 2026).

Question for your next meeting: Does our site still pay off with a thirty-year minimum charge at 90% of contracted capacity, and who owns that liability?

What it is for: Turns each connection into a liability that a named person owns, before the next capital approval.

If you set or approve network tariffs

Four jurisdictions have chosen four different ways to share the cost of the grid

What is changing: Twenty-three US states now have a special tariff for large loads (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026), and Spain would make network access itself revocable (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026).

Question for your next meeting: If the forecast load does not arrive, who pays for the network built for it: the developer or the household?

What it is for: Shows whether a tariff protects households when demand disappoints, not only when it arrives.

If you finance or insure generation assets

The scarce asset is a delivery slot, not the plant

What is changing: Turbine order books are full to the end of the decade (GE Vernova, Jul 2026) and new gas plants cost at least $2,500 per kW (IEEFA, Apr 2026).

Question for your next meeting: Does our capital plan assume a gas unit we cannot take delivery of before 2030, and what would bridging with solar and storage cost?

What it is for: Separates the plans that depend on equipment nobody can deliver from those that do not.

If your plant depends on river or sea water for cooling

Low rivers and warm water now take plants offline for weeks

What is changing: Romania's Cernavoda nuclear plant went fully offline (Euronews, Aug 2026), and low water on the Rhine cut coal barge loadings by about three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026).

Question for your next meeting: What happens to our revenue if our water limit binds for six weeks rather than six days, and does any contract we hold price that?

What it is for: Puts a number on a risk that most valuation models leave out.

1. Paying more did not buy more power

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Grid operators such as PJM, and the state governments that negotiate their price caps · Room to act: Low for most buyers, who pay the price set; medium for large buyers able to contract for supply directly

PJM's 2028/2029 auction hit the cap set by its rules, closed 6,831.3 MW below the amount it needs to keep the lights on, and brought forward only 317.1 MW of new generation (PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, Jul 2026). A rising price now signals that supply is missing, not that it is coming, and the cost lands on household bills rather than new plants.

PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction: what the ceiling price procured

Source: PJM 2028/2029 Base Residual Auction Report, July 2026. Bars are to scale against each other.

  • It was the third capped auction running, so the price no longer shows how short of power the system is (Utility Dive, Jul 2026).
  • Bills are already up, by around 20% in New Jersey, where capacity charges are 15% to 20% of a residential bill (New Jersey Board of Public Utilities, Aug 2026).

Outlook to 2030

PJM's own reading is that “The market is generating the price signal it was designed to generate during a shortage.” Its reform options include openly rationing power by place or type of customer (PJM, Powering Reliability Through Market Design, May 2026).

What it means: By 2030 the question is less what capacity costs than who gets it when there is not enough.

Counter-argument

A capped auction cannot be judged on what it bought, because the cap kept new supply out; Sierra Club argues the reverse, that annual customer costs in PJM have risen by $12.5bn since June 2025 and the cap is too loose (Sierra Club, Jul 2026). The test is whether the 2029/2030 auction brings forward materially more than 317.1 MW. If not, the problem is getting plants built, and this theme holds.

On the Causal Loop Diagram: B1 Price brings supply has stalled behind the equipment queue, and R1 Credibility trap turns high prices into caps that deter investment.

2. The regulator rewrote the rules for every large load

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Regulators: the US federal energy regulator, the Texas utility commission, Ofgem and Japan's energy agency · Room to act: High for developers, who choose where to connect and on what terms

Within four months, four jurisdictions turned a request to connect a large customer into a contract with conditions, without coordinating. The question has moved from whether power is available to what you must accept to get it.

  • On 18 June FERC ordered all six regional grid operators to justify or change how they connect very large users (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026), choosing regional rules over a national standard (Utility Dive, Jun 2026).
  • Texas went furthest: a large user sited next to a power plant must be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes (White & Case, Aug 2026).
  • Britain went after the queue: Ofgem is clearing speculative projects from a queue that grew from 41 GW to 125 GW (Ofgem, Jul 2026), with deposits and post-offer deadlines proposed for new users (Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Losing the connection as the penalty. Spain's draft decree would cut a data centre off the grid if it repeatedly fails to get 80% of its power from new renewable sources: “El incumplimiento reiterado podrá acarrear la pérdida del acceso a la red.” (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026) It gains weight if a second EU country copies the threat.
  • Weak signal Efficiency standards as a condition of entry. Japan requires centres built from fiscal 2029 to reach an efficiency rating (PUE, where 1.0 is perfect) of 1.3 (Japan's Agency for Natural Resources and Energy, May 2026), a hard number aimed at the data centre rather than the grid. It gains weight if a minimum efficiency appears in a US or EU tariff.

Outlook to 2030

The IEA expects data centres worldwide to roughly double their electricity use, from 485 TWh in 2025 to 950 TWh in 2030 (IEA, Apr 2026).

What it means: Connection terms written now will govern a much larger load by 2030.

Counter-argument

The reforms may change the price of connecting without limiting who connects: FERC's rulemaking aims at connection that is “timely, orderly, reliable, and non-discriminatory” (FERC Docket RM26-4, accessed Sep 2026), and NERC records 58.5 GW of new supply against 11 GW of extra peak demand (NERC 2026 Summer Reliability Assessment, May 2026). The test is whether any grid operator's response reduces how much new demand can connect, rather than who connects first.

On the Causal Loop Diagram: tighter connection terms clear speculative requests but push large loads towards their own supply, where R3 Race for turbines starts (leverage point 1).

Decision link: Strategic Implications 1 and 4.

3. Who pays for the grid became the live political question

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: State regulators and governments, often in an election year · Room to act: Medium: firms can shape terms in consultations; households have little say

Who pays for the grid stopped being a technical detail of utility pricing and became a policy tool, decided in public and often in an election year. The tools differ more than the problem does: the same data centre carries different costs in Oregon, Madrid and Munich.

Where Theme 1 meets Theme 3

Drawn from the cross-impact grid in the Energy, Infrastructure and Climate Resilience Change Tracker (27 August 2026 cycle), re-sourced against this cycle's evidence base.

  • Twenty-three US states have a special tariff for very large users and seven more have proposals pending (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026); regulators approved 29 in 2025 alone, against 14 in 2018 to 2024 (Smart Electric Power Alliance, Apr 2026).
  • Oregon shows a settled tariff: contracts of 10 to 30 years, the customer paying 100% of local network upgrades, and a minimum charge of 90% of the capacity it reserves (Utility Dive, May 2026).
  • In Europe, Germany subsidises transmission charges (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026) and the Commission proposed cutting network charges for some consumer groups (European Commission, updated Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Pennsylvania projects the price cap will be worth roughly $45bn to 67 million people over four years (Governor of Pennsylvania, Jul 2026). But the IEA sees 15 to 27 GW of gas plants built on site at data centres by 2030 (IEA, Apr 2026), which takes load, and the customers who pay for the network, off the shared grid.

What it means: By 2030 the argument may move from the cost of connecting to the grid to the cost of leaving it.

Counter-argument

A tariff written for a boom is tested by a bust: if demand does not arrive, households still pay for the network built for it, and new debt now funds 32% of the largest cloud companies' spending, against 9% in financial year 2024 (FactSet, Jul 2026). On the available evidence the tariffs move the cost, not the risk that demand falls short.

On the Causal Loop Diagram: B2 Make the large load pay moves cost off household bills, while R2 Cost of leaving shrinks the base that pays for the grid (leverage point 4).

Decision link: Strategic Implications 1, 3 and 4.

4. You cannot buy a turbine before 2030

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: The three large turbine makers, and whoever already holds a delivery slot · Room to act: High for buyers who can reserve a slot early or switch to solar, storage and flexible demand

New gas plants are not just expensive, they are unavailable: the three makers of large gas turbines have sold their output to the end of the decade, and prices are rising faster than utilities planned for. Whoever holds a delivery slot holds the scarce asset; everyone else waits at a rising price or builds something else.

  • GE Vernova's orders and reserved production slots for gas turbines grew from 100 GW to 116 GW in one quarter, and it expects at least 125 GW by the end of 2026 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026).
  • Mitsubishi's order book reached 35 GW, with first-quarter 2026 orders due for delivery between 2028 and 2030 (Utility Dive, Aug 2026); Siemens Energy's stands near 69 GW with waits of more than three years (Utility Dive, Aug 2026), and Ansaldo is expanding (POWER, Sep 2026).
  • The rising cost is what makes the gas build fragile. IEEFA puts the newest proposed gas plants at $2,500 per kW installed or more, roughly triple early-2020s costs (IEEFA, Apr 2026); Wood Mackenzie describes a supply-chain crisis in labour, components and trade costs that the makers' expansion has not relieved (Wood Mackenzie, reported by Utility Dive, Apr 2026); and utilities now compete for slots and defend the spend to regulators, with Duke alone deploying more than $1bn a month (Utility Dive, Aug 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Wood Mackenzie expects turbine prices to reach $600/kW by the end of 2027, up 195% since 2019, as developers chase 63 GW of gas capacity for 2026 to 2030 (Wood Mackenzie, reported by Utility Dive, Apr 2026). GE Vernova plans to raise its annual output to 30 GW only by 2030 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026).

What it means: Gas ordered today mostly arrives around 2030. Until then, new supply comes from solar, batteries and demand that can flex.

Counter-argument

A shortage of one technology is not a shortage of supply: in the first half of 2026 the United States added 11,458 MW of utility-scale solar and 8,207 MW of batteries against 2,707 MW of gas (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026). But solar at 32% of a Texan evening peak (US Energy Information Administration, Aug 2026) is not power guaranteed on a still winter night, the hour the capacity auction buys for.

On the Causal Loop Diagram: the turbine and transformer backlog delays new supply and sends buyers to solar and batteries; on-site gas lengthens the queue through R3 (leverage point 2).

Decision link: Strategic Implications 2 and 3.

5. Heat and low water stopped being a weather story

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Nobody: the weather decides, and plant operators and river authorities manage the consequences · Room to act: High for asset owners, lenders and insurers, who can price the risk and adapt the assets

How much water a river carries, and how warm it is, moved from a footnote in outage reports to a factor in which plants run, how fuel moves and what plants are worth. For a river-cooled coal, gas or nuclear plant, the assumption about how often it can run is now the number most likely to be wrong.

  • Romania's Cernavoda plant, normally a fifth of national electricity, was fully offline in August for only the second time in its history, the first being 2003 (Euronews, Aug 2026), with Danube flow at Romania's border at 1,650 cubic metres per second against a July average of 4,750 (Mediafax, Jul 2026).
  • France lost around 6% of nuclear output in July, with a record 15.6% of capacity missing (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026), and Alpine hydro ran at roughly half of normal in Austria and Switzerland (Euronews, Aug 2026).
  • Fuel could not move either. The Rhine at Kaub fell to 28 centimetres, the lowest since 2018, and inland coal barge loadings were cut by three-quarters (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Sea life as a cause of outages. Jellyfish blocked cooling-water intakes at Gravelines this summer (Agence France-Presse, via Boursorama, Aug 2026). It gains weight if another region reports the same within a year.
  • Weak signal A broader but shallower drought. Half of the EU and the UK was in drought in July but only 9% of territory at alert level, against 18% at the 2022 peak (the Commission's Joint Research Centre, Aug 2026). It points the other way if the 2027 season is again widespread but mild.

Outlook to 2030

EDF's losses to heat and low water, around 0.3% of output a year, could reach 1.4% by 2035 (Selectra, Aug 2026), and energy used for cooling worldwide is set to double by 2050 (MIT Technology Review, Jun 2026), so hot summers raise demand as they cut supply.

What it means: By 2030 a dry summer is a planning case, not an exception.

Counter-argument

2026 may have been widespread rather than severe, and France's system operator was right that supply security needed no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026). So the theme claims a risk to how often plants run and what that costs, not a risk of blackouts. It is wrong if 2027 passes with French outages back in their normal range.

On the Causal Loop Diagram: hot, dry summers are an outside switch that cuts thermal plant availability and widens the shortfall (leverage point 3).

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Re-assess large connections as long contracts, not places in a queue

Whoever owns site strategy should re-cost every existing and planned large connection against the terms now being approved, before the next investment approval. Oregon's terms run 10 to 30 years with minimum charges at 90% of reserved capacity (Utility Dive, May 2026), and twenty-three states have a similar tariff (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026). These are balance-sheet commitments with exit costs, hence Decide: the grid operators' responses to FERC (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026) will set the terms in the remaining markets during approvals.

Action: by 31 October 2026, the site or business development lead summarises the terms of each live connection on one page (length, minimum charge, upgrade costs, cost of leaving) and names an owner for each.

Decide Draws on Themes 1, 2 and 3. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 1, connection terms, where R3 Race for turbines starts.

Implication 2: Buy the delivery slot, not the technology

Capital planning should treat a place in the factory schedule as the scarce asset and decide whether to reserve one before the 2027 budget closes: turbine makers are sold out to 2030 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026) and large transformers take four years (pv magazine USA, May 2026). It is Prepare because the answer depends on how much guaranteed capacity is needed; for many patterns of demand, solar and batteries (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026) are the better stopgap. The move to avoid is waiting for turbine prices to fall, at $2,500 per kW and rising (IEEFA, Apr 2026).

Action: by 15 December 2026, capital planning states how much guaranteed capacity it needs, by season and hour, for 2029 to 2032, and whether to reserve a delivery slot against it.

Prepare Draws on Themes 4 and 1. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 2, the turbine and transformer backlog that stalls B1 Price brings supply.

Implication 3: Put a water and river-temperature test into valuation and fuel logistics

Asset owners, lenders and insurers should add a low-water case to the valuation of river-cooled plants, and to any fuel contract that moves by barge, before the next revaluation. This season brought a full Cernavoda shutdown (Euronews, Aug 2026) and a three-quarters cut in inland coal barge loadings (Insurance Journal, Jul 2026). It is Prepare because no blackout followed (RTE, Jun 2026): the risk is to how often plants run and what that costs, which valuation models absorb quietly.

Action: before the next annual revaluation, the risk team adds a six-week low-water case to every river-cooled plant and barge-supplied asset and reports the revenue at risk to the risk committee.

Prepare Draws on Themes 5 and 3. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 3, thermal plant availability, where the outside switch of hot, dry summers acts.

Implication 4: Track how each jurisdiction chooses to share the cost of the grid

Government affairs and regulatory teams should track the tool each market uses to share the cost of the grid, because each leaves the leftover cost with someone different: US state tariffs (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026), a voluntary federal pledge (the White House, Jul 2026), German subsidies for transmission charges (Bundesnetzagentur, May 2026) and Spain's revocable access (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026). Monitor is right because none has yet been tested by demand falling short.

Action: from Q4 2026, the regulatory lead keeps a one-line-per-jurisdiction list of these tools, reviewed quarterly, and flags any move from a voluntary promise to a binding tariff, or from a fine to disconnection.

Monitor Draws on Themes 3, 2 and 1. On the Causal Loop Diagram: leverage point 4, large-load tariffs, where B2 and R2 decide who carries the cost of the grid.

Build and Bill

↗ Shift from todayFrom 23 states with a special large-load tariff (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026) to long contracts that make every large load pay for the network it triggers.

Large loads connect to the shared grid on long contracts that make them pay for what they trigger, on the Oregon model (Utility Dive, May 2026). The network grows on their money, and capacity auctions clear below their caps again as solar, storage and flexible demand arrive.

Early indicators: Second and third states adopt Oregon-style terms; a capacity auction clears below its cap without a rule change.

Power Islands

↗ Shift from todayFrom 2,707 MW of new US gas plants in the first half of 2026 (Inside Climate News, Aug 2026) to large loads building their own supply, mostly gas, on site.

Large loads build or contract their own generation, often gas on site, and pay their own way: the IEA sees 15 to 27 GW of on-site gas at data centres by 2030 (IEA, Apr 2026). The grid loses customers it was counting on, regulators respond with standby and exit charges, and turbine slots become the prize.

Early indicators: On-site gas announcements outpace grid connection requests; standby-charge filings rise.

The Blame Auction

↗ Shift from todayFrom annual customer costs in PJM up $12.5bn since June 2025 (Sierra Club, Jul 2026) to a widening shortfall paid for by households under a political cap.

The closest to today. Large loads connect to the shared grid, but the cost lands on household bills, because tariffs are announced and not tested and price caps are set politically (Governor of Maryland, Jul 2026). The shortfall grows under the cap.

Early indicators: A fourth consecutive capped auction; electricity bills become an election issue in more states.

Behind the Meter

↗ Shift from todayFrom a voluntary pledge claimed to cover 80% of power delivered to US homes and businesses (the White House, Jul 2026) to households paying for a network the large loads have left.

Large loads supply themselves, but the network they leave behind is still paid for by everyone else. The base that pays for the grid shrinks, and the contest moves to the terms of leaving.

Early indicators: Exit-fee proposals appear; states debate charging departing loads for the network left behind.

Emerging

Disconnection, not price, becomes the standard penalty on large loads

Window: 2030 to 2035 · Probability band: Medium (40 to 60%)

Why it would surprise: Valuations treat a grid connection, once granted, as a permanent asset.

Spain's draft decree would remove grid access for repeated failure to source 80% of power from new renewables (Aragon Digital, Sep 2026), and Texas already requires co-located large loads to be able to disconnect fully within thirty minutes (White & Case, Aug 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a second EU country writes loss of grid access into law as a penalty for large users.
  • By December 2028: a US state tariff makes a large load's access revocable for breach of its terms.

Weak signal

A turbine glut after 2030 as the backlog lands on slower demand

Why it would surprise: Plans assume the turbine shortage lasts as long as the demand that caused it.

GE Vernova plans to make 30 GW a year by 2030 (GE Vernova, Jul 2026), while new debt now funds 32% of the largest cloud companies' spending (FactSet, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a turbine maker reports rising cancellations or resold delivery slots.

What We Are Not Planning For

Systemic reliability

A drought-driven European supply failure this winter

The 2026 season took generation offline without taking the system down: France's system operator said in advance that supply security required no particular vigilance (RTE, Jun 2026), and no shortfall followed. That risk sits inside Theme 5.

↺ Reinstated if a member state issues a winter supply warning attributable to hydrology, or French nuclear outages exceed their 2022 peak.

Jurisdiction

A single US federal rule on who pays for the grid, overriding the states

There is no evidence it is coming. FERC chose regional rules over a national standard (McGuireWoods, Jun 2026), and the federal instrument actually used was a voluntary pledge (the White House, Jul 2026). Twenty-three different state instruments already exist (Columbia's Sabin Center, Jun 2026).

↺ Reinstated if FERC starts writing rules on how retail customers share grid costs, or a federal bill on pricing for very large users gains committee support.

Demand

A collapse in data-centre electricity demand inside the planning horizon

It is not visible in the spending: the five largest cloud companies spent roughly $490bn in the twelve months to May 2026 (FactSet, Jul 2026), and ERCOT plans for roughly 175,000 MW by 2032 (The Texas Tribune, Jul 2026). Rising debt funding, tracked in Theme 3, is a risk to returns, not a near-term collapse.

↺ Reinstated if the largest cloud companies cut their spending plans for two quarters running, or a regional grid operator reports a net fall in requests to connect very large users.
