Five legislative vehicles the cycle's clusters depend on, and the state each is in today. The cycle's argument is what to do while these vehicles are missing, stalled or approaching.

Executive Synthesis

Transport Canada is being asked to move several emerging transportation technologies from pilot demonstration to standardised federal policy at the same moment its enabling legislative vehicles have either expired, been suspended or are mid-passage. The Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (Bill C-27) died at dissolution in 2025 (Osler, January 2026); the Electric Vehicle Availability Standard's 2026 model-year target was suspended in September 2025 amid an EV sales collapse from 14.5% to 8% year-on-year (Electric Autonomy Canada, September 2025); Bill C-8, the revived cyber-security reform Bill that would extend to federally regulated transport operators, was reintroduced in June 2025 and is still in passage (BLG, July 2025); and Ontario's Automated Commercial Motor Vehicle Pilot opened commercial driverless trucking testing in 2025 (Government of Ontario, August 2025) without a corresponding federal type-approval framework for L4 commercial deployment.

The cycle's spine is the autonomous-vehicles cluster because that is where the pilot-to-policy gap is most operationally consequential, most visible and most directly addressed by this report's audience. The other four clusters apply the same logic to adjacent domains: the supply-chain cluster against the 1 July 2026 USMCA Joint Review trigger (ALS International, October 2025); smart mobility around the recalibrating ZEV mandate and the standards-versus-pilot pivot; Arctic and Northern infrastructure around the Oceans Protection Plan funding cliff in 2026-27 and the largest-ever Canadian Arctic patrol in February 2026 (National Defence, February 2026); and the governance-innovation cluster bundling anticipatory regulation, the post-AIDA AI-governance gap and foresight as the policy advisor's own tool kit.

Five forces dominate attention: pilot-to-commercial movement in AV and smart mobility; the 1 July 2026 USMCA trigger; the legislative-vehicle gap (AIDA dead, ZEV mandate suspended, Bill C-8 in transit); the Arctic operational pivot; and a renewal cliff across OPP, the Connected and Automated Vehicles initiative funding, and the National Trade Corridors Fund execution rate (Macdonald-Laurier reports only around 20% of the $3.8bn approved 2017-2023 was actually spent (Macdonald-Laurier Institute, November 2025)).

Innovation policy at Transport Canada now operates more through sandbox, voluntary code and Treasury Board directive than through statute.

The insight that may surprise leadership is the directional one: innovation policy at Transport Canada now operates more through sandbox, voluntary code and Treasury Board directive than through statute. The Government of Canada AI Strategy 2025-2027 (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, February 2026), the Treasury Board Directive on Automated Decision-Making (Treasury Board, June 2025) and the Voluntary Code of Conduct on Generative AI together form a de facto governance framework that has filled the AIDA vacuum without a Bill, while the OECD's recent Regulatory Sandbox Toolkit (OECD, July 2025) maps the methodology the Strategic Policy and Innovation Branch is now operationalising. Whether this directive-and-sandbox layer is adequate for AV commercial deployment, supply-chain digital integration and Arctic operational doctrine is the policy-advisor question this cycle.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The central assumption is that the legislative-vehicle gap (AIDA dead, no AV anchor Bill, Bill C-8 still in committee) will persist through FY27 and force innovation policy to operate ex-statute. If Bill C-8 reaches Royal Assent with strong CAV and V2X coverage AND the government tables an AIDA replacement before the next Throne Speech, the governance-gap framing weakens materially and Transport Canada enters a phase of legislative implementation rather than directive-led work. The evidence to watch is the Senate Standing Committee on Transport and Communications timeline for Bill C-8 and any AI Bill tabled in the House of Commons during the autumn 2026 sitting.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Brief upward on the AV regulatory-fragmentation gap and propose a federal anchor instrument. Commercial AV trucking deployment in Ontario without federal L4 type-approval clarity is the single highest-priority regulatory gap in the advisor's portfolio.
  2. Build a TC USMCA Joint Review evidence pack framing supply-chain digitalisation as a tariff-friction mitigant. The 1 July 2026 trigger is five weeks away and the digital tools are TC's strongest available lever.
  3. Set the Branch position on foresight as institutional capability before the next federal foresight cycle. Policy Horizons Canada's last "Disruptions on the Horizon" was June 2024; the foresight slot is open for whichever department occupies it.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

1. Advancing Canada's Autonomous Land Vehicles: AI, Innovation & Security

Immediate

Canadian commercial autonomous-vehicle deployment is moving faster than the federal regulatory anchor that should govern it. Transport Canada's Connected and Automated Vehicles Safety Framework 2.0 provides oversight architecture but the underlying CAV initiative funding is sunsetting from 2025-26 to 2027-28; Ontario's Automated Commercial Motor Vehicle Pilot Program (2025) opens a driverless heavy-trucking deployment frontier on a fragmented federal-provincial jurisdictional base; the Artificial Intelligence and Data Act (Bill C-27) died at dissolution in 2025, leaving no horizontal AI governance statute to back the AV cyber and algorithmic-accountability frames; and Bill C-8, the revived cyber-security reform, would reach V2X and connected-vehicle infrastructure if enacted. The cluster forces the Branch to choose whether to lead with a federal anchor instrument or accept provincial leadership for the next two to three years.

Figure 1. The AV regulatory stack: where the federal lever sits today

Compiled from Transport Canada CAV Framework 2.0 (February 2025), Government of Ontario (August 2025) and Osler (January 2026).

  • Transport Canada's CAV Framework 2.0 updates the 2019 safety baseline but the Connected and Automated Vehicles initiative funding is sunsetting from 2025-26 through 2027-28, with no announced replacement (Transport Canada, February 2025).
  • Ontario's Automated Commercial Motor Vehicle Pilot Program opens driverless heavy-truck testing on public roads with a 10-year regulatory horizon to 2035, ahead of any federal L4 commercial deployment framework (Government of Ontario, August 2025).
  • Osler's January 2026 survey of the Canadian AV regulatory landscape finds federal type approval functions but no enacted L4 or L5 deployment regime, no general AI governance statute after AIDA's death, and substantial provincial variation across Ontario, Quebec, British Columbia, Manitoba and Nova Scotia (Osler, January 2026).
  • The October 2025 PowerCo Volkswagen plant construction start in St. Thomas, Ontario operationalises the federal-provincial battery and EV supply-chain industrial-policy bet, with implications for AV supply-chain assumptions (Electrive, October 2025).

Counter-argument

Victoria Transport Policy Institute's recurring AV implementation analysis cautions that full L4 and L5 commercial deployment is consistently overhyped on five- to ten-year timelines, with adoption curves repeatedly extending; on this reading the urgency of a federal anchor instrument is overstated and the existing provincial-pilot patchwork is appropriately calibrated to a slower deployment pace than industry advocacy suggests (Victoria Transport Policy Institute, April 2026). The counter-position implies the federal lever is the right one only when commercial deployment crosses a measurable threshold of share-of-fleet, not preemptively.

2. Supply-Chain Digitalisation and the USMCA Stress Test

Immediate

The USMCA Joint Review triggers on 1 July 2026, five weeks from this cycle date. Trucks and rail carry the great majority of Canada-US freight by value; tighter rules of origin on autos, steel and critical minerals will compound at the border unless digital trade and visibility instruments are operating at scale. The Trade Diversification Corridors Fund launched in March 2026 adds capacity, but Macdonald-Laurier's analysis of the National Trade Corridors Fund execution rate (around 20% of the $3.8bn approved between 2017 and 2023 actually spent) points to an internal delivery-capacity constraint that will compound the external trade shock. The Branch's task is to frame supply-chain digitalisation as the strongest available short-term tariff-friction mitigant in the USMCA evidence pack.

  • The USMCA Joint Review triggers 1 July 2026; failure of any party to confirm extension initiates annual reviews until potential expiry in 2036, with tighter rules of origin for autos, steel and critical minerals likely to increase border compliance costs across the corridors TC funds (ALS International, October 2025).
  • Transport Canada's Pre-Load Air Cargo Targeting programme (regulations in force 1 April 2025) uses AI to flag high-risk cargo before loading, illustrating both the opportunity in supply-chain digitalisation and the attack-surface and governance work it generates (Transport Canada, September 2025).
  • The $5bn Trade Diversification Corridors Fund launched 4 March 2026 is the principal new federal lever for non-US trade-corridor capacity, layered over the existing National Trade Corridors Fund (Transport Canada, March 2026).
  • Brookings' USMCA Forward 2026 analysis frames the Joint Review as the moment Canada and Mexico must demonstrate trade-bloc value to a US administration with weaker bilateral commitment, with logistics digitalisation and rules-of-origin compliance as the practical evidence (Brookings Institution, March 2026).

Counter-argument

Macdonald-Laurier Institute and Tim Sargent argue the binding constraint on Canadian transport is not digitalisation or external trade shocks but federal delivery capacity: TC portfolio spending rose 66.6% to $5.1bn while only around 20% of the $3.8bn National Trade Corridors Fund (2017-2023) was actually spent (Macdonald-Laurier Institute, November 2025). On this reading, the USMCA digitalisation frame is necessary but insufficient: Trade Diversification Corridors Fund money may follow the same underspend pattern unless the underlying approval and performance-tracking machinery is reformed first.

3. Smart Mobility: From Demonstration to Standardisation

Near-Term

The federal ZEV mandate's first compliance year was suspended in September 2025 after EV sales fell from 14.5% of total sales in 2024 to 8% in the first eight months of 2025, with C.D. Howe arguing the 2026 target would have required around 380,000 ZEV sales against a projected 270,000. The retreat creates room for non-mandate levers (charging infrastructure, vehicle-to-grid pilots, MaaS data exchange, transit data standards) where the Branch can lead. The strategic question is which smart-mobility pilots are now ready for federal standardisation and which still need to run pilot-mode before the policy instrument lands.

  • The federal government suspended the Electric Vehicle Availability Standard's 2026 model-year target on 5 September 2025, citing the collapse of ZEV sales from 14.5% to 8% year-on-year following the expiry of federal and provincial purchase incentives (Electric Autonomy Canada, September 2025).
  • C.D. Howe's working paper concludes the 2026 mandate would have required around 380,000 ZEV sales against a projected 270,000, a 110,000-unit shortfall, and recommends substantially revising future-year targets to align with market realities and tariff pressures (C.D. Howe Institute, November 2025).
  • Natural Resources Canada announced a $97m EV charging infrastructure injection in February 2026 under the Zero Emission Vehicle Infrastructure Program, signalling the charging-network lever continues to operate even as the mandate is recalibrated (Natural Resources Canada, February 2026).
  • Electric Autonomy Canada's April 2026 review of Canada's EV charging network reports continuing growth in public charge points but accelerating concentration in southern Ontario and the lower mainland, with the rural and northern coverage gap widening (Electric Autonomy Canada, April 2026).

Counter-argument

Pembina Institute argues the mandate retreat is premature and is itself eroding investor confidence in the charging network, the auto manufacturing transition and the demand-side incentive case; on this reading the federal retreat is causing the very market weakness it was meant to respond to, and the smart-mobility cluster should be reframed as a defence of the mandate rather than as movement toward post-mandate standards (Pembina Institute, May 2026). The Branch position needs to be explicit on which reading it adopts.

4. Arctic and Northern Infrastructure: Sovereignty, Security and the OPP Cliff

Near-Term

Canada recorded 13 complete Northwest Passage transits in 2025, the operational reality of climate-driven sea-ice retreat. Operation Nanook-Nunalivut 2026 deployed 1,300 Canadian Armed Forces personnel with US, Belgian, French and Danish partners in the largest Arctic patrol in Canadian history. At the same moment, the Oceans Protection Plan funding envelope sunsets in 2026-27 with no renewal confirmed, NORDREG was designed for a pre-commercial-transit era, and the Arctic Emission Control Area enters force in March 2026. The Branch is being asked to translate Arctic research and sovereign-presence work into operational federal regulatory doctrine that will hold for the next decade.

  • Operation Nanook-Nunalivut 2026 deployed 1,300 Canadian Armed Forces members (with US, Belgian, French and Danish partners) in the largest Arctic patrol in Canadian history, against a backdrop of US-Canada disagreement on the legal status of the Northwest Passage (National Defence, February 2026).
  • Transport Canada's 2025-26 Oceans Protection Plan Horizontal Initiative is funded at $180m in main estimates from the 2022 $3.5bn renewal, with most OPP and Program to Protect Canada's Coastlines and Waterways funding sunsetting in 2026-27 and no renewal decision publicly confirmed (Transport Canada, 2025-26 fiscal year).
  • The Canadian Arctic Emission Control Area entered force on 2 March 2026, requiring low-sulfur fuel or equivalent technology across Canadian Arctic waters and adding a new operational compliance dimension to growing commercial transit volumes (High North News, March 2026).
  • The US-Canada sovereignty dispute over the Northwest Passage (international strait vs internal waters) is escalating as commercial transits grow, with both sides positioning their respective doctrines for the post-ice era (Open Canada / CIC, December 2025).

Weak signals

  • Weak signal A latent marine-safety crisis under the OPP funding cliff: the most operationally critical OPP capabilities (oil-spill response, National Aerial Surveillance Programme, Indigenous-led coastal monitoring) sunset in 2026-27 with no renewal announced. Would gain weight on a major spill or sovereign-presence incident before renewal.
  • Weak signal Northwest Passage transit growth is not yet linear; 2025's 13 transits may plateau if ice conditions, insurance costs or vessel availability constrain the next two seasons. Would gain weight on the 2026 transit count being above or below the 2025 figure.

Counter-argument

Arctic shipping growth is often projected as exponential but the actual transit data is lumpy; insurance, Polar Code compliance, ice variability and crew availability create a higher operating floor than headline transit counts suggest, and a single bad ice season can suppress two years of commercial volume (Aker Arctic, 2025). On this reading, the regulatory-redesign urgency is real but the operational urgency is overstated; the Branch can prioritise the OPP-renewal evidence pack and let NORDREG modernisation follow.

5. Governance Innovation and Anticipatory Regulation: Foresight under the AIDA Gap

Near-Term

The Strategic Policy and Innovation Branch's own tool kit is the cluster. The Artificial Intelligence and Data Act died at dissolution in 2025, leaving Canada without a horizontal AI statute; the Treasury Board Directive on Automated Decision-Making, the Voluntary Code of Conduct on Generative AI and sectoral programmes (PACT, Bill C-8 cyber) have filled the vacuum at the directive layer. The OECD's Regulatory Sandbox Toolkit (July 2025) and Regulatory Policy Outlook 2025 map the anticipatory-regulation methodology the Branch is operationalising. The foresight function (Policy Horizons Canada) has not published a new Disruptions on the Horizon report since June 2024, leaving the federal long-range futures slot open. The cluster asks whether Transport Canada's innovation policy should anchor itself in directive-and-sandbox practice or push for a return to statute-based governance.

  • The Government of Canada AI Strategy 2025-2027, released February 2026, sets the cross-departmental direction for federal AI use and governance in the absence of an enacted AI Act (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, February 2026).
  • The Treasury Board Directive on Automated Decision-Making and Algorithmic Impact Assessment (June 2025 update) is the operational baseline for AI governance in federal departments, including Transport Canada's PACT and emerging AV-related AI uses (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat, June 2025).
  • The OECD's Regulatory Sandbox Toolkit and Regulatory Policy Outlook 2025 codify anticipatory regulation as a mainstream methodology for technology governance, positioning sandboxes as a route to standards rather than a substitute for them (OECD, July 2025; OECD, April 2025).
  • MLT Aikins' March 2026 review of Canadian AI governance argues a federal AI Act remains necessary and that the directive-and-voluntary-code layer cannot indefinitely substitute for statute, particularly for sectoral uses such as transport AI (MLT Aikins, March 2026).
  • Policy Horizons Canada's Disruptions on the Horizon (June 2024) remains the federal foresight community's most-cited frame, with no successor published in the intervening eighteen months, leaving the long-range federal futures slot open for whichever department occupies it (Policy Horizons Canada, June 2024).

Counter-argument

The Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives argues that Canada in 2026 still has no meaningful AI regulation and that the directive-and-voluntary-code stack is fundamentally unsuited to high-stakes uses including transport AI; on this reading, the Branch's role should be to push for a horizontal AI Bill rather than to build the sandbox layer further (Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives, February 2026). The implication is that anticipatory regulation can be a useful supplement to statute but is not a substitute for one, and the Branch needs to be honest about which lever it is choosing.

Scenario Matrix

Two uncertainties dominate TC's next 2-5 years for the Strategic Policy and Innovation Branch: whether the federal legislative pipeline produces an AV anchor and an AIDA replacement within FY27 (statute-led vs sandbox-led governance) and whether the 1 July 2026 USMCA Joint Review delivers a clean extension or triggers annual reviews (trade stable vs trade friction). The four scenarios below are planning aids, not forecasts.

Settled Pivot

A federal AV anchor instrument lands, an AIDA replacement reaches Royal Assent and the USMCA Joint Review confirms extension. TC pivots from policy-design mode to implementation mode: Branch capacity rotates toward standards work, regulatory amendments and federal-provincial coordination protocols. Innovation policy becomes execution policy. The directive-and-sandbox layer recedes into a complement rather than a substitute for statute, and the foresight function consolidates around implementation rather than horizon-scanning. The risk in this scenario is internal: TC may struggle to retool quickly from a policy-design culture to an implementation culture, and the Branch's distinctive expertise becomes less central.

Early indicators: Bill C-8 receives Royal Assent before end-2026; an AI Act tabled in the House of Commons during the autumn 2026 sitting; USMCA Joint Review concludes with formal extension without annual-review trigger.

Mandate vs Friction

The federal legislative pipeline lands the major Bills but the USMCA Joint Review triggers annual reviews or unwinds partial provisions. TC implements innovation policy under sustained trade-policy stress: AV deployment continues on the new federal framework but with constrained cross-border integration; supply-chain digitalisation becomes the central capability (the principal lever Canada has left); and the Trade Diversification Corridors Fund acquires real-time political salience. The Branch's policy work is well-equipped institutionally but its operating context is harder than expected. Most decisions get made on a faster cycle than the directive-and-sandbox era allowed.

Early indicators: USMCA Joint Review concludes with annual-review trigger; auto-sector tariffs reimposed on Canadian exports; CBSA processing times deteriorate at land borders in second half of 2026.

The Sandbox Era

No major federal Bill is enacted but USMCA extends. TC continues to operate through Treasury Board directives, voluntary codes and regulatory sandboxes; the OECD Sandbox Toolkit becomes a routine reference; the Branch's portfolio expands but operates ex-statute. Policy Horizons Canada or a successor function recovers its publishing cadence and foresight becomes an established Branch capability. The risk over time is legitimacy debt: directive-led governance for high-stakes sectoral AI uses (V2X, PACT, AV cyber) accumulates challenges in the courts and in Parliament, and the absence of statutory cover becomes a recurring vulnerability.

Early indicators: No AI Bill tabled in 2026; Bill C-8 stalls in Senate committee; OECD Sandbox Toolkit referenced in three or more federal Branch work plans by end-2026.

Improvised Resilience

Both axes adverse: the federal legislative pipeline produces no major Bill and USMCA Joint Review triggers annual reviews. TC operates without horizontal legislative tools while absorbing tariff and rules-of-origin friction. The Branch is asked to be the institutional shock absorber on multiple fronts at once: AV without a federal anchor, AI without an Act, USMCA without extension, OPP without renewal. This is the scenario in which the Branch's foresight and anticipatory-regulation tool kit is most needed and most exposed; the policy-design discipline becomes critical capability rather than discretionary expertise.

Early indicators: Federal Throne Speech autumn 2026 omits AI legislation; USMCA Joint Review triggers annual reviews and one or more goods-sector tariff disputes; OPP renewal envelope unannounced by end-2026.

What We Are Not Planning For

A serious AV incident shutting the Ontario ACMV pilot within FY27

Driverless commercial trucking pilots elsewhere (Texas, Arizona) have absorbed isolated incidents without programme suspension, and Ontario's pilot is structured to permit continued operation under regulatory adjustment. A high-profile incident is possible but a pilot-pause that removes the federal-provincial anchor question from the cycle is not the central case. The Branch should be ready with a federal anchor instrument options paper regardless.

Reinstate if: a serious Class A incident is reported under the Ontario ACMV programme, or Ontario announces a programme review or suspension.

A formal USMCA withdrawal within FY27

The Joint Review process is more likely to deliver an extension or annual-review trigger than a formal withdrawal in 2026 or 2027. The political costs of withdrawal for any of the three signatories remain substantial, and the trilateral architecture provides ample friction-management options short of dissolution. The Branch should focus on the annual-review and rules-of-origin friction scenarios rather than the withdrawal scenario.

Reinstate if: any signatory issues a formal six-month withdrawal notice under USMCA Article 34.6.

A horizontal AI Act enacted in 2026

An AIDA replacement reaching Royal Assent during this cycle would require Bill tabling in the autumn 2026 sitting, committee passage and Royal Assent within months. The federal legislative calendar, the political contention around the previous Bill C-27 and the absence of a tabled replacement as of cycle date all suggest this is unlikely. The Branch should monitor (per SI 4) but should not plan FY27 work around horizontal AI legislation arriving.

Reinstate if: an AI Bill is tabled in the House of Commons in the autumn 2026 sitting or earlier.
