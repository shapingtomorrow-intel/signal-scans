Executive Synthesis

UK transport enters the 2030-2040 window facing three simultaneous transitions rather than one. The decarbonisation transition is the most cited but it is no longer the only one with binding dates: the Automated Vehicles Act 2024 opened robotaxi pilots from spring 2026 under the Automated Passenger Services permit regime, with full Act implementation planned for the second half of 2027, per the DfT Ministerial Statement of 10 June 2025. The CAA's CAP 3169 Delivery Model (September 2025) targets first commercial passenger eVTOL flights by end-2028. The SAF Mandate has been in force since 1 January 2025 with a 2025 to 2040 trajectory rising from 2% to 22% per Edie. The UK is committed to phasing out new non-zero-emission HGVs under 26 tonnes by 2035 and all HGVs by 2040 per Greenly's policy summary. The decisions that shape the 2030s are landing in 2026 and 2027.

The spine of this cycle is the convergence: by 2030, AV fleets, eVTOL services, hydrogen HGVs, the first solid-state EV batteries, MaaS-orchestrated multi-modal journeys and a transformed active-travel network will all be moving from policy ambition into deployment, simultaneously. DfT's planning challenge is not which transition to prioritise; it is sequencing infrastructure, regulation and capital across all six in a way that compounds rather than competes. The Faraday Institution's call for the equivalent of six 20-GWh gigafactories by 2030 captures the scale of the parallel demand on industrial policy alone per the Faraday Institution Gigafactory Report 2024.

What has materially landed in the months leading up to this cycle: Wayve's $1.5B Series D at $8.6B post-money valuation in February 2026 makes the UK an AV-stack contender on the global capital map; the CAA's CAP 3169 (September 2025) dated the eVTOL launch window; the DfT "Better Connected" strategy of 2 April 2026 unlocked tap-and-go expansion across English public transport, beginning to convert MaaS from policy paper into commuter reality; AESC's Sunderland gigafactory commenced operations in December 2025 per Electrive, the UK's first major operational gigafactory; and the multi-pathway-vs-pick-winners debate first crystallised by the Transport Committee's 2023 Fuelling the Future report remains live going into the 2026 spending review.

The non-obvious read: the three transitions interact in ways DfT's siloed delivery structures may not handle well. AV regulation needs V2X infrastructure that overlaps with MaaS data platforms; hydrogen HGV refuelling networks share supply-chain economics with hydrogen-electric short-haul aviation; gigafactory siting affects both passenger EV affordability and the unit economics of e-bike, e-scooter and small-vehicle micromobility. The 2030-2040 decade rewards integration across the modal silos DfT has historically managed separately, and exposes departments that cannot coordinate. The Innovation, Industrial Strategy and Skills lens (Snapshot 4) is the one most at risk of being treated as residual rather than central.

The decisions that shape the 2030s are landing in 2026 and 2027.

The implications also expose a posture choice. The Transport Committee's 2023 pick-winners challenge — still unresolved going into the 2026 spending review — is essentially a question about whether the UK backs a single decarbonisation pathway per mode (hydrogen for rail, SAF for aviation, battery for road) or maintains multi-pathway optionality. The first reduces total-cost risk and concentrates supply chains; the second preserves resilience but spreads industrial investment thin. The 2026 spending review window is the right place to make that call deliberately rather than letting it emerge by default.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The synthesis rests on the assumption that the 2026 to 2028 implementation windows for AV regulation, eVTOL commercial launch and the SAF Mandate ramp all hold roughly to their stated calendars. If two or more slip by 18 months, the convergence narrative weakens and the report's integration argument becomes premature. A material revision would be triggered by AV Act full implementation slipping past mid-2028, by CAA eVTOL commercial flights slipping past 2030, or by the SAF Mandate's 10% 2030 trigger being relaxed in the next mandate review. None is the central case, but each is plausible and would shift the report's posture from coordination-now to coordination-when.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Decide which decarbonisation pathways are backed and which are deprioritised. The Transport Committee's pick-winners challenge needs a deliberate answer at the 2026 spending review, not a tacit one.
  2. Prepare the cross-modal infrastructure stack now. V2X, hydrogen refuelling, vertiports, charging and the tap-and-go payment layer are easier to build aligned than retrofitted.
  3. Decide the DfT-as-data-steward posture. The MaaS and AV transitions create a national mobility-data platform by 2030 whether DfT designs it or not.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Leadership Snapshots

Four lenses on the same intelligence base, one per leadership function. Each card surfaces the one question this cycle puts to that function.

Policy & Regulation

What is the regulatory build-order across AV, eVTOL, hydrogen and MaaS?

The shift: Four major regulatory frameworks need delivery in the same 2026 to 2028 window: AV Act secondary regulations, CAA eVTOL ops, hydrogen safety and refuelling, MaaS data standards.

The question to brief: Which two of the four get prioritised under the 2026 spending review's parliamentary-time allocation, and which two get a credible 2027 second-tranche commitment?

Infrastructure & Investment

Where does capital land first across vertiports, charging, V2X and hydrogen?

The shift: Six 20-GWh gigafactories, hydrogen refuelling corridors, vertiport siting, V2X roadside units and the national charging network are all 2026 to 2030 capital-allocation decisions; the cumulative envelope is multi-tens of billions.

The question to brief: Where is the geographic clustering thesis that lets V2X, charging, hydrogen and vertiport investment compound on shared corridors?

Decarbonisation & Net Zero

Does the multi-pathway approach survive the 2026 spending review?

The shift: The Transport Committee's 2023 Fuelling the Future report called on government to pick winners on low-carbon fuels for aviation and rail; that challenge remains unresolved going into the 2026 spending review, and multi-pathway optionality has real costs as well as benefits.

The question to brief: What evidence base would support a pathway-narrowing decision per mode, and what would be lost if the UK retains optionality longer?

Innovation & Industrial Strategy

Does the UK keep AV, eVTOL, battery and SAF supply chains, or import them?

The shift: Wayve at $8.6bn is a UK-headquartered global AV-stack contender; Vertical Aerospace is on a 2028 certification path; UK gigafactory capacity is roughly one-sixth of 2030 need; SAF plant pipeline is light against the 2030 mandate.

The question to brief: Which two of the four supply chains is the UK structurally capable of leading on, and what does each require from industrial strategy in the next 18 months?

Signal Clusters

The cycle's signals are organised into six clusters, ranked by impact on DfT's near-term decisions. Immediate: changes the 2026 spending review, the corporate structure or the proposition. Near-Term: changes DfT's competitive position over the next twelve months. Longer-Range: a multi-year structural factor to track and revisit each cycle.

1. Autonomous and connected vehicles move from pilot to fleet

Immediate

The Automated Vehicles Act 2024 has converted UK AV regulation from prospective to operational. Robotaxi pilots launch in spring 2026 under the automated passenger services permit regime, with secondary regulations expected in Q1 2026; full Act implementation is planned for the second half of 2027. Wayve raised $1.5B in February 2026 at an $8.6bn post-money valuation, putting a UK-headquartered AV-stack vendor on the global capital map alongside Waymo and Tesla. For DfT, the cluster is Immediate because it forces decisions on V2X infrastructure investment, kerbside management, liability frameworks and the data flows that AV operators must share with regulators in time for fleet expansion in the early 2030s.

  • The Automated Vehicles Act 2024 establishes the Authorised Self-Driving Entity (ASDE) regime, with the ASDE carrying ongoing safety, financial standing and cooperation duties through vehicle lifetime.
  • Robotaxi pilots launch spring 2026 with final automated-passenger-services regulations expected in early 2026; full Act implementation second half of 2027 per the DfT Ministerial Statement of June 2025 and (Hogan Lovells, February 2026).
  • Uber and Wayve confirmed a London robotaxi trial in spring 2026; Waymo, Lyft-Baidu and Uber-Baidu also announced 2026 launches (Automotive World, July 2025).
  • Wayve raised $1.5B Series D on 25 February 2026, $8.6bn post-money valuation, with Microsoft, NVIDIA, Uber, Mercedes-Benz, Nissan and Stellantis participating per Wayve.
  • 5G V2X roadside units enable real-time traffic optimisation, vulnerable-road-user alerts and cooperative AV manoeuvres (UKTIN evergreen reference page); a December 2025 DfT call for evidence on "Developing the automated vehicle regulatory framework" closed 5 March 2026 (Herbert Smith Freehills Kramer, January 2026).

Counter-argument

Robotaxi pilot launches are not fleet operations. International experience (San Francisco, Phoenix, Beijing) shows that scaling from pilot to a meaningful share of urban journeys takes a decade and depends heavily on consumer trust, accident-narrative management and operator unit economics. A counter-reading is that UK AV deployment remains small-scale and bounded through 2030, with the structural shift arriving in the second half of the 2030s only. In that case the cluster is genuinely Near-Term rather than Immediate, and DfT's V2X build-out can pace itself accordingly.

2. Heavy ground transport decarbonisation: hydrogen, battery and the modal split

Immediate

The UK has committed to phasing out new non-zero-emission HGVs under 26 tonnes by 2035 and all HGVs by 2040, with rail decarbonisation by 2040 and the DfT's March 2025 Maritime Decarbonisation Strategy targeting 30% GHG cut by 2030 and 80% by 2040 (relative to 2008). The hydrogen production target is up to 10 GW of low-carbon capacity by 2030, at least 5 GW electrolytic. The cluster is Immediate because the modal split (which mode goes hydrogen, which goes battery, which goes synthetic fuels) needs deliberate setting at the 2026 spending review rather than left to default by procurement-time pragmatism. The Transport Committee made a structural "pick winners" challenge in its 2023 Fuelling the Future report; the question remains open.

UK heavy-transport decarbonisation: dates and modal split, 2025-2040

Source basis: UK Hydrogen Strategy 10 GW target by 2030; SAF Mandate Statutory Instrument (in force 2025); UK Transport Decarbonisation Plan 2035/2040 HGV milestones; UK Maritime Decarbonisation Strategy 2025.

  • UK is first country to commit to phasing out new non-zero-emission HGVs under 26t by 2035 and all HGVs by 2040; rail decarbonisation target 2040 (Greenly, updated June 2025).
  • UK hydrogen production target 10 GW low-carbon by 2030, at least 5 GW electrolytic, per the European Hydrogen Observatory (June 2025 country page summarising the December 2024 UK hydrogen strategy update).
  • The UK Maritime Decarbonisation Strategy (DfT, 25 March 2025) sets zero fuel-lifecycle GHG by 2050 with at least 30% reduction by 2030 and 80% by 2040 (vs 2008 baseline); the UK ETS is expanded to domestic maritime from 2026.
  • The Transport Committee's structural 2023 Fuelling the Future report called on government to abandon its "deliberately technology agnostic" stance and pick winners on low-carbon fuels per mode — a challenge that remains unresolved going into the 2026 spending review (UK Parliament Transport Committee, March 2023).
  • The CCC's Progress in Reducing Emissions 2025 report to Parliament (July 2025) projects EV price parity 2026-2028 and EV deployment as the largest single contributor to road transport decarbonisation; POST's Decarbonising surface transport briefing (April 2025) sets out the multi-pathway cost and trade-off architecture.

Counter-argument

The multi-pathway approach is not necessarily wasteful. Different modes have genuinely different physics (hydrogen energy density favours rail and maritime; batteries favour LGV and fleet; SAF is the only feasible near-term aviation route). Picking winners prematurely risks locking in technology choices before solid-state batteries, alternative SAF feedstocks or hydrogen pipeline economics mature. A counter-reading is that DfT's right posture is principled optionality through 2030 with a 2030-2032 pathway-narrowing review point, rather than picking winners in 2026.

Decision link: Strategic Implications 2 and 4.

3. The future of flight: eVTOL, SAF, hydrogen-electric and the airspace question

Near-Term

UK aviation is the cluster where new-mode arrival and existing-mode decarbonisation converge most sharply. The CAA's CAP 3169 Delivery Model targets first commercial passenger eVTOL flights by end-2028, with legislative recommendations going to DfT in 2026. Vertical Aerospace's VX4 is on the same certification timeline. The SAF Mandate is in force and ramps to 10% by 2030 and 22% by 2040, with penalties of £4.70 per litre. The cluster is Near-Term rather than Immediate because the inflection lands 2028-2030 rather than 2026-2027, but the regulatory framework decisions DfT needs to take are this cycle's work, not next cycle's.

  • The CAA's CAP 3169 Delivery Model (September 2025) targets first commercial passenger eVTOL flights in the UK by end-2028 with legislative recommendations to DfT in 2026; the Advanced Air Mobility programme integrates with the Future of Flight portfolio per the CAA programme reference page (consultation on VTOL policy frameworks ran November 2025 to January 2026).
  • Vertical Aerospace VX4 certification target 2028 per Vertical Aerospace (September 2025), matching CAA timeline; trade-press confirmation (eVTOL Insights, September 2025).
  • SAF Mandate entered force on 1 January 2025 with trajectory 2% in 2025, 10% in 2030, 22% in 2040 per Edie and the official DfT SAF Mandate collection.
  • UK Jet Zero strategy targets net-zero domestic aviation by 2040 and at least five commercial SAF plants intended under construction by 2025, per the (DfT SAF Mandate consultation response, August 2024).
  • Ricardo's net-zero flight plan models the 2040 trajectory with combined SAF, hydrogen-electric and operational measures (Ricardo, April 2025); the Royal Society's structural anchor flags feedstock and land-use constraints (Royal Society, February 2023).

Counter-argument

The eVTOL commercial-flight target may slip materially. Cross-jurisdictional certification timelines have repeatedly slipped in the US (Joby, Archer, Wisk) and battery-energy-density limits constrain payload economics. Even if first commercial flights happen in 2028, regional eVTOL networks may not reach meaningful UK passenger share until the mid-2030s. SAF supply is the harder constraint: the UK plant pipeline appears light against the 2030 10% mandate, and the Royal Society's feedstock warning suggests scaling beyond 10% to 22% by 2040 requires resource decisions the UK has not yet taken. A counter-reading is that the cluster is structurally Near-Term and the realistic UK aviation reshape is post-2035.

4. Battery technology breakthroughs and the EV value chain

Near-Term

UK battery production capacity has just reached an inflection: AESC's Sunderland gigafactory commenced operations in December 2025 (initial 15.8 GWh, the first major operational UK gigafactory), and Volklec, backed by former Britishvolt investors, plans a £1bn / 10 GWh UK gigafactory via a tech-transfer agreement with China's Far East Battery, with initial production at UKBIC during 2025. Even so, the country sits at roughly one-sixth to one-quarter of the Faraday Institution's projected need by 2030: the equivalent of six 20-GWh gigafactories. Solid-state batteries are projected to take only 4% of the EV battery market by 2030 globally per the Faraday Institution, meaning the cluster's industrial-strategy implications are more about lithium-ion capacity in the late 2020s than solid-state in the early 2030s. For DfT, the cluster is Near-Term because gigafactory siting, charging-network build-out and the V2G/V2H regulatory question all feed forward into AV fleet economics and the active-travel micromobility electrification story.

Weak signals worth tracking

  • Weak signal The Volklec partnership with Chinese supplier Far East Battery (Power Technology, February 2025; SOCI, February 2025) suggests the UK gigafactory pipeline depends on inbound Asian capital, IP licensing and engineering talent. Gains weight if a second UK-Asian battery JV is announced in 2026 or if government adjusts foreign-investment screening for the sector.
  • Weak signal The Faraday Institution's projection that solid-state will reach only 4% of EV battery market by 2030 (Faraday Institution, September 2024) suggests the lithium-ion-to-solid-state transition is post-2035, not pre-2030. Gains weight if any Tier-1 OEM commits to majority solid-state by 2030.
  • Weak signal AESC's Sunderland gigafactory began operations in December 2025 (Electrive) at 15.8 GWh initial capacity; the Agratas Somerset 40 GWh plant remains under construction with backing from a £380m UK government grant. Gains weight if a second nameplate operational gigafactory announces FID by end-2026.
  • UK needs the equivalent of six 20-GWh gigafactories by 2030 and ten by 2040; solid-state projected at 4% of EV battery market and 7% of consumer electronics by 2030 per Faraday Institution Gigafactory Report 2024.
  • Volklec, backed by former Britishvolt investors, plans a £1bn UK gigafactory at 10 GWh through tech-transfer with China's Far East Battery, with initial production from UKBIC during 2025 (Power Technology, February 2025; SOCI, February 2025).
  • AESC's Sunderland gigafactory commenced operations in December 2025 at an initial 15.8 GWh capacity (Electrive) — the UK's first major operational gigafactory.

Counter-argument

The Faraday Institution's six-gigafactory target may overstate UK structural need. EV demand could be met by importing battery cells from European or Asian gigafactories at scale, with the UK retaining only assembly and module-pack steps. The counter-reading is that the strategic question is not how many UK gigafactories to build but whether the UK is content to be a battery-import economy with strong recycling and second-life capability, and a smaller domestic cell-manufacturing base than six 20-GWh facilities implies.

5. Active travel and micromobility transformation

Near-Term

UK active travel and micromobility sit in regulatory limbo at the start of this cycle. Private e-scooters remain illegal on UK roads; rental trials are extended to May 2028; a second national evaluation is due in 2026, and the E-Scooters (Review and Awareness) Bill was introduced in Parliament in February 2026. The Living Streets charity sector position is that strategic transport authorities (including the GLA) should be given regulatory powers over micromobility via the English Devolution Bill. The Near-Term framing reflects that the 2026 evaluation plus a likely 2027-28 legislative window will define the micromobility framework for the 2030s. For DfT, the under-weighted decision is how active travel and micromobility integrate with the AV-and-MaaS layer rather than being treated as a separate modal silo.

  • UK is not introducing rules legalising private e-scooters in 2026 despite growing usage; rental trials extended to May 2028; second national evaluation due 2026 (Smart Cities World, February 2026); legal-sector view of the likely 2027-28 regulatory clarification window and the February 2026 E-Scooters Bill (Lexology, March 2026).
  • Walking and micromobility policy reference from Living Streets (evergreen policy page) calls for integrated active-travel infrastructure tying micromobility into the broader walking and cycling network, and for strategic transport authorities to be given micromobility regulatory powers via the English Devolution Bill.
  • The Transport Committee's structural 2023 Fuelling the Future position on principled choice across motive-power options also bears on the micromobility framework: even at the bicycle / e-scooter end of the modal spectrum, the choice between rental and private regulatory routes is itself a "pick winners" decision.

Counter-argument

The micromobility transformation may not be transformative at all. Active travel has substantial behaviour-change ceilings (weather, distance, freight needs), and previous rounds of cycling-infrastructure investment have produced incremental rather than step-change modal share. A counter-reading is that DfT's right posture is steady incremental investment in active-travel infrastructure plus minimum-viable e-scooter regulation, without expecting micromobility to absorb material share of urban journeys by 2040. In that case the cluster is a Longer-Range cultural shift rather than a Near-Term infrastructure call.

6. Mobility-as-a-Service and AI-orchestrated demand-responsive transit

Longer-Range

MaaS, after a decade of policy interest and limited operational reality, is being unlocked by the contactless tap-and-go payment layer in 2026. The DfT's April 2026 "Better Connected" strategy, paired with £40m of local-trial funding for digital ticketing, integrates tap-and-go across English public transport modes and converts MaaS from policy paper into commuter experience for the first time. AI-orchestrated demand-responsive transit, dynamic routing and the Alan Turing Institute's energy-transport digital twin work create the analytical layer underneath. For DfT, the Longer-Range framing reflects that operational MaaS at scale (one app, all modes, all operators, all journeys) is a 2030-2035 build rather than a 2026-2028 one, but the design decisions that determine whether it works land in this cycle.

  • The DfT's "Better Connected" integrated transport strategy of 2 April 2026 makes contactless the preferred option for city-region integrated ticketing by 2030 and launches a £40m local-authority trial fund for digital ticketing.
  • Trade-press confirmation that the April 2026 strategy integrates tap-and-go across English public transport modes: single payment for journeys across operators (Travel and Tour World, April 2026).
  • The 2024 Transport Committee MaaS inquiry mapped UK deployment, integration challenges and policy levers per submissions (MAS0009 - WSP, January 2024) and (MAS0050, April 2024).
  • The Alan Turing Institute's dynamic digital twin programme (Turing-Accenture collaboration, evergreen project page) links energy and transport networks for AI-driven optimisation; the DfT's foundational MaaS implications paper (2018) remains the structural anchor.

Counter-argument

MaaS at national scale may never quite arrive in the form policy papers describe. The UK's transport-operator landscape is fragmented (bus deregulation outside London, rail franchising in flux, local authority transport powers patchy) and even a flawless payment layer does not solve the underlying scheduling and ticketing fragmentation. A counter-reading is that the 2026 to 2030 reality is much closer to a single payments layer across already-published timetables than to AI-orchestrated demand-responsive transit at scale, and DfT's posture should treat the latter as a 2035+ longer-range possibility rather than a 2030 plan.

Strategic Implications

Four decisions turn this cycle's signals into the 2026 spending review and the 2030-2040 trajectory. Each names the move, a horizon and a decision posture.

SI 1: Build the AV-eVTOL-V2X regulatory and infrastructure stack in coordinated tranches

The Policy & Regulation lead, with Infrastructure & Investment, should propose a single coordinated tranche programme by the end of 2026 covering: AV Act secondary regulations (Q1 2026 baseline), CAA eVTOL operational rules (CAA submission to DfT in 2026), V2X roadside-unit standards, kerbside management framework, vertiport siting principles and AV liability detail. The intent is to avoid the historic pattern of mode-by-mode regulation arriving incrementally and inconsistently. Decide rather than Prepare because the AV Act implementation calendar already requires several of these decisions before mid-2027.

Action: Director General Strategy proposes the coordinated tranche programme to the Permanent Secretary by 31 December 2026, with a 2027 to 2029 delivery calendar and a named lead per workstream.

Decide Draws on Cluster 1 (AV/CV) and Cluster 3 (Future of flight).

SI 2: Resolve the multi-pathway-vs-pick-winners decision at the 2026 spending review

The Decarbonisation & Net Zero lead, with Treasury counterparts, should bring a paper to the 2026 spending review setting out two options: (a) UK retains multi-pathway optionality through 2030 with a structured 2030-2032 narrowing review, or (b) UK picks per-mode winners now (hydrogen for rail and maritime, SAF for aviation, battery for road and LGV). The Transport Committee has already raised this publicly; deferring the answer concedes the framing to others. Prepare reflects the spending review timing rather than uncertainty about the need to choose.

Action: Director General Decarbonisation submits the pathway-choice option paper to the spending review by 30 September 2026, with the scenario costs, supply-chain implications and 2030-2032 review-trigger criteria for each option.

Prepare Draws on Cluster 2 (Heavy decarb) and Cluster 3 (Future of flight).

SI 3: Establish DfT's data-steward posture for the national mobility-data platform

The MaaS tap-and-go integration plus AV operator data-sharing requirements plus V2X roadside data flows together create a national mobility-data platform by 2030 whether DfT designs it or not. The Innovation lead, with Policy & Regulation, should propose a design principles paper by mid-2026 covering data governance, equity, accessibility, third-party access, AV operator obligations, and the relationship to the National Digital Twin Programme. The choice is between DfT actively shaping the platform or letting it emerge from operator-specific systems with incompatible standards.

Action: Chief Digital Officer publishes the National Mobility Data Platform design principles paper by 30 June 2026, with a 2027 to 2028 standards build-out plan.

Prepare Draws on Cluster 1 (AV/CV), Cluster 5 (Active travel) and Cluster 6 (MaaS).

SI 4: Track the UK industrial-strategy share-of-stack across AV, eVTOL, battery and SAF

The Innovation, Industrial Strategy & Skills lead should establish a quarterly dashboard from Q3 2026 tracking the UK's share-of-stack position across the four supply chains where the 2030-2040 economic-competitiveness story will be decided: AV software (Wayve trajectory and competitors), eVTOL OEM (Vertical Aerospace plus international entrants), battery cell capacity (Volklec plus successors, gigafactory pipeline), SAF production (plant pipeline against the 2030 mandate trigger). Monitor reflects that the responses (industrial-strategy interventions, foreign-investment screening, R&D allocation) are downstream of this read.

Action: Director Innovation publishes the quarterly share-of-stack dashboard from 30 September 2026, with the first half-yearly read presented to the Permanent Secretary by 31 March 2027.

Monitor Draws on Cluster 1 (AV/CV), Cluster 3 (Future of flight) and Cluster 4 (Battery tech).

Scenario Matrix

The two critical uncertainties for UK transport's 2030-2040 window are (i) whether the decarbonisation pathway choice converges on a single per-mode winner or remains multi-pathway, and (ii) whether AV, eVTOL and MaaS deployment is led by UK-headquartered firms or by inbound global platforms. The four scenarios below are planning aids, not forecasts.

UK-led convergence

Government picks per-mode decarbonisation winners at the 2026 spending review, anchoring industrial-strategy investment behind hydrogen for heavy transport, SAF for aviation and battery for road. Wayve, Vertical Aerospace and a UK gigafactory anchor capture the early 2030s deployment economics. DfT operates as integrated infrastructure planner across modes. The UK becomes a net exporter of AV stack and eVTOL OEM IP.

Early indicators: Pathway-narrowing decision at 2026 spending review; second UK gigafactory FID by 2027; Vertical Aerospace VX4 certification on schedule.

Multi-pathway resilience

UK retains multi-pathway optionality through 2030 with deliberate 2030-2032 review point. Infrastructure investment spreads thinner across hydrogen, battery, SAF and electric short-haul. The UK industrial base remains capable across all four but does not dominate any one. AV and eVTOL deployment proceeds at the pace of regulation rather than capital. Resilient against single-pathway technology shocks but slower to scale any one mode.

Early indicators: Spending review preserves multi-pathway funding; CAA eVTOL rules attract international entrants alongside UK; SAF Mandate trigger reviewed in 2030.

Platform-led restructuring

Inbound global platforms (Waymo, Joby, Asian gigafactory operators, US SAF majors) lead UK deployment across AV, eVTOL, battery and aviation fuels. UK becomes deployment-market for technology stack developed elsewhere; regulatory framework strong, industrial-strategy share-of-stack modest. DfT focus shifts to consumer-protection, data-governance and equity oversight of foreign-operated networks rather than UK-supply-chain stewardship.

Early indicators: Waymo or Baidu robotaxi share exceeds Wayve in 2027; Asian battery majors take majority of new UK gigafactory pipeline; Joby or Archer enters UK eVTOL market.

Delayed convergence

Two or more of the 2026-2028 windows slip materially: AV Act full implementation past mid-2028; eVTOL commercial flights past 2030; SAF Mandate 10% trigger relaxed. The convergence narrative weakens; DfT planning calendar resets to 2030-2032 inflection rather than 2028-2030. The 2030-2040 decade still sees the transition but compressed into a tighter 2032-2040 window, with sharper integration challenges and less margin for sequencing error.

Early indicators: AV Act secondary regulations slip past Q1 2026; Vertical Aerospace certification delayed; SAF plant pipeline below five-plant commitment by end-2026.

What We Are Not Planning For

Hyperloop or vacuum-tube transit reaching UK commercial deployment by 2040

The hyperloop and vacuum-tube transit category has experienced multiple major company failures and unit-economics challenges over the past five years. The 2030-2040 window is realistically too short for the technology to reach UK commercial deployment. The high-capacity transport need it would address is better met by next-generation rail (HS2 successor planning, Northern Powerhouse Rail) and by expanded short-haul aviation alternatives.

Reinstate if: a major sovereign-backed hyperloop programme reaches operational testing at intercity distances.

Full Level 5 private-car autonomy at UK consumer-vehicle scale by 2035

While robotaxi fleets reach meaningful share in the early 2030s under this report's central case, mass-market Level 5 private-car autonomy at consumer-vehicle scale (sold to individuals, not operated as fleets) remains a 2040s rather than 2030s technology. Consumer affordability, insurance pricing and infrastructure coverage all need to mature. Planning the 2030s on the assumption of mass private AV ownership is premature.

Reinstate if: a major OEM announces Level 5 private-car sale at sub-£40k price point with full UK road coverage.

Carbon-capture-equipped synthetic fuels displacing SAF and hydrogen as the primary aviation pathway by 2040

Power-to-liquid synthetic fuels with carbon capture remain the highest-cost pathway in the aviation decarbonisation stack and require renewable-electricity inputs that compete with electrification and hydrogen production. While they will play a niche role in the 2040s long-haul segment, they are unlikely to displace SAF and hydrogen-electric short-haul as the dominant pathways within this report's horizon.

Reinstate if: a major UK airline commits to power-to-liquid SAF as its primary 2040 fuel pathway, or if SAF feedstock constraints force the mandate to lean on PtL.

Discussion Points for the DfT Leadership Team

  1. If we have to pick winners on decarbonisation pathways at the 2026 spending review, the politically easy answer is one technology per mode. Are we comfortable with the resilience cost, or do we plan to preserve some multi-pathway optionality despite the Transport Committee pressure?
  2. Wayve's $8.6bn valuation makes the UK a credible AV-stack contender. What does industrial strategy need to look like to ensure that does not become a UK-headquartered firm with non-UK production and deployment dominance?
  3. The CAA's eVTOL ops recommendations land with us in 2026. Are we structurally ready to handle a new airspace and vertiport regulatory regime in parallel with the AV Act roll-out, or does sequencing need explicit Cabinet Office coordination?
  4. The mobility-data platform will emerge by 2030 whether we design it or not. Do we have the standards-setting capability and the institutional appetite to make it a national asset rather than a fragmented operator landscape?
  5.
