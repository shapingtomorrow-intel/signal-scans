Accumulation risk hardens while AI liability cover thins

Two movements dominate this cycle: data-centre value accumulation is becoming a named reinsurance recalibration problem, while carriers pull affirmative AI cover from casualty and management-liability lines faster than standalone products can replace it.

A Verisk briefing prepared for insurer and reinsurer clients: underwriters, exposure managers, actuaries and reinsurance buyers.

The consensus treats AI infrastructure as a growth story for insurers, and the premium pool is real: Swiss Re Institute projects data-centre-linked premiums rising to USD 24.2 billion by 2030 from USD 10.6 billion (Swiss Re Institute, March 2026). The momentum read is less comfortable. Single-site insured values of USD 10 billion to USD 30 billion are outrunning capacity, turning accumulation into a reinsurance recalibration question (The Insurer, May 2026), while carriers withdraw affirmative AI cover from casualty and management-liability lines (CSO Online, April 2026). The question: is the gap widening because the risk is new, or because the industry is repeating the silent-cyber sequence?

Pattern narrative

Physical-asset accumulation outrunning capacityEvolving Risk

Two signals converge: data-centre value accumulation and the catastrophe and geopolitical exposure of hyperscale sites. Single-site insurable values of USD 10 billion to USD 30 billion now exceed what individual carriers will hold, and Swiss Re frames the next inflection as reinsurance recalibration once mega-campuses are operational (The Insurer, May 2026). March 2026 drone strikes on AWS sites in the UAE and Bahrain also made hyperscale compute a kinetic target (World Economic Forum, April 2026). The binding constraint is measuring correlated value before a first major loss tests it.

AI liability cover thinning at both endsEvolving Risk

Two signals dominate: AI exclusions hardening in standard casualty and management-liability lines, and silent AI sitting unpriced inside legacy wordings. Carriers are declining affirmative cover for AI-generated outputs because the model's reasoning path is not traceable (CSO Online, April 2026), while Gallagher Re finds cyber, E&O, product and general liability wordings were not designed for AI failure modes (Gallagher Re, March 2026). On the available evidence, the gap is being created as much by carrier retreat as by novel risk.

The capacity and product responseTransformation Driver

Two signals describe the market's structural answer: dedicated lifecycle capacity for physical assets, and standalone AI-specific liability products. Aon's Data Center Lifecycle Insurance Program reached USD 3.5 billion in its second 2026 expansion in four months (Aon, April 2026), and Mosaic with Munich Re's aiSure introduced parametric-like AI-performance cover (The Insurer, February 2026). The Geneva Association finds more than 90% of businesses want AI-risk cover (Geneva Association, October 2025); the open inflection is whether capacity scales fast enough, on disciplined terms, to close the gap.

Signals gaining momentum

  1. Data-centre value accumulation. A reinsurer declining or repricing a named mega-campus renewal would turn recalibration into a hard capacity decision.
  2. AI exclusions hardening in standard lines. Wider default AI exclusions across casualty and management-liability forms turn the gap into a board-level shortfall.
  3. Silent AI in legacy policy wordings. A first claim litigated under a policy silent on AI would force pricing of unmeasured back-book risk.
  4. Standalone AI liability and parametric cover. A capacity commitment beyond pilot-scale lines would signal the market believes the risk is underwritable.

Wild Cards to Watch

Correlated foundation-model failure across portfolios

Disruptive Risk Potential impact: Very High

Surprise characteristics: A fault in one widely used foundation model triggers near-simultaneous claims across thousands of insureds and several lines.

Early warning indicators: Insured AI deployments concentrating on one model provider; cross-line claims clustering on one vendor.

Gallagher Re warns that faults in commonly adopted systems could generate correlated losses across sectors and geographies (Gallagher Re, March 2026), and EIOPA flags AI as an amplifier of systemic vulnerability (EIOPA, December 2025). On the available evidence the pathway is plausible but unrealised.

First contested mega-campus catastrophe claim

Disruptive Risk Potential impact: Very High

Surprise characteristics: A fire, catastrophe or kinetic event at a USD 10 billion-plus campus triggers a layered tower with inconsistent wordings and disputed claims.

Early warning indicators: Catastrophe events near clustered data-centre corridors; coverage-tower disputes on a smaller loss.

No major data-centre claim has yet been tested at scale (Data Center Dynamics, March 2026), and because large sites are often placed across separate programmes one event can hit several at once (Swiss Re Institute, March 2026). The first contested claim sets the precedent.

Implications

Three things warrant attention for those carrying AI-infrastructure risk. Accumulation visibility: where data-centre risk is placed across separate construction, equipment and power programmes, a single-event view across the full tower is worth building before the first major loss. Back-book silent AI: casualty and management-liability portfolios written before AI exclusions became standard carry unpriced exposure, and a wording sweep is a defensible step. Capacity discipline: with new products scaling fast, the question for reinsurers is whether to back growth on terms that hold if the wild cards materialise. The protection gap remains a pricing problem the market can shape.
