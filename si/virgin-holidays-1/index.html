Executive Synthesis

2 min read

The long-haul leisure package is being squeezed from the demand side and the cost side at the same time. UK consumers cut travel spending in March 2026 for the first time in five years, down 3.3% year on year, yet only 28% would cut holiday spending to cover day-to-day costs: the market is bifurcating, not collapsing. Meanwhile the UK SAF Mandate raises the long-haul cost base on a fixed schedule, AI is rewiring the booking journey, and the UK package market has consolidated around Jet2. The eight signal clusters below carry the underlying evidence; the four Strategic Implications carry the decisions; the four leadership snapshots tell each function which clusters apply most directly.

The long-haul leisure package is being squeezed from the demand side and the cost side at once. The strategic question is which segment Virgin Holidays defends, and with what.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Segment the customer base into premium-resilient and mass-market-squeezed (FY27 planning round). One proposition cannot serve both; the H2 2026 premium booking curve is the test of whether the bifurcation holds.
  2. Establish a presence in the AI discovery layer (end-Q3 2026). AI-native trip planning is now a measurable inbound channel; destination-first queries play to Virgin Holidays' strength only if the expertise is discoverable.
  3. Build the SAF Mandate ramp into the long-haul cost-base model (FY27 plan). The 2% to 10% to 22% schedule is a known input; pressure-test which parts of the proposition hold as per-trip cost rises faster than for short-haul competitors.
  4. Define what makes long-haul premium structurally defensible (FY27 strategy review). Jet2 made its mass-market position structural through deliberate moves; name Virgin Holidays' equivalent moves with owners.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The thesis assumes the premium long-haul traveller is structurally more resilient to the cost-of-living squeeze than the mass-market traveller. That is the consensus reading of the ABTA data, but the March 2026 spending cut was the first in five years; a second consecutive quarterly decline that reaches the premium segment would invalidate it. The thesis also assumes AI trip planning supplements rather than replaces the tour operator. If agentic booking platforms move from inspiration to transaction faster than expected, the disintermediation risk is larger than this report frames it.

1. The first UK travel-spending cut in five years

Mandate-flow

In March 2026, UK consumers cut travel spending for the first time in five years. It is the clearest signal yet that the cost-of-living squeeze has reached discretionary travel, and it lands directly on the FY27 plan. The question is whether it is a cyclical dip tied to a specific external shock or the start of a structural break.

  • UK consumers cut travel spending in March 2026 for the first time in five years, total travel purchases down 3.3% year on year as cost-of-living pressure intensified alongside global instability. Travel And Tour World (April 2026).
  • UK consumer travel spending rose 2.4% in 2025 to an average of £1,455 per person, outperforming overall and discretionary spend, so the March cut is a change of direction rather than a continuation. Barclays (April 2026).
  • Middle East instability redirected bookings toward Western Europe in early 2026, with Spain flight bookings up 32% year on year. Travel And Tour World (April 2026).

One quarter is not a trend, but it is the trigger to watch

A single quarterly decline coinciding with a specific external shock (Middle East instability, fuel price spikes) is not yet a structural break. But it is the first negative print in five years, and it is the metric that tells Virgin Holidays whether the FY27 plan is being built on a cyclical dip or a new baseline. The discriminating test is the H2 2026 spending data: a recovery confirms a blip, a second decline confirms a break.

Counter-argument

The March 2026 cut may be a clean cyclical artefact. Travel spending rose through 2025, holiday participation is near a decade high, and the dip coincides with an identifiable external shock rather than a behaviour change. If H2 2026 recovers, the cut was noise.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalIf the European booking redirect persists into the 2027 season, it is structural substitution away from long-haul, not a one-cycle event. Would gain weight if European short-haul holds its share gain.
  • Weak signalUK domestic holiday parks are reporting strong 2026 demand at affordable price points, evidence of active trading-down. Would gain weight if domestic share keeps rising through the summer season.
What this means. The H2 2026 travel-spending trajectory is the trigger metric for the whole cycle. Strategic Implication 1 ties the customer segmentation decision to this curve.

2. The bifurcation of UK outbound leisure demand

Mandate-flow

Beneath the headline spending cut, the UK outbound leisure market is splitting in two. A premium-resilient segment is still spending and still travelling; a mass-market-squeezed segment is trading down, booking later, and substituting toward European and domestic alternatives. Virgin Holidays cannot serve both with one proposition.

  • Only 28% of people would cut holiday spending to cover day-to-day costs, the lowest figure ABTA has recorded in recent years, and 87% took a holiday in the past 12 months, near the 2019 decade high. ABTA Holiday Habits 2025-26 (November 2025).
  • Destination ranks first as the priority booking driver for 45% of people (rising to 57% for over-65s); cost ranks second at 33%. ABTA (November 2025).
  • 2026 hotel and accommodation booking data shows shorter lead times and heightened value sensitivity in how UK consumers research and commit. UKHospitality (2026).

The 28% number is the whole strategy question

ABTA's finding that only 28% would cut holiday spending says the holiday is a defended purchase for roughly seven in ten UK households, prioritised over consumer goods and eating out. But defended does not mean unchanged: defended budgets get reallocated, traded down within, and booked later. The question for Virgin Holidays is not whether the customer still travels, but whether they still travel long-haul, or protect the holiday budget line by substituting a cheaper European or domestic trip.

Counter-argument

The bifurcation framing may overstate a temporary cyclical dip. If H2 2026 spending recovers across all segments evenly, the single-market model still works and segmenting the customer base is a solution to a problem that resolved itself.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalThe 25 to 34 cohort, identified by ABTA as travel trendsetters, may be the leading edge of a generational shift away from the traditional long-haul package. Would gain weight if Virgin Holidays' under-35 booking share falls below its 2025 baseline.
  • Weak signalIf average transaction values in the premium segment fall in step with the mass market through H2 2026, the bifurcation has not held. Would gain weight on the first quarter of converging premium and mass-market lead times.
What this means. Virgin Holidays needs separate pricing, lead-time and inventory strategies for the premium-resilient and mass-market-squeezed segments. Strategic Implication 1 commits the segmentation.

3. AI-native trip planning becomes a measurable channel

Positioning

The customer's first move in the holiday journey is shifting from a search engine or a brochure to an AI assistant. AI-native trip planning is no longer a demonstration; it is a measurable inbound channel showing up in source-of-traffic data. For a destination-led business, the question is whether Virgin Holidays' destination expertise is discoverable where the search now begins.

  • AI-native trip planning (Perplexity, Claude, ChatGPT) is now a measurable inbound channel, showing up consistently in source-of-traffic data for properties that track it. Digital Applied (2026).
  • March 2026 marked the point at which agentic travel booking moved from demonstration to a real, measurable channel. OAG (March 2026).
  • Twice as many UK travellers used AI for holiday inspiration this year as last year. ABTA Holiday Habits 2025-26 (November 2025).

Destination-first queries play to Virgin Holidays' strength, if it is discoverable

ABTA finds destination ranks first as a booking driver for 45% of UK travellers. AI trip planners answer destination-first queries by surfacing structured, authoritative destination content. Virgin Holidays' long-haul destination expertise is exactly the kind of content an AI planner wants to surface, but only if it is structured and discoverable. The risk is not that AI replaces the destination expert; it is that the AI surfaces a competitor's destination content because Virgin Holidays' is not in a form the planner can use.

Counter-argument

AI may not change the long-haul package journey much. Long-haul packages are high-consideration, high-value, financially protected purchases where ABTA finds people specifically value the ease, time-saving and protection of a travel professional. The disintermediation story may be real for simple flights and largely irrelevant for the complex protected package.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalCruise lines are the flagship agentic-AI use case because the booking is complex and multi-step, like a long-haul package. Would gain weight if a major UK tour operator announces an agentic booking deployment in 2026.
  • Weak signalIf AI platforms move from inspiration to transaction, the disintermediation risk steps up sharply. Would gain weight if ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini add confirmed travel-transaction capability with named partners.
What this means. Virgin Holidays needs a presence in the AI discovery layer before competitors lock the position. Strategic Implication 2 commits the pilot.

4. Supercharge or bypass: the intermediary question

Positioning

The travel industry is split on whether agentic AI disintermediates the intermediary or reinforces it. The two readings imply opposite strategies for Virgin Holidays, and the evidence does not yet settle which one wins. That is why this is a positioning question to prepare for, not a decision to make this cycle.

  • One school argues agentic AI will reinforce the centrality of intermediaries that already sit at the intersection of inventory, data and demand, because the agent needs somewhere to transact. PhocusWire (2026).
  • The opposing school argues agentic AI lets suppliers sell directly on GenAI platforms, cutting out the intermediary entirely. Hospitality Net (2026).
  • Skyscanner was a launch partner for OpenAI's Operator agent and has systematically embedded itself across AI platforms to ensure its data answers wherever a consumer asks about flights. PhocusWire (2026).

The two strategies are not the same, and Virgin Holidays cannot run both

If AI supercharges intermediaries, the move is to be the inventory-rich, data-rich, demand-aggregating layer the agent transacts through, leaning on the integrated Virgin Atlantic relationship. If AI bypasses intermediaries, the move is to be discoverable and bookable directly inside the AI platforms, as Skyscanner has done. The two strategies require different investments and different partnerships. The pilot in Strategic Implication 2 is designed to produce the evidence that resolves which way to commit.

Counter-argument

The supercharge-or-bypass debate may be a false binary. The likely outcome is a hybrid where AI changes the discovery layer but the transaction stays with whoever holds inventory, protection and trust. In that case Virgin Holidays' integrated model is naturally well-placed and the strategic anxiety is overdone.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalGoogle Cloud has shown agentic AI handling an estimated 40 to 60% of cruise booking inquiries that previously needed human agents. Would gain weight if that share is independently confirmed and extends to packages.
  • Weak signalIf a major airline or tour operator strikes a direct-sell deal with a GenAI platform, the bypass thesis gains ground fast. Would gain weight on the first named supplier-to-platform direct-sell agreement.
What this means. The pilot in Strategic Implication 2 produces the evidence to choose between the supercharge and bypass strategies. The presence cannot wait for that evidence; the commitment can.

5. The SAF Mandate ramp and the long-haul cost base

Positioning

Aviation decarbonisation has moved from a reputational topic to a structural cost-base topic. The UK SAF Mandate is law, ramping on a fixed schedule, and it raises the long-haul cost base faster than the short-haul cost base because long-haul burns more fuel per trip. The Revenue Certainty Mechanism caps the direct ticket pass-through, but not the underlying cost.

  • The UK SAF Mandate started in 2025 at 2% of total UK jet fuel demand and ramps linearly to 10% in 2030 and 22% in 2040; it is law, not aspiration. Department for Transport (updated October 2025).
  • SAF generally costs between 2 and 5 times more than fossil jet fuel today. ICCT (September 2025).
  • The Revenue Certainty Mechanism, taking effect by the end of 2026, is designed to limit the direct ticket-price pass-through to around £1.50 per ticket per year on average, funded by a levy on aviation fuel suppliers. edie (2025).

The £1.50 cap is real but it is not the whole cost story

The Revenue Certainty Mechanism limits the direct pass-through to roughly £1.50 per ticket per year. That is genuine. But it understates the long-haul picture in two ways. First, the £1.50 is an average across all UK aviation; a transatlantic or Caribbean flight burns far more fuel than a European hop, so the long-haul share of the SAF cost is disproportionate. Second, the mandate compounds: 2% in 2025 is modest, 10% by 2030 and 22% by 2040 is not, and the RCM caps the pass-through, not the underlying cost. For a long-haul-only leisure business the cost base rises on a known schedule.

Counter-argument

The cost pressure may be smaller than it looks. The RCM exists specifically to keep the pass-through modest, the £1.50 figure is a government estimate presented to Parliament, and 2% SAF in 2025 is marginal. If SAF production scales and the premium falls, the 2030 and 2040 ramp points may never bite as hard as a straight-line projection suggests.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalIf SAF production scales faster than expected and the 2-to-5x premium narrows, the long-haul cost penalty eases. Would gain weight if the ICCT or DfT revises the cost-premium range downward.
  • Weak signalConsumer flight shame remains weak as a revealed preference, but a shift from attitude to behaviour would add a demand pressure on top of the cost pressure. Would gain weight if ABTA records a measurable fall in long-haul intent attributed to environmental concern.
What this means. The SAF Mandate ramp is a known, scheduled input to the long-haul cost-base model. Strategic Implication 3 builds it into the FY27 plan.

6. The Jet2 ascent and UK package-holiday consolidation

Mandate-flow

The UK package-holiday market has consolidated around an integrated airline-plus-operator model that Virgin Holidays cannot out-scale. Jet2holidays is now the largest UK tour operator. The consolidation is settled fact, not a forecast, and it sets the competitive frame for the FY27 strategy.

  • Jet2holidays overtook TUI as the largest UK tour operator in February 2023 after expanding its operating licence, taking it to roughly 5.9 million versus TUI's roughly 5.3 million. City AM (February 2023, structural anchor).
  • Jet2 Holidays holds the largest value share of the UK package holiday market at around 20%, ahead of TUI. Statista (2025).
  • The largest companies in the UK tour operator industry are Jet2 plc, TUI Travel Ltd and Virgin Holidays Ltd, in that order. IBISWorld (2026).

The integrated model won mass-market: what wins long-haul premium

Jet2's ascent is a story about the integrated airline-plus-tour-operator model winning the UK mass-market short and mid-haul segment through scale, control of seat inventory and value positioning. Virgin Holidays has the same structural advantage, an integrated airline in Virgin Atlantic, but in a different segment: long-haul premium. The strategic risk is treating that position as a given rather than building it deliberately, as Jet2 built its mass-market position through fleet, licence expansion and relentless value consistency.

Counter-argument

The consolidation may not threaten Virgin Holidays at all. Jet2's strength is short and mid-haul mass-market; Virgin Holidays' core is long-haul premium. These are arguably different markets that share the package-holiday label, and if the segments do not converge the Jet2 ascent is a competitor's success in an adjacent market, not a structural threat.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalIf Jet2 extends its integrated model into long-haul, the competitive insulation Virgin Holidays enjoys narrows. Would gain weight if Jet2 announces long-haul leisure routes or a long-haul package range.
  • Weak signalTUI restructuring could free up mid-market long-haul share that either consolidates to Virgin Holidays or fragments to online players. Would gain weight if TUI announces a material long-haul UK capacity change.
What this means. The competitive frame is set: Virgin Holidays competes on long-haul premium, not scale. Strategic Implication 4 makes that position deliberate.

7. Virgin Holidays' long-haul premium position as a deliberate moat

Horizon

Virgin Holidays' defensible position is long-haul premium, and the integrated Virgin Atlantic relationship is the structural advantage that makes it defensible. The financial evidence shows the model works; the horizon question is whether the position is being built deliberately or treated as a given.

  • Virgin Atlantic Holidays reclaimed the top market share position in Florida holidays in 2024, carrying 255,000 holiday customers and posting £517 million in revenue, and operates as both a distribution channel and a demand stabiliser for the airline. Virgin Atlantic Holidays (2026).
  • Virgin Atlantic has pivoted toward profitable long-haul leisure segments, with capacity increases on India and Caribbean routes. Aviation Outlook (2026).
  • Virgin Atlantic's Winter 2026/27 network changes include route suspensions and new launches, evidence of active long-haul network rebalancing. UK Aviation News (2026).

The integrated relationship is the moat, if it is used as one

Virgin Holidays' long-haul premium position rests on a structural advantage a standalone tour operator does not have: seat-inventory access, network alignment with Virgin Atlantic, ancillary economics, and a direct customer relationship. The £517m revenue and reclaimed top Florida share show the model works. The deliberate moves that would make it a durable moat rather than an assumed position are network alignment, the Caribbean-Florida core, premium-cabin packaging, and the integrated ancillary economics, each with a named owner.

Counter-argument

The long-haul premium position may be naturally insulated and need no deliberate defence. If Jet2 stays in short and mid-haul and the segments do not converge, Virgin Holidays' position holds without a formal defensibility programme, and building one is effort spent on a moat that already exists.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalVirgin Atlantic's network rebalancing (capacity up on India and the Caribbean) is the operational frame Virgin Holidays plans product around. Would gain weight as a positive if the rebalancing concentrates further on Virgin Holidays' core leisure destinations.
  • Weak signalCross-competitor supply relationships are now normal (TUI Musement became Jet2's tours-and-activities partner). Would gain weight if Virgin Holidays' own supply chain becomes more exposed to competitor-owned intermediaries.
What this means. The long-haul premium moat needs deliberate construction, not assumption. Strategic Implication 4 names the moves and the owners.

8. Destination substitution: the European and domestic redirect

Horizon

The early-2026 redirect of UK bookings toward Western Europe and domestic alternatives may be a one-cycle response to a specific shock, or the leading edge of a structural substitution away from long-haul. For a long-haul-only business, telling the difference is a multi-year horizon question worth tracking from this cycle.

  • Demand is shifting, not falling: Middle East disruption redirected bookings toward Western Europe and flight-free holidays, with Spain flight bookings up 32% year on year. Travel And Tour World (April 2026).
  • UK domestic holiday parks are reporting strong 2026 demand and record availability at affordable price points. Travel And Tour World (2026).
  • Operator trends data points to UK staycation momentum and value-led booking through 2026. Away Resorts (2026).

Substitution toward Europe is the quietest long-haul threat

The cost-of-living squeeze does not have to remove the holiday to hurt Virgin Holidays. It only has to move the holiday from long-haul to short-haul. A household that protects its holiday budget line but swaps a Caribbean package for a Spanish one has not stopped travelling; it has stopped being a Virgin Holidays customer. The European and domestic redirect is therefore the most direct demand threat in the cycle, and the slowest to show up in headline numbers because total holiday participation stays high.

Counter-argument

The redirect is plausibly a clean response to a specific geopolitical shock that will unwind. If Middle East instability eases, the bookings that moved to Europe may move back, and the substitution was a one-cycle event, not a structural shift in long-haul preference.

Weak signals to watch

  • Weak signalIf European short-haul holds its 2026 share gain into the 2027 booking season, the substitution is structural. Would gain weight on a second consecutive season of elevated European short-haul share.
  • Weak signalIf Virgin Holidays' own repeat-customer cohort shows a measurable shift toward shorter trips or lower transaction values, the substitution has reached the core. Would gain weight on the first negative repeat-customer transaction-value print.
What this means. Destination substitution is the quietest demand threat and the one to track across cycles. It threads into Strategic Implication 1 as a segmentation input.

Scenario Matrix

Two axes, three-year horizon. X axis: the UK consumer squeeze, does the premium long-haul segment hold, or does the squeeze reach it. Y axis: AI disintermediation, does AI supplement the tour operator, or route around it. Virgin Holidays should be able to tell which cell it is in by the end of the 2027 booking season.

1. The Defended Core (premium holds, AI supplements)

The bifurcation holds, the premium long-haul customer keeps spending, and AI trip planning sends qualified inbound demand to operators that are discoverable and inventory-rich. Virgin Holidays' long-haul premium position is the right one; the work is to deepen the Caribbean-Florida core, win the AI discovery layer, and absorb the SAF cost ramp through value rather than price.

Early indicators: premium booking curve holds through H2 2026; AI-attributed inbound demand grows; premium average transaction values stable.

2. Squeezed and Bypassed (premium squeezed, AI routes around)

The most demanding scenario: the squeeze reaches the premium customer and AI routes around the operator at the same time. Virgin Holidays faces a contracting market and a loss of booking-journey control simultaneously. Survival depends on the integrated Virgin Atlantic relationship: seat-inventory access, network alignment, and the direct-customer economics a standalone operator does not have.

Early indicators: premium lead times shorten with the mass market; AI platforms add transaction capability; standalone operators consolidating or exiting.

3. The Broad Squeeze (premium squeezed, AI supplements)

The cost-of-living squeeze reaches the premium long-haul segment; a second and third quarterly spending decline confirm it. AI still supplements rather than bypasses. Virgin Holidays faces a contracting addressable market and has to compete on value in a segment it assumed was resilient. The work is rapid repositioning of the proposition and cost base before the FY27 plan locks.

Early indicators: premium average transaction values fall; trading-down reaches the long-haul customer; the European redirect holds.

4. The Defended Channel (premium holds, AI routes around)

The premium customer keeps spending, but AI platforms move from inspiration to transaction and route around the tour operator. Virgin Holidays still has the right customer but loses control of the booking journey. The work shifts to being bookable directly inside AI platforms and to defending the direct-relationship economics the integrated Virgin Atlantic model makes possible.

Early indicators: AI platforms add confirmed travel-transaction capability; direct-booking share falls; AI-intermediary commission pressure rises.

Opportunity Capture

Win the AI discovery layer before competitors lock the position

Skyscanner moved early to embed across AI platforms. Virgin Holidays' long-haul destination expertise is exactly what AI trip planners surface against destination-first queries, and destination ranks first as a booking driver for 45% of UK travellers. Being present and structured in the AI discovery layer in 2026 is a low-cost, high-leverage move while the position is still open.

Make the premium-resilient segment a deliberate target, not a residual

Roughly seven in ten UK households still defend the holiday line on the budget. The premium long-haul customer within that group is Virgin Holidays' structural core. Segmenting explicitly and building a proposition, a pricing model and a loyalty mechanic specifically for that customer is the offensive version of the bifurcation insight, not just the defensive one.

Use the integrated Virgin Atlantic model as the long-haul-premium moat

Jet2 proved the integrated airline-plus-operator model wins its segment. Virgin Holidays has the same structural advantage in long-haul premium: seat-inventory access, network alignment, ancillary economics, and a direct customer relationship. Naming and deliberately building those advantages, as Jet2 did for mass-market, converts an assumed position into a defended one.

Turn the SAF cost ramp into a value-leadership narrative

The SAF Mandate is fixed and known, and Virgin Atlantic has a public decarbonisation track record. The premium long-haul customer is the segment most receptive to a credible sustainability proposition, so a scheduled cost can become a brand-and-value differentiator if the narrative is genuine and specific.

What We Are Not Planning For This Cycle

A demand shock on the scale of the 2020 to 2021 travel collapse

The base case is a cost-of-living squeeze and bifurcation, not a pandemic-scale demand collapse. A shock of that magnitude would re-anchor every cluster and shift the horizon from years to weeks.

Reinstate if: a long-haul corridor closure or a global health event materially removes addressable demand.

Jet2 entering long-haul leisure at scale

The base case is that Jet2's integrated model stays in short and mid-haul, leaving Virgin Holidays' long-haul premium segment competitively insulated. A deliberate Jet2 move into long-haul would collapse that insulation and make Cluster 6 the dominant strategic question.

Reinstate if: Jet2 announces long-haul leisure routes or a long-haul package range.

AI platforms becoming the dominant booking transaction layer within the cycle

The base case is that AI supplements the booking journey through inspiration and research within the 2 to 5 year horizon, with the transaction still occurring through established channels. A faster shift would move Cluster 4 from a positioning question to a decision.

Reinstate if: a major AI platform adds confirmed travel-transaction capability with named airline or operator partners.

A structural reversal of the UK SAF Mandate

The base case is that the SAF Mandate ramps as legislated. A material weakening or reversal would ease the long-haul cost-base pressure in Cluster 5, but the policy direction across the UK and EU makes this unlikely within the horizon.

Reinstate if: the UK government legislates a material delay or reduction to the SAF Mandate ramp.

Discussion Points for the Leadership Team

  1. Which segment are we for? If the bifurcation holds, Virgin Holidays cannot serve the premium-resilient and the mass-market-squeezed customer with one proposition. Which one is the structural core, and what does the other become?
  2. Is the premium long-haul customer actually holding? What does our own H2 2026 premium booking curve have to show before we treat the bifurcation thesis as confirmed rather than assumed?
  3. Where do we show up when the customer asks an AI first? Do we have a presence in the AI discovery layer, and have we decided whether our play is to be the layer agents transact through or to be bookable inside the platforms?
  4. Have we built the SAF ramp into the cost-base model? Is the 2% to 10% to 22% schedule a known input in the FY27 plan, and have we pressure-tested which parts of the long-haul proposition hold as the per-trip cost rises faster than for short-haul competitors?
  5. What makes long-haul premium structurally defensible? Jet2 made its mass-market position structural through deliberate moves. What are our equivalent deliberate moves, and who owns each one?
  6. Is destination substitution reaching our core? A household that swaps a Caribbean package for a Spanish one has not stopped travelling, it has stopped being our customer. What in our own data tells us whether that substitution has reached the repeat-customer cohort?
