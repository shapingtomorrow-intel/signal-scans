Why This Matters Now

For decades the official information about a medicine has come on a folded paper leaflet in the box, with the same text online at emc. That is starting to change. Across Europe, leaflets are moving to QR codes and official databases, and new EU law will let countries go further from 2028.

At the same time, people are asking AI tools instead of reading leaflets, pharmacists are becoming prescribers, and the NHS App is where millions of people now order their medicines. In the UK, nobody has yet decided who should host the digital leaflet, or which sources AI tools must use.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: leaflets will go digital. The open questions are who hosts them, and whether a person or an AI tool reads them first.

Executive Synthesis

Which plans on your desk assume people will keep looking up medicines information on a website?

In June 2026 about 7.4 million repeat prescriptions were ordered through the NHS App (NHS England Digital, Jul 2026), one adult in seven has used an AI chatbot instead of contacting a GP (King's College London, May 2026), and France now links medicine boxes to a leaflet on a state database (ANSM, in French, Jul 2026).

emc exists because the official leaflet for every UK medicine needs one trusted home, and drug companies pay to keep it current. That arrangement is starting to move. France links about 170 pharmacy medicines to a digital leaflet on the state's own database (ANSM, in French, Jul 2026), and the EU's new pharmaceutical law, due to apply from 2028, lets each country allow electronic-only leaflets (DLRC, Jul 2026). UK rules lag: the MHRA's guidance still deals only with paper (MHRA, Jun 2026), and its new three-year programme will set the UK model, including where the digital leaflet lives (the MHRA in The Pharmaceutical Journal, Jul 2026).

Who reads that information is changing too. One adult in seven has used an AI chatbot instead of contacting a GP (King's College London, May 2026), and OpenEvidence, a leading clinical AI tool, left the UK in April, citing unsettled rules (Telehealth.org, May 2026). From this autumn pharmacists begin prescribing within NHS services (Department of Health and Social Care, May 2026). For the drug companies that fund emc, the 2026 UK deal improved pricing (House of Commons Library, Jul 2026), but supply problems remain common (Department of Health and Social Care, Aug 2026).

Looking ahead to 2030, all medicines approved through the EU's central route can switch to digital leaflets from the second half of 2027 (DLRC, Jul 2026), and EU rules for high-risk AI in medical devices apply from August 2028 (Council of the EU, in German, Jun 2026). The analysis expects the UK to decide how digital leaflets work before 2030, and AI tools to become a main route to medicines answers. Whether emc becomes the host, the checked data under those tools, or neither, is the leadership team's choice: section 7 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the UK will actively choose a host for digital leaflets. The MHRA could simply let each company put a QR code on its pack pointing to its own website, as Spain's rules allow (AEMPS, in Spanish, May 2026). Then emc's value would rest on being the most complete and current single source, not on a formal role.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Offer emc to the MHRA as ready infrastructure for the UK digital leaflet. The regulator's programme is at its start, and in France the regulator set the model.
  2. Set the terms on which AI tools may use emc data. Health AI rules favour checked sources, and the leading tools are waiting outside the UK.
  3. Put emc where the new prescribers work. Pharmacists begin prescribing this autumn and will look for answers inside their own systems.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. Medicine leaflets are going digital, and the UK has not chosen who hosts them

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The MHRA and government; Datapharm can shape it · Room to act: Wide now; narrows once a host is chosen

Digital leaflets are arriving as a regulator-led change on a fixed timetable. The EU's new law applies from 2028 and lets each country decide how far to go within it, and France and Spain are already putting it into practice through their regulators. The UK is behind: its guidance still treats the paper leaflet as the thing it regulates, and the MHRA's three-year programme will set the UK model, including where the digital leaflet lives. For Datapharm the question is how to help the regulator design that model, with emc as ready infrastructure.

  • The EU reform was agreed in December 2025, and EMA says: “The adopted acts of the new pharmaceutical legislation are expected to enter into force in 2026.” (EMA, Jun 2026) It then applies from 2028 and lets each country allow electronic-only leaflets for some or all medicines, with paper free on request (DLRC, Jul 2026). EMA published guidance for companies in September and invited them to help test the system (EMA, Sep 2026).
  • France's regulator is running a two-year trial in which QR codes on about 170 community-pharmacy medicines, including statins, vaccines and paracetamol, and about 420 hospital medicines link to the leaflet on the state's public medicines database. Pharmacy patients still get paper; hospitals are testing paperless supply (ANSM, in French, Jul 2026).
  • Spain's medicines agency published instructions on 13 May 2026 that fit QR codes into the normal labelling rules: codes can link to the official product information or other material, without changing the usual approval procedures (AEMPS, in Spanish, May 2026).
  • In the UK the regulator is still at the design stage. The MHRA's head of product information quality writes that some patients still value paper while many want information presented more usefully, and that the MHRA has launched a three-year programme on how medicines information is delivered (the MHRA in The Pharmaceutical Journal, Jul 2026). Its guidance on leaflet changes still deals only with paper leaflets, so UK rules lag the EU's (MHRA, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Pharmacy chains are pressing for electronic leaflets. The Company Chemists' Association set up a supply group in 2024 to introduce electronic leaflets as part of a wider push to strengthen the medicines supply chain (The Pharmaceutical Journal, Sep 2026). It is an early sign of the arguments the MHRA will hear as it designs the UK model.

Outlook to 2030

All medicines approved through the EU's central route can move to digital leaflets voluntarily from the second half of 2027, and the new law applies from 2028 (DLRC, Jul 2026). The MHRA's programme runs for three years (the MHRA in The Pharmaceutical Journal, Jul 2026), so on the analysis's reading the regulator will set the UK model, including where the digital leaflet is hosted, before 2030.

What it means: By 2030 the UK is likely to have a regulator-defined digital leaflet. emc's place in it depends on how useful Datapharm is to the MHRA while the model is designed.

Counter-argument

Only 32% of patients fully understand the leaflet they get now, and a learning disability charity warns that a move to electronic leaflets could create significant barriers for people who face digital exclusion (The Pharmaceutical Journal, Sep 2026). Those concerns may keep paper in the box for years, as France has done for pharmacy medicines, which would slow the whole shift and give the MHRA little reason to decide the model quickly.

2. AI now answers medicines questions, but the biggest tools have stepped back from the UK

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The MHRA, the EU and the AI firms · Room to act: Moderate; emc sets the terms for its own data

Clinicians and patients increasingly ask AI tools about medicines instead of looking them up. The tools that did best on drug questions have stepped back from the UK and EU because the rules are unsettled, and the rules now being drafted favour tools that can show where their answers came from. That could make emc's checked, current data more valuable. It could also see AI answers bypass emc altogether.

  • One UK adult in seven (15%) has used an AI chatbot for health advice instead of contacting a GP or other NHS service, and 21% of those users decided against professional advice because of something a chatbot said (King's College London, May 2026). Among GPs, 28% already use AI tools at work and 84% are concerned about the lack of regulatory oversight (Nuffield Trust, Dec 2025).
  • OpenEvidence, which says more than 40% of US physicians use it, halted access in the UK and EU in late April, citing regulatory uncertainty (Telehealth.org, May 2026). OpenAI launched a free version of ChatGPT for clinicians in April, starting in the US (Fierce Healthcare, Apr 2026).
  • In published tests on drug information questions, OpenEvidence scored 95% accuracy on one set of dosing questions against ChatGPT's 40%, but every chatbot scored lower than pharmacists (Pharmacy Times, Jul 2026).
  • On 10 September the national commission on AI in healthcare, set up by the MHRA, published recommendations based on evidence from more than 12,000 people (AI in Healthcare Commission, Sep 2026). Its central proposal: “The Commission recommends that the MHRA introduce staged authorisations for new AI models, similar to 'L-plates' for learner drivers.” The EU has moved its deadlines for high-risk AI to December 2027 and August 2028 (Council of the EU, in German, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal UK clinicians are losing AI tools they can use in the room. A UK clinician-run digest reports that UK and EEA users were excluded from ChatGPT for clinicians, and that Heidi's evidence tool, which still operates in the UK, cannot be used during a live consultation (The Monday Clinical Brief, May 2026). If more tools restrict UK use this way, clinicians will need an approved source they can use while the patient is in front of them.

Outlook to 2030

EU rules for high-risk AI built into products, including medical devices, apply from 2 August 2028 (Council of the EU, in German, Jun 2026), and the UK government will respond to the commission's recommendations separately (MHRA, Sep 2026). By 2030, on the analysis's reading, AI tools that answer medicines questions in the UK are likely to have to show which approved source they used.

What it means: emc's data becomes more valuable to AI firms if the rules ask for checked sources, and less valuable if tools can answer without them.

Counter-argument

AI firms may not need emc. They can read leaflets published anywhere, and the EU is building its own digital leaflet system (EMA, Sep 2026). If regulators judge AI tools on the quality of their answers rather than their sources, being the checked source brings little advantage.

Decision link: Strategic Implication 2.

3. More people now prescribe, and they need trusted answers where they work

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: NHS England, the Department of Health and pharmacy system suppliers · Room to act: Wide; partnerships decide it

Prescribing is spreading beyond doctors. From this autumn community pharmacists can prescribe within NHS services, and most new pharmacists now qualify as prescribers. People who prescribe need reliable medicines information at the moment they decide, which increasingly means inside pharmacy systems and the NHS App rather than on a website. Stretched pharmacies may slow the change, but are unlikely to reverse it.

  • Most pharmacists joining the register from summer 2026 will be prescribers from the day they register (GPhC, Jun 2026). In June, 83% of 3,326 candidates passed the registration exam, including the first cohort to qualify as prescribers automatically (The Pharmacist, Jul 2026).
  • The £340 million pharmacy deal for 2026/27 brings prescribing into NHS community pharmacy nationally from autumn 2026 (Department of Health and Social Care, May 2026), starting with new Pharmacy First conditions such as acne and migraine (Community Pharmacy England, Sep 2026). NHS England wants at least 95% of pharmacies that sign up to be prescribing by 31 March 2027, with £51,000 of support for each local NHS area (NHS England, Aug 2026).
  • Pharmacy First delivered over 3.3 million consultations in the year to February 2026, up 43% (Department of Health and Social Care, May 2026). In June 2026 more than 15 million people logged into the NHS App, and 3,163 pharmacies were live with prescription tracking (NHS England Digital, Jul 2026).
  • Up to 1,200 newly qualified pharmacists will receive funded supervision, with priority for those working alone in independent pharmacies (The Pharmaceutical Journal, May 2026).

Outlook to 2030

By 31 March 2027 NHS England expects prescribing activity in every pharmacy that signs up (NHS England, Aug 2026), and a projected shortfall of 16,000 community pharmacists by 2036/37 (House of Commons Library, Mar 2026) means the prescribers who exist will be short of time. By 2030 the analysis expects many routine prescribing decisions to be made in pharmacies, using whatever information their systems put in front of them.

What it means: emc needs to be inside pharmacy and prescribing systems well before 2030, or a growing group of its users will learn to work without it.

Counter-argument

The Pharmacists' Defence Association warns that pharmacies are already at capacity after a 25% rise in Pharmacy First work, adding: “The introduction of independent prescribing represents a substantial increase in clinical responsibility, rather than a straightforward extension of existing services.” (Pharmacists' Defence Association, May 2026) England has also lost 9% of its pharmacies in ten years (House of Commons Library, Mar 2026). If prescribing stalls, demand for new decision support will grow slowly.

4. Drug companies got a better UK deal, but shortages have not gone away

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Government, the NHS and drug companies · Room to act: Narrow on policy; wide on services

emc is paid for by drug companies, so their position in the UK matters directly. The 2026 UK deal with the US improved pricing and brought new investment, which should steady their budgets. But supply problems and product withdrawals remain frequent, and each one changes what prescribers and patients need to know. Accurate, current supply information is becoming as important as the leaflet itself.

  • Under the UK-US deal, the US will not put tariffs on UK medicines until 19 January 2029, NICE's cost threshold rose to £25,000 to £35,000 per quality-adjusted life year from April 2026, and the repayment rate drug companies pay on newer medicines is capped at 15% (House of Commons Library, Jul 2026).
  • The industry body ABPI reports an extra £2 billion of UK investment since September 2025, a repayment rate down to 14.5% in 2026 from 22.9% in 2025, and nine more medicines approved under the higher threshold (ABPI, Sep 2026). The government counts more than £3 billion of new life sciences investment in a year (Department for Science, Innovation and Technology, Jul 2026).
  • Around 700 supply problems and around 200 discontinuations were reported to the government in the first half of 2026 (Department of Health and Social Care, Aug 2026). The count is falling, from 1,013 in the first half of 2024 to 687 in 2026, but pharmacy leaders say they have felt no easing on the ground (The Pharmaceutical Journal, Aug 2026).
  • A House of Lords committee found that 73% of pharmacy workers said supply problems put patients at risk, and that only a quarter of the generic medicines the NHS uses are made in the UK (House of Lords Public Services Committee, Feb 2026). The EU reached political agreement on its Critical Medicines Act in May to spread supply across more sources (European Commission, in French, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Launches are falling in European markets that US pricing looks at. Launches in European markets used as reference points by US pricing models fell by 43% on average in the ten months after the US ‘most favoured nation’ pricing order (Pharmaceutical Technology, Mar 2026). If companies delay UK launches too, emc will carry fewer new medicines and more withdrawals.

Outlook to 2030

US tariffs on UK medicines stay at zero until January 2029, a replacement for the current repayment scheme is due by 1 January 2029, and NHS spending on new medicines is to rise to at least 0.6% of GDP by 2036 (House of Commons Library, Jul 2026). The industry body expects up to 81,300 more jobs by 2035 if growth speeds up (ABPI, Sep 2026).

What it means: Customers' UK budgets should hold to 2029. What they buy from Datapharm may shift toward supply and change information.

Counter-argument

The deal's cost is disputed: the government puts it at about £1 billion to 2028/29, while independent estimates run to £1.3 billion to £2.3 billion a year by 2029 (House of Commons Library, Jul 2026). If the NHS has to find that money elsewhere, pressure on medicines budgets could return before the repayment scheme is replaced.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Offer emc to the MHRA as ready infrastructure for the UK digital leaflet

The MHRA's three-year programme on how medicines information is delivered is at its start (the MHRA in The Pharmaceutical Journal, Jul 2026), and in France the regulator put the digital leaflet on the state's own database (ANSM, in French, Jul 2026). Where the UK digital leaflet lives will be the regulator's decision. Datapharm should give the MHRA and the Department of Health and Social Care a concrete offer: emc as ready infrastructure behind the QR code, meeting the EU's digital leaflet standard (EMA, Sep 2026), with paper and accessible formats for people who need them. The CEO owns this with the board, because it sets the company's role to 2030.

Action: The CEO to present a written proposal to the MHRA's patient information programme by 31 December 2026.

Decide Draws on Theme 1

Implication 2: Set the terms on which AI tools may use emc data

The UK commission's staged approvals (AI in Healthcare Commission, Sep 2026) and the EU's rules for high-risk AI (Council of the EU, in German, Jun 2026) both point toward AI tools having to show where their answers came from. Datapharm should decide now how AI tools may license emc data, how they can show an answer came from the current approved leaflet, and what it will not allow. The terms should be ready before the leading tools return to the UK (Telehealth.org, May 2026).

Action: The Chief Product Officer and Chief Technology Officer to publish AI data-use terms and a source-checking offer by 31 March 2027.

Prepare Draws on Themes 2 and 1

Implication 3: Put emc where the new prescribers work

Pharmacists begin prescribing within NHS services this autumn (NHS England, Aug 2026), and the NHS App already handles millions of repeat prescriptions a month (NHS England Digital, Jul 2026). Datapharm should agree links with the main pharmacy system suppliers and explore a route into the NHS App, so that prescribers and patients meet emc content where they already work.

Action: The Chief Commercial Officer to agree at least two pharmacy system partnerships by 31 March 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 3

Implication 4: Track launches, withdrawals and shortages every quarter

Customer budgets look steadier to 2029 (House of Commons Library, Jul 2026), but launches and withdrawals are the early signs of change (Pharmaceutical Technology, Mar 2026) (Department of Health and Social Care, Aug 2026). A quarterly count of new UK listings and withdrawals on emc, set against the government's shortage figures, would show early whether customer demand is shifting.

Action: The commercial and data teams to report the count to the leadership team each quarter from January 2027.

Early indicators: The NHS App adds medicine information pages; the MHRA consults on a state-run leaflet database.

The data behind the chatbot

In 2030 most medicines questions go to AI tools, which have returned to the UK under staged approvals (AI in Healthcare Commission, Sep 2026). Few people open a leaflet, but the tools need current, checked data, and emc licenses it to them. Income shifts from drug company listing fees toward data licences.

Early indicators: AI tools return to the UK naming their data sources; the government response to the commission asks for traceable sources.

Bypassed

In 2030 the state hosts the leaflet, and AI tools read it directly from free official databases (EMA, Sep 2026). People rarely visit any medicines website. emc keeps some specialist professional users but loses its central role, and drug companies question what they pay for.

Early indicators: The UK adopts the EU's open digital leaflet format with a state database; AI tools cite government sources, not emc.

Keep emc ready and wait

Make emc's leaflets meet the EU digital leaflet format and work behind QR codes, but let the MHRA and government decide the host.

Trade-off: Cheap and low risk, but the decision may be made without Datapharm.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The NHS App leaflet; Bypassed

Option B · Middle course

Offer emc as a shared host

Propose emc as the host behind UK QR codes, working alongside the NHS App so the state keeps the front door.

Trade-off: A strong position that asks less of government, but emc becomes one layer of a state service.

Option C · Biggest shift

Offer to run the digital leaflet for the MHRA

Offer to operate the UK digital leaflet on the regulator's behalf, with commitments on accessibility, paper on request and reliability.

Trade-off: The largest prize, but it brings public-service duties and scrutiny.

⊞ Fits scenarios: emc as the national leaflet

What We Are Not Planning For

Not planned for

Paper leaflets abolished in the UK before 2030

France keeps paper in the box for pharmacy medicines during its trial (ANSM, in French, Jul 2026), the EU reform keeps paper free on request (DLRC, Jul 2026), and UK charities warn about digital exclusion (The Pharmaceutical Journal, Sep 2026). This briefing plans for paper and digital side by side.

Not planned for

AI tools banned from UK clinical use

The commission's proposals aim to make AI safer to use, not to stop it (AI in Healthcare Commission, Sep 2026), and 28% of GPs already use AI tools at work (Nuffield Trust, Dec 2025).

↺ Reinstated if a serious patient-safety incident leads to a halt on clinical AI.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If the MHRA asked tomorrow who should host the UK's digital leaflet, what would our answer be, and what would we commit to?
  2. What would we need to show an AI tool, or a regulator, to prove that an answer came from the current approved leaflet?
  3. Which pharmacy systems do the new pharmacist prescribers use, and do we have a working relationship with any of their suppliers?
  4. If more AI tools cannot be used during UK consultations, as reported for Heidi's evidence tool (The Monday Clinical Brief, May 2026), could emc become the source clinicians use with the patient in the room?
  5. Which of our customers are delaying UK launches or withdrawing products, and what does that mean for our income to 2029?

ANSM (ansm.sante.fr) (in French)Tier 1Jul 2026France's medicines regulator ANSM is running a two-year e-notice pilot, begun in autumn 2025, in which QR codes on boxes link to the digital leaflet hosted on the state's public medicines database (BDPM), covering about 170 community-pharmacy medicines (statins, vaccines, PPIs, oral paracetamol), where the paper leaflet stays in the box, and about 420 hospital medicines, where hospitals are testing fully paperless leaflets.
European Medicines Agency (ema.europa.eu)Tier 1Jun 2026EMA says the Parliament and Council reached political agreement on the pharma reform on 11 December 2025, that the adopted acts are expected to enter into force in 2026, that 2026-2028 is a transition phase in which Member States update national laws, and that the new legislation becomes applicable in 2028.
MHRA (gov.uk)Tier 1Jun 2026MHRA guidance makes self-certification the default route for changes to labels and patient information leaflets unless updates fall in categories P1-P4 needing full assessment, with an acceptance letter within 14 days of validation, and it deals only with printed mock-ups and paper leaflets, with no mention of QR codes or electronic leaflets.
AEMPS (aemps.gob.es) (in Spanish)Tier 1May 2026On 13 May 2026 Spain's medicines agency AEMPS published instructions on using QR codes in the package leaflet and/or labelling of nationally, decentralised and mutually recognised authorised medicines, allowing QR codes to link to statutory product information or other supplementary information, without changing the usual variation procedures.
DLRC Group (dlrcgroup.com)Tier 2Jul 2026Under the EU reform, Member States have discretion to allow exclusive use of electronic product information for specific categories of medicines or all medicines, with a paper copy available on request at no extra cost to the patient, and EMA's voluntary ePI adoption starts in late 2026 for vaccines, early 2027 for oncology products and the second half of 2027 for all centrally authorised products.
The Pharmaceutical Journal (pharmaceutical-journal.com)Tier 3Sep 2026The feature reports that only 32% (n=743) of patients fully understand their leaflet and 55% (n=1,124) report readability issues (2023 KSS AHSN study), that the Company Chemists' Association's Medicines Supply Resilience Group has pushed for electronic PILs since 2024, and that the British Institute of Learning Disabilities warns ePILs could create significant barriers for people with learning disabilities who face digital exclusion.
The Pharmaceutical Journal (pharmaceutical-journal.com)Tier 3Jul 2026Writing for the MHRA, Smita Robinson (head of the product information quality unit) says MHRA-commissioned research by WoodNewton found some patients still value paper leaflets while many want information presented more usefully, and that the MHRA has launched the Improving Patient Information (IPI) project, a three-year programme on how medicines information is delivered, noting other markets are moving to QR codes, apps and online platforms.

Theme 2: AI now answers medicines questions, but the biggest tools have stepped back from the UK

SourceTierDateKey claim used
MHRA / National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare (gov.uk)Tier 1Sep 2026On 10 September 2026 the MHRA-established National Commission (set up September 2025) published its recommendations after gathering evidence from more than 12,000 people over a year, proposing staged 'L-plate' authorisations for new AI models under close supervision and tight guardrails, continuous real-world monitoring, and transparency to patients about when AI is used; a cross-government response will follow separately.
Rat der Europäischen Union (consilium.europa.eu) (in German)Tier 1Jun 2026On 29 June 2026 the EU Council gave final approval to the AI Act 'digital omnibus', which moves the application dates for high-risk AI obligations to 2 December 2027 for stand-alone high-risk AI systems and 2 August 2028 for high-risk AI systems embedded in products (the route that covers AI medical devices), instead of August 2026/2027.
King's College London (kcl.ac.uk)Tier 2May 2026A King's College London Policy Institute / King's Health Partners / Responsible AI UK survey found that one in seven (15%) of the UK public have used AI chatbots for health advice instead of contacting a GP or other NHS service, and 10% used AI for mental health support instead of a professional; among AI health-advice users, 20% say the tool did not encourage them to seek a professional opinion and 21% decided against seeking professional advice because of something a chatbot said.
Nuffield Trust (nuffieldtrust.org.uk)Tier 2Dec 2025A Nuffield Trust and RCGP survey of over 2,100 GPs found 28% use AI tools at work: 13% use practice-provided tools, 11% use tools obtained independently (such as ChatGPT) and 4% use both; 84% cited a lack of regulatory oversight as a concern, and GPs described a 'wild west' with no consistent national guidance.
Pharmacy Times (pharmacytimes.com)Tier 3Jul 2026Reviewing peer-reviewed studies, the article reports that OpenEvidence had the highest mean CLEAR score on drug information questions (17.82/25 vs ChatGPT 15.72/25) and 95% accuracy on long-acting injectable antipsychotic dosing queries against ChatGPT's 40%, but every AI chatbot, OpenEvidence included, scored statistically lower than pharmacists.
Telehealth.org (telehealth.org)Tier 3May 2026OpenEvidence, a US clinical evidence and decision-support AI that says more than 40% of US physicians use it across more than 10,000 hospitals and medical centres, halted access in the EU and UK (users saw the notice on 28 April 2026), citing 'mounting regulatory uncertainty regarding the treatment of AI systems in the European Union and the United Kingdom, including... the EU Artificial Intelligence Act'.
Fierce Healthcare (fiercehealthcare.com)Tier 3Apr 2026OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Clinicians, free to any verified physician, nurse practitioner, physician assistant or pharmacist 'starting in the U.S.', with expansion to other countries 'over time'; OpenAI says clinician use of ChatGPT has more than doubled in a year, and 81% of physicians surveyed by the AMA say they use AI professionally.
The Monday Clinical Brief (mondayclinicalbrief.co.uk)Tier 4May 2026A UK clinician-run digest gives this timeline: OpenEvidence withdrew from the UK and EU on 28 April 2026 with no replacement; OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Clinicians on 23 April 2026 with the UK and EEA explicitly excluded at regional verification; and from May 2026 Heidi Evidence limits evidence querying in the UK/EU to out-of-session use (not during live consultations) and excludes NHS UK accounts from Evidence entirely.

Department of Health and Social Care (gov.uk)Tier 1May 2026A £340 million government-funded 2026/27 community pharmacy deal agreed with Community Pharmacy England lets pharmacists with an independent prescribing qualification assess and prescribe directly, rolled out nationally from autumn 2026; over 3.3 million Pharmacy First consultations were delivered between March 2025 and February 2026, up 43% on the previous 12 months, with 86% reporting a positive experience.

SourceTierDateKey claim used
Department of Health and Social Care (gov.uk)Tier 1Aug 2026DHSC recorded around 700 supply issue notifications and around 200 discontinuation notifications from January to June 2026, after around 1,400 supply issue notifications in 2025 (down from about 1,900 in 2024) and around 480 discontinuations in 2025 (versus about 400 in each of 2023 and 2024); manufacturing problems caused 62% of 2025 notifications.

Department for Science, Innovation and Technology / Office for Life Sciences (gov.uk)Tier 1Jul 2026Marking one year of the Life Sciences Sector Plan, government says the sector attracted more than £3 billion of new public-private investment in 12 months (including AstraZeneca £300 million, Moderna £1 billion over 10 years, UCB £500 million), clinical trial set-up fell from 169 to 122 days, and a Jobs Plan targets 66,000 additional jobs by 2035.

House of Lords Public Services Committee (committees.parliament.uk)Tier 1Feb 2026The Lords Public Services Committee report 'Medicines security - a national priority' found 73% of pharmacy workers in 2025 said supply issues put patients at risk, noted 80% of NHS prescribed medicines are generics of which only a quarter are made in the UK, and urged that medicine security be treated as a national security issue.
ABPI (abpi.org.uk)Tier 2Sep 2026The ABPI's 2026 Competitiveness Report says industry has committed an additional £2 billion of UK investment since September 2025, the newer-medicines VPAG rate has fallen to 14.5% in 2026 from a 2025 peak of 22.9%, the higher NICE threshold (£25,000-35,000 per QALY) has already enabled nine more medicines, and accelerated growth could add 81,300 jobs by 2035 (to about 200,000) and lift direct GVA to £33.4 billion from £20.4 billion; it warns the UK R&D tax credit (20%) lags Ireland (35%) and Japan (40%).
The Pharmaceutical Journal (pharmaceutical-journal.com)Tier 3Aug 2026The government received 687 supply issue notifications in the first half of 2026, compared with 857 in the same period of 2025 and 1,013 in 2024, but community pharmacy leaders say they have felt no easing of shortages on the ground.

Claim-fidelity self-disclosure

This appendix records where the evidence is thinner than a source count suggests, and what was done about it.

Plain language, same evidence. At the commissioning analyst's request this edition avoids technical language. Terms are explained where they first appear, and the Abbreviations appendix follows. No claim was simplified beyond what its source says; the source's own wording is kept in the evidence record.

Datapharm's own pages. Two Tier 4 sources are Datapharm's own pages (Datapharm, Aug 2026) (Datapharm, Aug 2026). They are used only to record what Datapharm has announced, in the Continuity ledger.

Directional sources. The account of AI tools restricting UK use comes from a clinician-run digest (The Monday Clinical Brief, May 2026) and is marked as a weak signal. Quotation. Three sources are quoted word for word, all in English: EMA on when the EU law enters into force, the national commission on staged approvals for AI, and the Pharmacists' Defence Association on prescribing. Four sources in French, Spanish and German (France's and Spain's medicines agencies, the Council of the EU and the European Commission) are summarised in English and cited, not quoted. Analyst judgements and added sources. The theme weights, the "who decides" and "room to act" lines, the Options and the scenario axes are the analysis's proposals for discussion, not findings. Abbreviations

The abbreviations used in this briefing, in alphabetical order.

ABPI: Association of the British Pharmaceutical Industry, the industry body for UK drug companies
AEMPS: Spain's medicines agency
AMA: American Medical Association
ANSM: France's medicines agency
aRMMs: additional risk minimisation materials, extra safety material for some medicines
BDPM: France's public medicines database
CPCF: Community Pharmacy Contractual Framework, the NHS contract for community pharmacies in England
CPPE: Centre for Pharmacy Postgraduate Education
EEA: European Economic Area: the EU plus Iceland, Liechtenstein and Norway
EMA: European Medicines Agency
emc: electronic medicines compendium, Datapharm's service publishing UK medicines information
ePACT: the NHS system that records prescribing data
ePI: electronic product information, the digital version of a medicine's leaflet and prescribing information
ePILs: electronic patient information leaflets
GP: general practitioner, a family doctor
GPhC: General Pharmaceutical Council, the regulator for pharmacists in Great Britain
GVA: gross value added, a measure of economic output
ICB: integrated care board, the local NHS body that plans and pays for services
IPI: Improving Patient Information, the MHRA's three-year programme
KSS AHSN: Kent Surrey Sussex Academic Health Science Network
MFN: most favoured nation, a US policy tying US drug prices to prices abroad
MHRA: Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency, the UK medicines regulator
NICE: National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, which judges whether the NHS should pay for a medicine
PDA: Pharmacists' Defence Association
PILs: patient information leaflets
PPIs: proton pump inhibitors, medicines that reduce stomach acid
QALY: quality-adjusted life year, a measure combining length and quality of life
QR code: a square barcode that a phone camera opens as a web link
RAPS: Regulatory Affairs Professionals Society
RCGP: Royal College of General Practitioners
SmPCs: summaries of product characteristics, the official prescribing information for professionals
VPAG: the voluntary scheme for branded medicines pricing, access and growth, under which companies repay part of NHS spending
