Why This Matters Now

For most people money still lives in a bank account. That is starting to change. Digital dollars issued by private firms, known as stablecoins, are worth around $300 billion. Banks are turning ordinary deposits into digital tokens that move around the clock. Europe is building a digital euro. And AI assistants are starting to spend money on people's behalf.

This summer showed that the rules are being written piece by piece, and often after the fact. The US Senate failed to pass its crypto market law on 15 September. Europe is still arguing over how much digital cash each person may hold. Nobody has yet decided who pays when an AI agent makes a bad purchase. And savers in private credit funds found they could not always get their money out.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: by 2030 your money is likely to sit in more places than your bank, and the protections you rely on today were written for a world where it did not.

Executive Synthesis

If a customer's AI assistant spends their money on the wrong thing next year, who pays: the customer, the bank or the firm that built the assistant?

Nobody has decided. The Bank of England says its rules were not built for agents that act on their own, and a US banking group warns the usual cap on what customers lose may not apply.

In May this series described money rewiring itself onto new rails, and in July it argued that money was starting to leave the bank account. Two months on, the direction is the same, but the fight has changed. It is no longer about whether new kinds of digital money arrive. It is about where they are allowed to compete with the bank deposit, and on what terms.

The clearest example is interest. A stablecoin is a digital token that is meant to be worth one dollar, backed by cash and government bonds. If a stablecoin can pay interest, it starts to look like a savings account without the bank. The US Senate's final version of its crypto market law would have banned interest on stablecoin balances (Senator Lummis's office, Sep 2026), but it never reached a final vote: “A procedural vote on the crypto market structure bill failed 49 to 50.” (American Banker, Sep 2026). Elsewhere the line is being drawn in the same place. The Bank of England's final stablecoin policy says it will “Maintain our policy position that systemic stablecoins should not pay interest to coinholders.” It still allows rewards linked to using the coin (the Bank of England, Jun 2026). Korea's parliamentary budget office, looking at a won stablecoin, points to the same approach (Korea's National Assembly Budget Office, Sep 2026). The pattern is the same everywhere: no interest for simply holding digital dollars, but rewards for using them.

Banks are not waiting. Eighteen US banks, including Bank of America, Citi, J.P. Morgan and Wells Fargo, are building a shared system to move tokenized deposits between them around the clock (The Clearing House, Jun 2026). A tokenized deposit is an ordinary bank deposit, still insured and regulated, turned into a digital token that can move like a stablecoin. One advisory firm counts that “More than half (52%) of the top-50 U.S. banks are now evaluating or implementing tokenized deposits, based on our analysis of each bank's public statements and filings, a steep increase from just 18% at the end of 2025.” (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026). The settlement system for US shares and bonds ran its first live trades with tokenized assets in July (DTCC, Jul 2026).

Governments are building too. The European Parliament voted for a digital euro, a form of digital cash issued by the central bank, by 416 votes to 169 (the European Parliament, Jul 2026), partly because Visa and Mastercard handle most card payments in the euro area (Euronews, Jun 2026). And AI agents, software that acts for people rather than advising them, are starting to make payments. The International Monetary Fund notes that “Artificial intelligence is entering a new phase in which systems can act autonomously on behalf of users.” (the IMF, Apr 2026).

By 2030 your money is likely to sit in more places than your bank, and the protections you rely on today were written for a world where it did not.

Two risks run underneath all of this. The first is about protection. Payment rules assume a person presses "pay". A Bank of England deputy governor put it plainly: “Our frameworks were not built to contemplate autonomous agents, and relying on a human in the loop for all agent actions is unlikely to be realistic.” (the Bank of England, Jun 2026). In the United States, a banking trade group warns that “the general rule in EFTA that limits consumers' liability for unauthorized transactions may not apply when agents are involved.” (the Consumer Bankers Association, Jan 2026). People are cautious for now: “only 24% of US online adults trust AI agents to act on their behalf to make routine purchases” (Forrester, Nov 2025). But the firms building agent payments expect far more spending to run through them by 2030 (Fortune, Sep 2026).

The second is about where risk ends up when lending leaves banks. Private credit, meaning loans made by investment funds rather than banks, has grown to $1.5 to $2 trillion, and banks have given those funds around $220 billion of credit lines (the FSB, May 2026). This year the funds sold to ordinary investors faced their first real test. Withdrawal requests jumped and “Most managers chose to cap redemptions at 5 percent of NAV.” (the Federal Reserve, May 2026). In one case investors asked for 40.7% of a fund back and received a fraction of that (Investment Executive, Apr 2026).

The common thread is that money is moving to places the old protections do not fully reach: deposit insurance, refund rules for unauthorised payments, and limits on how much savers can lose. None of these moves is reckless on its own. Together they mean the next five years will decide which protections follow money into its new homes, and which do not.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The deposit drain may be small. The White House's economists find that “At baseline calibration of CEA's model, eliminating stablecoin yield increases bank lending by $2.1 billion and has a net welfare cost of $800 million.” (the White House Council of Economic Advisers, Sep 2026), which suggests banning interest protects very little lending. Federal Reserve researchers reach a different answer: “for each $100 billion of net deposit drain not recycled to banks, empirical pass-throughs imply a $60 to $126 billion contraction in bank lending based on empirical estimates of the money multiplier” (the Federal Reserve, Dec 2025). Much depends on whether stablecoin issuers keep their reserves in banks. If they do, money stays inside the banking system and simply changes owner.

Bank tokens may not take off. Earlier bank consortia failed, and a Bank of America payments executive has said clients are not beating down the door for tokenized deposits (Forbes, Jul 2026). Dozens of schemes are being built separately (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026). The claim here is only that banks are now building, not that they will win.

Private credit may be less dangerous than it looks. The European Central Bank finds that “ECB supervisory data estimate that euro area banks' exposures to private credit worldwide total €62.5 billion, which is 0.2% of total assets or 2.5% of total equity.” (the ECB, May 2026), and Federal Reserve staff judge the direct risk to large US banks limited (Federal Reserve staff, May 2025). The risk this briefing points to is to savers in funds that promise easy withdrawals, not to banks.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Decide how you will compete on rewards, not interest. With the US law stalled and the UK and Korea drawing the same line, the contest for deposits will be fought on rewards for using digital money.
  2. If you build a deposit token, join a shared scheme first. Bank tokens only compete with stablecoins if they can move between banks, and almost none of the schemes being built connect.
  3. Set your own rules for AI agents that pay before regulators do. Who carries the loss when an agent gets it wrong is not settled in the UK, the US or Hong Kong.
  4. Check where your savings or balance sheet touch private credit. The first test came through withdrawal limits on retail funds, not bank failures.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications.

Audience Snapshots

If you run a bank's deposits

The contest for deposits is moving from interest rates to rewards

The shift: Most governments now ban interest on digital dollars but allow rewards for using them (the Bank of England, Jun 2026) (Korea's National Assembly Budget Office, Sep 2026), and US banks are building a shared deposit token for 2027 (Forbes, Jul 2026).

The question to brief: Which of our customers would move money for a payments reward, and what would it cost us to match it?

If you run payments

The shift: Swift's shared ledger is ready for 24/7 payments with tokenized deposits (Swift, Jul 2026), the Bank of England is lengthening settlement hours (the Bank of England, May 2026), and Hong Kong is testing AI agents in payments (the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, Aug 2026).

Themes

The cycle's signals are organised into five themes, ranked by impact on near-term decisions. Immediate: changes the near-term plan, the structure or the proposition. Near-Term: changes position over the next twelve months. Longer-Range: a multi-year factor to track and revisit each cycle.

1. Governments are banning interest on digital dollars but letting rewards through

Immediate

A stablecoin that pays interest competes directly with a savings account. That is why interest became the single hardest issue in the US crypto market law, and why the law stalled. The same line is now being drawn almost everywhere: no interest for simply holding a digital dollar, but rewards for using one. The question that decides how fast money leaves banks has therefore moved from Washington to the small print on what counts as a reward.

  • The final Senate text would have banned interest on stablecoin balances and given the Treasury a new power to stop sudden deposit outflows (Senator Lummis's office, Sep 2026). It did not reach a final vote: the procedural motion failed 49 to 50 on 15 September, and banks named interest as their leading issue (American Banker, Sep 2026).
  • The compromise behind the Senate text banned rewards that work like deposit interest but allowed rewards tied to activity, such as payments or loyalty programmes (the Cato Institute, Jun 2026).
  • The Bank of England's final approach keeps its ban on interest for large sterling stablecoins, allows rewards for using them, drops the per-person holding limits it once proposed and sets a £40 billion ceiling on each coin at first (the Bank of England, Jun 2026).
  • Korea, drafting rules for a won stablecoin, is looking at the same model and suggests capping the reward rate (Korea's National Assembly Budget Office, Sep 2026).
  • The US bank lobby argues the White House study asked the wrong question: allowing interest as the market grows from about $300 billion could cut lending by $4.4 to $8.7 billion in a single state such as Iowa (the American Bankers Association, Apr 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A new emergency brake on deposit flight. The final Senate text gave the Treasury a power to stop deposit outflows linked to stablecoins (Senator Lummis's office, Sep 2026). The bill stalled, but the idea of a legal brake on deposit flight is now on the table for the next attempt.

Counter-argument

The strongest objection is that none of this matters much, because deposits that go into stablecoins mostly come back to banks as the issuers' reserves (the Federal Reserve, Dec 2025), and the White House's own model finds a ban on interest barely changes bank lending (the White House Council of Economic Advisers, Sep 2026). That is a fair reading of today's market. It is weaker as the market grows, and it assumes rewards stay modest. If rewards for using a coin become generous, the ban on interest stops meaning very much.

2. Banks are turning ordinary deposits into digital tokens to keep money from leaving

Near-Term

Banks' answer to stablecoins is to make the ordinary deposit behave like one. A tokenized deposit is still a regulated, insured bank deposit, but it can move around the clock and carry instructions, such as paying automatically when goods arrive. The building has started in earnest in 2026. Whether it works depends on one thing banks have historically been poor at: making their systems talk to each other.

  • Eighteen US banks will clear and settle tokenized deposits between each other on a shared system run by The Clearing House, whose networks already move more than $2 trillion a day (The Clearing House, Jun 2026).
  • “More than half (52%) of the top-50 U.S. banks are now evaluating or implementing tokenized deposits, based on our analysis of each bank's public statements and filings, a steep increase from just 18% at the end of 2025.” (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026)
  • Swift says its shared ledger is ready for early use, with 17 banks set to pilot round-the-clock cross-border payments using tokenized deposits (Swift, Jul 2026).
  • “The tokenized trades were processed on July 15 and marked a significant milestone that sets the stage for the DTCC Tokenization Service to launch in October 2026.” (DTCC, Jul 2026)
  • Central banks are building the connections. The Bank of England wants tokenized deposits usable between banks and says “We are already on track next year to extend RTGS settlement hours” from 12 to 16½ hours each working day (the Bank of England, May 2026). The ECB plans to go live this year with a bridge that lets tokens settle in central bank money, with a 24/7 service by mid-2028 (the ECB, Aug 2026). Seven central banks tested the idea for cross-border payments and will move to real transactions (the BIS, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Japan's central bank is testing settlement on a blockchain. Governor Ueda said the Bank of Japan will test settling central bank reserves on a blockchain system, and that tokenized deposits can carry smart contracts (the Bank of Japan, Mar 2026).

Counter-argument

Banks have tried shared networks before and walked away, and a Bank of America payments executive says clients are not asking for tokenized deposits (Forbes, Jul 2026). Dozens of schemes are being built that do not connect (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026). The claim is not that bank tokens will win. It is that banks are now building a direct answer to stablecoins, and that the answer only works if a token at one bank can pay someone at another.

3. Europe is building its own digital cash to depend less on American payment giants

Near-Term

The digital euro is often described as a technology project. In 2026 it became a question of independence. European officials now argue openly that everyday payments rely too heavily on American card schemes and, soon, on dollar stablecoins. The design choice that will decide whether the digital euro matters is simple to state: how much of it each person may hold.

  • “With 416 votes in favour, 169 against and 22 abstentions for the creation of a digital euro”, the European Parliament adopted its position on 9 July, backing a digital euro that works online and offline, with a cap on how much any person may hold (the European Parliament, Jul 2026).
  • “US payment giants Visa and Mastercard account for 61% of card payments in the euro area and almost all cross-border card transactions.” (Euronews, Jun 2026) The ECB adds that “13 out of the 21 euro area countries depend entirely on international card schemes or mobile solutions for in-store payments” (the ECB, Mar 2026).
  • The ECB has picked 36 payment firms for a twelve-month pilot starting in the second half of 2027 (the ECB, Jul 2026). The Banque de France sets out the timetable, in French, “pour un déploiement auprès du grand public à l'horizon 2029.”, which reads in English as “for roll-out to the general public by around 2029.” (the Banque de France, Apr 2026)
  • The cap being negotiated is €3,000 per person. “Under the scenario the ECB calls most likely, ordinary use with no crisis involved, a holding limit of €3,000 for consumers is estimated to cost banks 8-18 basis points of net interest income, a rounding error against normal year-to-year swings in profitability.” (OMFIF, Sep 2026)
  • Other countries show what public payment systems can do. Brazil's central-bank-run Pix is used by 148 million people, about 86% of adults (Brazil's central bank, Aug 2026). The UK has still not decided whether to build a digital pound and says it will set out next steps later this year (the Bank of England, Mar 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Europe's own banks are the loudest critics. Germany's banking associations warn, in German, “Ein überladener Funktionsumfang führt zu Umsetzungsrisiken und bremst den Start.”, which reads in English as “An overloaded set of functions creates implementation risks and slows the launch.” (Germany's banking associations, Jun 2026) They fear the digital euro will undercut Europe's own private schemes rather than Visa and Mastercard.

Counter-argument

The objection is that a capped digital euro will be too small to matter, and that Europe's private schemes such as Wero could achieve independence without it. German banks make that case (Germany's banking associations, Jun 2026). The Banque de France argues the opposite, that Wero will at first reach only a limited number of countries (the Banque de France, Apr 2026). The honest answer is that a €3,000 cap makes the digital euro an everyday payment tool, not a savings product, which is exactly what its designers intend.

4. AI agents can now pay for you, but nobody has decided who pays when they get it wrong

Near-Term

An AI agent is software that acts for a person rather than just advising them: it can compare, choose and pay. Payment rules were written on the assumption that a human presses the button. Those rules decide who carries the loss when a payment is unauthorised, and they do not yet say what counts as authorised when a person has handed an agent a budget.

  • A Bank of England deputy governor says agents are mostly used to recommend so far, with people still making the payment, and that disputes and liability are the biggest open questions (the Bank of England, Jun 2026).
  • Under UK rules the payment firm usually carries the loss for unauthorised payments, but a monthly budget given to an agent does not authorise any single payment, so the answer is unclear (Pinsent Masons, Jul 2026).
  • “The report finds there is already consumer appetite for the use of agentic AI in personal finance, with research commissioned by the FCA showing that a fifth of people – equivalent to 11 million UK adults – are likely to use AI that can act autonomously within pre-set goals.” (the FCA, Jul 2026)
  • Hong Kong's regulators received nearly 100 proposals and picked 36 projects from 30 financial firms for a supervised test focused on AI agents, covering payments from start to finish (the Hong Kong Monetary Authority, Aug 2026).
  • “Agents could orchestrate as much as $5 trillion in global consumer spending by 2030, according to a January report from McKinsey.” (Fortune, Sep 2026) Ant International, Visa and Mastercard are working on a way to verify agents, which the industry calls know your agent.

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Global regulators are asking the question, not yet answering it. The FSB's draft practices for responsible AI ask explicitly whether they deal with agentic AI (the FSB, Jun 2026). The final report is due in October.

Counter-argument

The counter-case is that agents will stay advisers for years, because people do not trust them with money: only a quarter of US online adults do (Forrester, Nov 2025). That is likely true for now. But the rules will be set during the cautious years, not after, and the firms building agent payments are already agreeing standards among themselves (Fortune, Sep 2026).

5. Ordinary savers are now carrying the risk of lending that moved out of banks

Immediate

Private credit means loans made by investment funds instead of banks, mostly to companies. It has grown into a $1.5 to $2 trillion market. The new development is who holds it: funds sold to ordinary investors, promising they can withdraw a slice of their money each quarter. In early 2026 that promise was tested for the first time, and most managers limited what savers could take out.

  • “Across FSB members, the available data captures direct exposures of around $220 billion of drawn and undrawn bank credit lines to private credit funds, while some commercial estimates range from $270-$500 billion.” (the FSB, May 2026) The sector is estimated at $1.5 to $2 trillion.
  • Withdrawal requests at funds sold to individuals rose sharply in early 2026, and more money went out than came in for the first time (the Federal Reserve, May 2026). At one Blue Owl fund investors asked to withdraw 40.7% of shares (Investment Executive, Apr 2026).
  • The Bank of England reports several retail funds limiting withdrawals and is testing how stress could spread; private credit's share of AI financing rose from 9% to 34% in a year, on an OECD estimate (the Bank of England, Jul 2026).
  • “Although liquidity mismatches in private credit remain limited, rising borrower stress and growing retail exposure could test semiliquid structures.” (the IMF, Apr 2026) France's market regulator says the sector is not yet a significant risk but that opening it to retail savers raises concerns about liquidity and valuation (France's AMF, Jun 2026).
  • The rules have gaps. “Since AIFMD II's private credit-specific measures are only applicable in relation to AIFs that originate loans, loan-participating funds do not fall within the scope.” (the Oxford Business Law Blog, Sep 2026) Meanwhile US regulators propose lowering the capital banks must hold (Holland & Knight, Jun 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal AI is being financed through private credit. The Bank of England, citing the OECD, reports that private credit's share of AI investment financing rose from 9% in 2024 to 34% in 2025 (the Bank of England, Jul 2026). A slowdown in AI spending would now reach private credit funds first.

Counter-argument

Banks look safe from this. “ECB supervisory data estimate that euro area banks' exposures to private credit worldwide total €62.5 billion, which is 0.2% of total assets or 2.5% of total equity.” (the ECB, May 2026), and bank losses in the ECB's stress test stay small. Federal Reserve staff reach a similar view for US banks (Federal Reserve staff, May 2025). The theme accepts that. Its point is that the risk has moved to savers in funds that promise easy withdrawals from assets that cannot be sold quickly.

Strategic Implications

Four implications, each tied to one or more themes and each with a posture. Decide means the choice is live now and putting it off is itself a choice. Prepare means the work has to start now for a decision that comes later. Monitor means the evidence does not yet justify acting, and says what would change that.

SI 1: Decide how you will keep deposits: with rewards, with tokens, or both

The US law that would have settled interest has stalled (American Banker, Sep 2026), and the UK and Korea are drawing the line between interest, which is banned, and rewards for use, which are allowed (the Bank of England, Jun 2026) (Korea's National Assembly Budget Office, Sep 2026). Estimates of the effect on bank lending range from almost nothing (the White House Council of Economic Advisers, Sep 2026) to $60 to $126 billion for every $100 billion that leaves banks and does not come back (the Federal Reserve, Dec 2025). Meanwhile more than half of the largest US banks are building or evaluating tokenized deposits (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026), and shared networks are forming (The Clearing House, Jun 2026) (Swift, Jul 2026).

Action: Model your deposit base against both lending estimates and identify which customers would move for a payments reward. Then decide whether to match rewards, partner with a stablecoin issuer, or offer a tokenized deposit, and if you build a token, join a shared network first so it can pay customers of other banks.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 2

SI 2: Write your own rules for AI agents that pay before regulators write them for you

The Bank of England says its rules were not built for agents that act on their own (the Bank of England, Jun 2026), a US banking group warns the usual cap on customer losses may not apply (the Consumer Bankers Association, Jan 2026), and the FSB's final AI report is due in October (the FSB, Jun 2026). Until the rules change, each firm's own terms decide who carries the loss.

Action: Set out, in customer terms, what an agent may do with a customer's money, how a customer withdraws that permission, and who pays when an agent's payment is disputed. Join the industry work on verifying agents (Fortune, Sep 2026) rather than inventing a private standard.

SI 3: Plan for a capped digital euro from 2029

The pilot starts in the second half of 2027 (the ECB, Jul 2026) and public launch is aimed at 2029 (the Banque de France, Apr 2026). The cap under discussion is €3,000 per person, which makes it a payment tool rather than a place to save (OMFIF, Sep 2026). Firms outside the euro area should watch whether the UK decides to build a digital pound (the Bank of England, Mar 2026).

Action: Watch three triggers: the final holding cap agreed between Parliament and member states, the fee model for shops and banks, and the UK's decision on a digital pound. Any one of them settled moves this to Prepare.

Monitor Draws on Theme 3

SI 4: Find where your savings or balance sheet rely on private credit withdrawals

Most managers capped withdrawals at 5% when requests surged (the Federal Reserve, May 2026), and the Bank of England is testing how stress could spread from these funds (the Bank of England, Jul 2026). Bank credit lines to the funds run to around $220 billion (the FSB, May 2026). The pressure point is the promise of easy withdrawal from loans that cannot be sold quickly.

Action: List every product, fund or credit line you hold or sell that promises regular withdrawals from private credit, and check what happens to customers and to you if withdrawals are capped for a year.

Scenario Matrix

Two uncertainties shape where money lives in 2030. The first is how fast money leaves bank accounts for stablecoins, tokens, digital cash and funds. The second is whether the rules for these new homes are joined up across countries and products, or written separately by each.

Fast shift, joined-up rules

Everyone builds their own

Banks, card networks and governments each build their own tokens and systems, and few connect (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026). Money moves slowly because nothing works everywhere. Customers see little change by 2030 beyond faster payments.

Early indicators: Bank token schemes launch without links to each other; the digital euro is delayed; no agreed standard for agents.

The rush without guard rails

Generous rewards pull money into stablecoins, agents handle a growing share of spending, and retail funds keep selling easy withdrawals. Protections lag. The first big agent fraud or fund freeze decides the rules after the event (the Consumer Bankers Association, Jan 2026).

Early indicators: Reward rates on digital dollars rise; agent payment volumes grow before liability rules change; more funds cap withdrawals (the Federal Reserve, May 2026).

A managed upgrade

Deposit tokens connect through shared networks and central bank bridges (the ECB, Aug 2026), the digital euro launches with a modest cap, and regulators agree how agents are authorised. The bank account survives, upgraded.

Early indicators: Cross-bank token payments go live; the FSB's final AI report sets expectations for agents; the digital euro deal is agreed on time.

Money everywhere, protected

Money moves quickly into new forms, but the protections move with it: clear rules on rewards, agents and fund withdrawals. The hardest and most expensive outcome to build, and the only one in which speed does not cost customers their protection.

Early indicators: Liability rules for agent payments written into law; rewards regulated consistently across the US, UK and EU; fund withdrawal promises matched to what the assets can bear.

What We Are Not Planning For

Out of scope this cycle

A US central bank digital dollar

The United States has ruled out a retail digital dollar, as the July edition recorded, and nothing in this cycle's evidence reopens it. The US question is about private digital dollars and bank tokens.

↺ Reinstated if a US administration or Congress reopens the question of a retail central bank digital currency.

Out of scope this cycle

Crypto prices and speculative tokens

This briefing is about money people use to pay and save, not about crypto assets held as investments. Price swings in those assets matter here only if they spill into stablecoins or bank funding.

↺ Reinstated if a price shock in crypto assets causes withdrawals from a major stablecoin or a bank.

Deliberately not modelled

A collapse of a major stablecoin

A run on one of the largest stablecoins would test every theme here at once, but nothing in the evidence points to one now, and the new regimes in the US and UK require high-quality backing (the Bank of England, Jun 2026). Modelling it would crowd out the more likely story, which is gradual movement rather than a sudden break.

↺ Reinstated if a large stablecoin trades materially below one dollar for more than a day, or an issuer suspends redemptions.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. If a customer's AI agent makes a payment they did not want, who pays today under our terms, and would we be comfortable defending that answer in public (Pinsent Masons, Jul 2026)?
  2. How much of our deposit base would move for a payments reward, now that most governments ban interest on digital dollars but allow rewards (the Bank of England, Jun 2026)?
  3. Which shared token network will we join, and what would it cost us to be the bank whose tokens cannot pay customers of other banks (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026)?
  4. What does a €3,000 digital euro do to our payments income in the euro area after 2029 (OMFIF, Sep 2026)?
  5. Where do we promise customers easy withdrawals from assets that cannot be sold quickly, and what happens if we have to cap them for a year (the Federal Reserve, May 2026)?

Claim-fidelity self-disclosure

This appendix records where the evidence is thinner than a source count suggests, and what was done about it.

Plainer language, same evidence. At the commissioning analyst's request this edition uses less technical language than the July edition. Technical terms are explained where they first appear. No claim was simplified beyond what its source says; where a figure is summarised, the source's own wording is kept in the evidence record.

One figure is cited rather than quoted. The Banco Central do Brasil figure for Pix users is cited but not quoted, because the original sentence contains a dash that this house style does not print and a quotation may not be altered (Brazil's central bank, Aug 2026).

Industry and lobby sources are labelled. The American Bankers Association (the American Bankers Association, Apr 2026), the Consumer Bankers Association (the Consumer Bankers Association, Jan 2026) and Germany's banking associations (Germany's banking associations, Jun 2026) argue positions. They are used to show those positions, not as neutral evidence. The Flagship count of banks building tokenized deposits comes from a payments consultancy's reading of public statements (Flagship Advisory Partners, Aug 2026).

Forecasts are marked as forecasts. The $5 trillion figure for agent-led spending by 2030 is a McKinsey projection reported by Fortune (Fortune, Sep 2026), and dates after September 2026 in the timeline are targets set by the bodies concerned.

Two older sources are used on purpose. A Federal Reserve staff note from May 2025 on bank lending to private credit (Federal Reserve staff, May 2025) is carried as a standing reference, and a Federal Reserve note from December 2025 on deposits and stablecoins (the Federal Reserve, Dec 2025) provides the counter-estimate to the White House study.

Quotation. Twenty-four sources are quoted verbatim, two of them in the original language: French and German. Each non-English quotation is reproduced in the original, the language is named, and an English rendering follows. Japanese, Portuguese, Chinese and Korean sources are cited, not quoted. The Korean parliamentary budget office report is held in attachments that could not be checked against the live page, so it is used only as directional support (Korea's National Assembly Budget Office, Sep 2026).
