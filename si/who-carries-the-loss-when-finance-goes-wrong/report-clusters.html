Why This Matters Now

When finance goes wrong, someone carries the loss, and this autumn the authorities agree that the next loss is less likely to start in the banks than in the funds, pension schemes and payment systems around them.

Companies building artificial intelligence are borrowing heavily, and their shares and bonds sit in the funds that hold ordinary savings. Private credit funds are limiting how much savers can take out. Governments are losing the patient buyers of their long-term debt. New rules are moving the cost of scams onto platforms and phone networks, while payments made by AI agents have no rule at all. And countries are building their own controls into new kinds of money.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: find out how much of the money you are responsible for could be locked in a fund, or caught in a payment dispute, just when you need it, and watch whether withdrawal requests at private credit funds start rising again.

Executive Synthesis

Which decisions on your desk this week assume a fall in AI shares would stay in the stock market?

The evidence in this briefing says it would travel through funds, pensions and credit.

The authorities that watch the financial system agree on one point this autumn: banks are well placed to absorb a shock, and the risk now sits with the funds, insurers, pension schemes and savers around them. The European Central Bank finds vulnerabilities more pronounced in certain non-banks (European Central Bank, via the Banque de France, Nov 2026); the IMF warns that a correction in AI-related asset prices would be made worse by leverage and by insurers’ and pension funds’ illiquid holdings (International Monetary Fund, Oct 2026). Debt raised for advanced computing passed $680 billion this year (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026). September’s briefing asked where money will live; this edition asks who carries the loss when it is lost.

Three things are open. Will easing withdrawal requests at private credit funds hold, with $10.7 billion of requests already unmet (Knowledge at Wharton, Oct 2026)? Who will buy long-dated government debt as more than €900bn of Dutch pension assets move to new contracts in 2027 (ING, Jun 2026)? And who pays when an AI agent overspends, when no clear legal rule says (Payments Dive, Oct 2026)?

Looking ahead to 2030, major US technology firms’ cumulative capital spending is projected to reach $5.5 trillion (Lovguiden, on the European Central Bank, Nov 2026); private credit lending is expected to approach $4 trillion (IQ-EQ, on Moody’s, Oct 2026); and global public debt is projected to exceed 100% of GDP before 2030 (Bernama, on the IMF, Oct 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that losses from a fall in AI shares would travel into funds and savings rather than stopping in share prices. Withdrawal requests at private credit funds trended lower this autumn (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026). If a correction stays in the stock market and the funds’ limits hold, savers see paper losses, not locked-up money, and this briefing overstates the strain.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Set a limit on how much savers’ and company money sits in funds that can stop withdrawals. Once a fund limits withdrawals, the decision is made for you.
  2. Test portfolios and pension funds against a fall in AI shares that spreads to credit and government bonds. The warnings describe the three moving together.
  3. Agree with your partners who pays when a scam or an agent’s payment goes wrong. Terms written now are cheaper than terms set by the first dispute.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. A fall in AI share prices would reach savers through funds and pensions

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Investors’ own sentiment; central banks and securities regulators on the rules for funds and hedge funds · Room to act: Moderate: savers, trustees and fund boards choose how much they hold and how quickly they could sell

A fall in technology shares used to be a stock-market story. It is becoming a savings story: the companies building AI now borrow heavily, and their shares and bonds sit in the funds, pension schemes and insurers that hold ordinary people’s money. The banks look strong; the money around them is where a fall would travel.

Sources: (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026); (European Central Bank, Aug 2026); (International Monetary Fund, Oct 2026); (European Central Bank, via the Banque de France, Nov 2026). The three figures measure different things (banks’ exposure, households’ holdings, insurers’ and pension funds’ asset mix) and do not add up.

  • The build-out runs on debt. Debt raised for advanced computing passed $680 billion by October 2026, and outstanding public debt related to it is more than $1.1 trillion (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026). AI firms’ share of investment-grade bond issuance has risen from 2% in 2023 to 10% this year (Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, Oct 2026).
  • The exposure sits with savers. Euro area households have around €440 billion of exposure to US technology shares, and most euro area exposure to the largest US technology firms runs through investment funds (European Central Bank, Aug 2026); those funds have significantly increased their AI exposure in both shares and bonds (Lovguiden, on the European Central Bank, Nov 2026).
  • Losses would spread between sectors. The IMF warns that weaker AI investment or profits could trigger a correction, made worse by leverage, and that insurers and pension funds have grown their illiquid holdings, raising the chance of forced sales (International Monetary Fund, Oct 2026); the Bundesbank says funds are ever more connected with banks, insurers and pension institutions, so losses can spread faster between them (Bundesbank, in German, Nov 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The Bank of England may make AI turmoil the subject of its next system-wide stress test. AI turmoil is among the options being discussed for the Bank’s next exercise (Insurance Journal, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Spending: Cumulative capital spending by major US technology firms is projected to reach $5.5 trillion by 2030, increasingly financed with debt (Lovguiden, on the European Central Bank, Nov 2026).

Obligations: Beyond their bonds and loans, the firms have taken on residual value guarantees whose undiscounted values exceed $2 trillion (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026).

What it means: Assume an AI correction would show up first in fund prices and withdrawal limits, and size savings and pension holdings for that.

Counter-argument

Banks are well placed to withstand shocks (European Central Bank, via the Banque de France, Nov 2026), and the authorities describe a channel, not a forecast: if AI earnings keep up with expectations, prices hold and the funds never face the redemptions this theme describes.

On the System Map: loop R1 (forced sales feed falling prices) is priming: funds hold more AI shares and debt, and insurers and pension funds more illiquid assets, but no fall has started; leverage point 2 sits on the combined stress test.

2. Savers in private credit funds will find it harder to get their money out

Near-Term

Weight: high · Who decides: Fund managers and boards, who set withdrawal limits; securities regulators and central banks on how the funds are sold and supervised · Room to act: High for savers and advisers before they invest; low once a fund has limited withdrawals

Private credit funds lend directly to companies, and many have been sold to savers with the promise of regular, limited withdrawals. More savers now ask for their money back than the funds will pay, so the rest must wait, while the funds’ own promises to lend keep running.

Source: (Knowledge at Wharton, Oct 2026), reporting research on 52 semi-liquid funds.

  • Withdrawal limits are biting. Semi-liquid private credit funds had their first net outflow in the second quarter, $7.1 billion paid out against $3.8 billion of new money, and $10.7 billion of requests has been left unmet by their 5% cap (Knowledge at Wharton, Oct 2026). Blackstone’s flagship fund received requests of about $4.3 billion, roughly 10% of its shares, in the third quarter (CRE Daily, Sep 2026) and turned away about half (CRE Daily, Nov 2026).
  • The pressure is broad. Fitch counts third-quarter requests averaging 9.8% of shares at 13 of 15 such funds that never list on an exchange (InvestmentNews, on Fitch, Oct 2026); the Bank of England says redemptions at foreign private credit funds sold to retail and wealth investors remained elevated (Bank of England, Sep 2026).
  • There is little cash for exits. Semi-liquid funds hold cash of only 5.5% of net assets on average (Knowledge at Wharton, Oct 2026), and nearly 90% of bank lending to such funds is in credit lines they can draw (Federal Reserve, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Funds that cap what savers can take out still owe their borrowers money on demand. Some direct-lending funds have committed to provide funding on demand (International Monetary Fund, Oct 2026): their promises to borrowers run on a different clock from savers’ withdrawals.

Outlook to 2030

Growth: Moody’s expects private credit lending to approach $4 trillion by 2030 (IQ-EQ, on Moody’s, Oct 2026).

Tests: Its links with banks are channels for risk that have yet to be tested in a sharp tightening of financial conditions, the IMF says (International Monetary Fund, Oct 2026).

What it means: Treat money in these funds as available over quarters, not weeks, and match it only to needs that can wait.

Counter-argument

Requests are easing. The Federal Reserve says requests at certain of these funds trended lower (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026), and Goldman Sachs’ fund received requests for 2% of its shares (PitchBook News, Sep 2026). Paying out half of requests is the funds’ terms working, not failing.

On the System Map: loop R2 (queues prompt more requests) is turning: requests near 10% against a 5% cap leave a queue that gives savers a reason to ask early; leverage point 1 sits on limits for money in these funds.

3. Governments will pay more to borrow as their most patient lenders step back

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Finance ministries and debt offices on what they issue; central banks on how fast they shrink their holdings; pension funds and insurers on what they buy · Room to act: Low over the rate itself; moderate over when, and for how long, organisations borrow

Central banks, pension funds and life insurers used to hold long-dated government bonds for the life of the loan. All three are stepping back at once, and the buyers replacing them sell faster, making long-term borrowing dearer and less predictable for governments and for everyone whose loans are priced off theirs.

Sources: (Reuters, in Japanese, Oct 2026); (UK Debt Management Office, Oct 2026); (De Nederlandsche Bank, in Dutch, Oct 2026); (ING, Jun 2026); (Bernama, on the IMF, Oct 2026). The debt projection is the IMF’s, for the world as a whole.

  • Flightier buyers are replacing the central bank. As the Eurosystem reduces its presence in euro area bond markets, the buyer base is shifting towards foreign investors, who tend to be flightier in stress, and hedge funds (European Central Bank, Nov 2026); the ECB says this raises the risk of abrupt repricing (European Central Bank, via the Banque de France, Nov 2026).
  • Pension funds and insurers are buying less long debt. More than €900bn of Dutch pension assets move to new contracts in 2027, with less need for hedges of 30 years and beyond (ING, Jun 2026). In Japan, the 30-year yield hit a record 4.25% in early October, and four large life insurers, including Nippon Life and Dai-ichi Life, expect to cut their yen bond holdings (Reuters, in Japanese, Oct 2026).
  • Britain is borrowing shorter. The Debt Management Office cut planned long conventional gilt sales by £7.7 billion and cancelled three long auctions in October (UK Debt Management Office, Oct 2026); overseas investors now hold more than 30% of gilts (BlackRock, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A 4% yield is bringing some patient buyers back. Meiji Yasuda says it will keep buying 30-year bonds at around 4% whether rates rise further or stall (Reuters, in Japanese, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Debt: Global public debt is projected to exceed 100% of GDP before 2030 (Bernama, on the IMF, Oct 2026).

Pensions: The Dutch transition period ends on 1 January 2028 (De Nederlandsche Bank, in Dutch, Oct 2026).

What it means: Plan long-term borrowing, pension funding and property finance on long rates staying at today’s level or higher through 2028.

Counter-argument

Markets have continued to function in an orderly manner (European Central Bank, via the Banque de France, Nov 2026), and hedge funds absorb government issuance and provide liquidity (Hedgeweek, Nov 2026). If inflation falls and rates are cut, the loss of patient buyers may cost governments little.

On the System Map: loop B1 (higher yields bring buyers back) is priming: a record 4.25% in Japan is drawing some insurers back, while the Dutch transition removes others; leverage point 2 sits on the combined stress test, which includes a rise in long yields.

4. The cost of scams and payment mistakes will be shared beyond the banks

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Payment regulators and governments; banks, card schemes, large platforms and phone networks; AI companies on their agents’ terms · Room to act: Moderate: firms can agree loss-sharing terms with partners and set limits for agents before the rules arrive

When a payment goes wrong, someone covers the loss. That cost used to sit mainly with customers and banks. Governments are now spreading it to the platforms and phone networks where scams start, and payments made by software have no rule yet at all.

  • Making banks repay has cut fraud. UK push-payment fraud losses fell by an estimated £73 million a year after reimbursement became mandatory (Payment Systems Regulator, Jul 2026). But 30% of that fraud by value and 69% by volume starts on online platforms (Bratby Law, Jul 2026), and the regulator has no powers to make them cooperate (Grant Thornton, Sep 2026).
  • Others are being made to share the cost. Australia fines banks, phone networks and platforms up to $50 million per breach (Dynamic Business, Jul 2026); the EU’s new rules make payment providers refund impersonation scams in full, with the texts due at the end of 2026 (Addleshaw Goddard, Sep 2026).
  • Payments by AI agents have no rule. There is no clear legal rule for who bears the loss when an AI tool exceeds its mandate (Payments Dive, Oct 2026), and HM Treasury concedes the UK’s payment rules may not fully work for agents (HM Treasury, Jul 2026). Meanwhile OpenAI has launched a wallet for agent purchases at retailers’ checkouts (Digiday, Nov 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Retailers are writing agents’ spending limits before regulators do. Walmart lets customers cap an agent’s spending at $50, $100 or $200. Amazon has blocked Meta’s shopping agent from its site (Digiday, Nov 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Agents: AI agents could account for 15-25% of US online sales by 2030, worth $300-500 billion, on Bain’s forecast (commercetools, on Bain, Oct 2026).

Rules: Full application of the EU’s new payment rules will likely extend well into 2028 (Addleshaw Goddard, Sep 2026).

What it means: Agree in contracts now who pays when a scam or an agent’s payment goes wrong, rather than leaving it to the first dispute.

Counter-argument

In card disputes issuers tend to side with the cardholder (Worldpay, Jul 2026), and Grant Thornton finds the net benefit of reimbursement may be close to zero once displaced scams are counted (Grant Thornton, Sep 2026), which argues for prevention rather than moving the bill.

On the System Map: loop B2 (refunds push prevention upstream) is turning: refund rules in the UK, the EU and Australia are pushing prevention onto platforms and phone networks; leverage point 3 sits on loss-sharing terms.

5. Countries will police the platforms where money crosses their borders

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: Central banks and finance ministries; regulators in emerging markets on stablecoin platforms; lawmakers on who may issue · Room to act: Low over the rules; moderate over which platforms and payment routes an organisation depends on

Governments are putting checkpoints on the platforms where local money is swapped for digital dollars: who sends money, to whose wallet and how much. Whether the checks hold the money back is less clear. For anyone who pays across borders, the platforms will ask more questions.

  • Emerging markets are policing the platforms. Since 1 October, Brazil has barred stablecoins from settling payments between foreign-exchange providers and their counterparties abroad (FCM Law, in Portuguese, Oct 2026). Thailand’s draft rules cap stablecoin transfers through a licensed platform at 5 million baht per customer per day (Securities and Exchange Commission, Thailand, Nov 2026), and Nigeria treats value sent to an unverified wallet as a cross-border transaction (Central Bank of Nigeria, Nov 2026).
  • Others are choosing who may issue. Korea’s draft law would require any consortium issuing a won stablecoin to be majority-owned by banks (the “51% rule”), and the bill has been stuck for more than a year (IB Tomato, in Korean, Aug 2026). Britain has instead rejected limits on what people hold, capping each systemic stablecoin’s issuance (Bank of England, Jun 2026).
  • The checks may not hold the money. Research across more than 130 economies finds stablecoin flows largely unaffected by capital-flow restrictions (Bank for International Settlements, Jul 2026), and stablecoins’ cost advantage in cross-border payments reflects capital controls rather than the technology (Bank for International Settlements, Oct 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Thailand would ban stablecoin transfers to other people’s wallets. Its draft rules allow transfers only to an account or wallet verified as belonging to the same customer (Securities and Exchange Commission, Thailand, Nov 2026): the platform becomes a border post.

Outlook to 2030

Euro: The ECB plans a pilot in 2027 and first issuance of a digital euro in 2029, with its holding limit a design choice to be set later (European Central Bank, Jul 2026).

Limits: Britain will cap each systemic stablecoin at an initial maximum issuance of £40 billion (Bank of England, Jun 2026).

What it means: Expect the platforms on each cross-border route to ask more questions by 2030, and keep a second route for payments that cannot wait.

Counter-argument

The direction is not one way. The IMF wants Kenya to let foreign stablecoin issuers keep reserves abroad (Business Daily Africa, Nov 2026), and the dollar still holds 56.70% of world reserves (International Monetary Fund, Sep 2026). If the checks prove easy to route around, governments may give up on them rather than tighten them.

On the System Map: no loop of its own yet: platform checks are an outside switch that acts on every route money takes, and they keep some savers’ money at home; leverage point 4 sits on tracking the checks.

System Map

Read together, the five themes describe one question: when something goes wrong, whose balance sheet takes the hit. Four loops connect them. Forced sales feed falling prices (R1): a fall in AI shares prompts withdrawals, and funds that sell to meet them push prices down further. Queues prompt more requests (R2): withdrawal limits leave a queue that gives savers a reason to ask early. Higher yields bring buyers back (B1): as patient buyers of government bonds step back, yields rise until some return. Refunds push prevention upstream (B2): rules that make platforms and phone networks share scam losses give them a reason to stop scams.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Set a limit on how much savers’ and company money sits in funds that can stop withdrawals

Semi-liquid private credit funds are paying out about half of what savers ask for (CRE Daily, Nov 2026), hold cash of only 5.5% of net assets (Knowledge at Wharton, Oct 2026), while private credit lending is expected to approach $4 trillion by 2030 (IQ-EQ, on Moody’s, Oct 2026). Trustees, wealth managers and treasurers should decide now how much of the money they look after can wait several quarters.

Action: Investment committees and treasurers to set, and tell clients, a maximum share of client or company money in funds that can limit withdrawals, by 31 March 2027.

Decide Draws on Themes 2 and 1. On the System Map: leverage point 1, money held in funds with withdrawal limits, where loop R2 (queues prompt more requests) can be slowed.

Implication 2: Test portfolios and pension funds against a fall in AI shares that spreads to credit and government bonds

Debt raised for advanced computing passed $680 billion this year (Federal Reserve, Nov 2026), euro area households hold around €440 billion of US technology shares mostly through funds (European Central Bank, Aug 2026), and long-dated government bonds are losing their patient buyers (European Central Bank, Nov 2026). Investment chiefs and trustees should model a fall that hits shares, corporate bonds and long gilts together.

Action: Chief investment officers to run one combined test (AI shares down, AI-related credit repriced, long yields up) and report the result to the board by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Themes 1 and 3. On the System Map: leverage point 2, the combined stress test, which exposes loop R1 (forced sales feed falling prices) before it turns.

Implication 3: Agree who pays when a scam or an AI agent’s payment goes wrong, before the rules decide for you

30% of UK push-payment fraud by value starts on online platforms (Bratby Law, Jul 2026), the EU’s new rules are due at the end of 2026 (Addleshaw Goddard, Sep 2026), and no clear legal rule says who bears an agent’s mistake (Payments Dive, Oct 2026). Banks, payment firms, platforms and retailers should write loss-sharing terms with their partners while they can still shape them.

Action: Heads of payments and fraud to agree loss-sharing terms with their three largest platform or agent partners, and set agent spending limits, by 30 September 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 4. On the System Map: leverage point 3, loss-sharing terms, where loop B2 (refunds push prevention upstream) decides who invests in stopping scams.

Implication 4: Track which countries put checks on the platforms your cross-border payments use

Brazil, Thailand and Nigeria are putting checks on stablecoin platforms (Securities and Exchange Commission, Thailand, Nov 2026), though research finds such controls often fail to hold the money (Bank for International Settlements, Jul 2026). Treasurers and payment firms should keep a register of the checks on each route they use, so a new rule is a known cost rather than a surprise.

Action: Treasury and payments teams to keep a quarterly register of checks on their cross-border routes, starting with Thailand’s rules after its consultation closes on 26 November 2026.

Monitor Draws on Themes 5 and 4. On the System Map: leverage point 4, the controls on each route, an outside switch that acts on every loop.

The Shared Safety Net

↗ Shift from todayFrom banks judged well placed to absorb shocks (European Central Bank, via the Banque de France, Nov 2026) to central banks and deposit schemes that stand behind funds and pension schemes too.

In 2030 money moves freely, but the state has widened its protection. Central banks lend to funds in a crisis, refund rules cover new kinds of payment, and hedge funds face limits on borrowing. Savers are protected; the price is tighter rules and lower returns.

Early indicators: Central banks open lending for funds; limits on hedge funds’ borrowing are adopted; refund rules are extended to payments made by agents.

Open Season for Risk

↗ Shift from todayFrom $1.9 trillion in US private credit vehicles (Federal Reserve, Oct 2026) to savers holding most of the economy’s risky lending directly, with money free to move.

In 2030 savers carry most of the risk of lending to companies and governments, through funds, pensions and wallets they can move freely. Returns are higher, but losses land on savers directly, withdrawal limits are routine, and long-term rates swing with investors’ mood.

Early indicators: Private credit keeps growing fast; withdrawal limits become routine; long-term yields stay volatile.

Fortress Finance

↗ Shift from todayFrom a 56.70% dollar share of world reserves (International Monetary Fund, Sep 2026) to national currencies, public digital money and controls that keep savings at home and protected.

In 2030 governments keep their savers’ money at home and protect it. Public digital money, bank tokens and checks on platforms keep payments in national lanes, and states lean on domestic pension funds to hold their bonds. Losses are contained; cross-border payments are slower and dearer.

Early indicators: More countries cap or license stablecoin platforms; governments push domestic holdings of their bonds; the digital euro launches with a low limit.

Locked In

↗ Shift from todayFrom Thailand’s draft cap of 5 million baht a day on stablecoin transfers (Securities and Exchange Commission, Thailand, Nov 2026) to savers carrying losses in funds and markets they cannot easily leave.

In 2030 money moves in national lanes, but the state does not stand behind it. Savers carry the losses of the funds they hold, while withdrawal limits and border checks make it hard to move money out. Those with cash and choices do well; those in illiquid funds wear the losses.

Early indicators: Fund withdrawal limits last a year or more; controls spread to more currencies; long-dated bond sales are cut again.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes. In November the reading leans towards savers carrying losses, and towards money moving in national lanes.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

Central banks lend directly to investment funds in a crisis

Window: 2027 to 2033 · Probability band: Medium (40 to 60%)

Why it would surprise: Plans assume only banks can turn to a central bank for emergency cash, and that funds must sell assets instead.

The ECB’s Vice-President wants limits on hedge funds’ borrowing and larger cash buffers at money market funds (Lovguiden, on the European Central Bank, Nov 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: a G7 central bank opens a standing lending facility for non-bank funds; evidence: the central bank’s published notice.
  • By December 2029: a central bank lends to a private credit, property or bond fund during market stress; evidence: the central bank’s operations data.

What it touches today: Fund and pension liquidity plans that assume selling assets is the only way to raise cash.

Weak signal

Governments require pension funds and insurers to hold their bonds

Window: 2029 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Plans assume governments must compete on price for every buyer of their debt.

Britain cut its planned long gilt sales and cancelled three long auctions (UK Debt Management Office, Oct 2026), and global public debt is projected to exceed 100% of GDP before 2030 (Bernama, on the IMF, Oct 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2028: an advanced-economy government consults on minimum holdings of its own bonds for pension funds or insurers; evidence: the government’s consultation.
  • By December 2029: such a minimum holding is adopted in law or regulation in an advanced economy; evidence: the official gazette or the regulator’s rulebook.

What it touches today: Pension and insurance investment strategies, and the returns savers are promised from them.

Emerging

Platforms and phone networks, not banks, pay most of the cost of scams

Window: 2027 to 2032 · Probability band: Medium (40 to 60%)

Why it would surprise: Plans assume the bank that moves the money carries the loss, whatever happened before the payment.

Australia fines platforms and phone networks up to $50 million per breach (Dynamic Business, Jul 2026), and 30% of UK push-payment fraud by value starts on online platforms (Bratby Law, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: the UK government proposes to make online platforms or phone networks reimburse scam victims; evidence: a consultation or bill on gov.uk.
  • By December 2029: a large platform or phone network reports paying scam reimbursement under a legal duty in the EU, the UK or Australia; evidence: its annual report or the regulator’s data.

What it touches today: Fraud budgets and partnership contracts with platforms.
