  • Employers that redesign junior roles around AI can recruit from a deep pool of eager graduates. Competition for UK graduate jobs reached 141 applications per vacancy, even as graduate hiring grew again (Times Higher Education, Oct 2026). See Theme 1 →

Why This Matters Now

Work is changing in three ways at once: who gets a first job, who sets the rules for AI at work, and who is in charge of the AI tools now doing parts of people's jobs.

Young people are finding it harder to get first jobs in the work AI does well, while employers say they are changing those jobs rather than removing them. People have stopped moving between employers, so careers now have to be built inside organisations that train less than they used to. Unions and works councils are agreeing rules for AI before governments do, new duties on pay and contracts arrive before national laws are ready, and AI agents are being added to teams before anyone has agreed who manages them.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: decide where your experienced staff of 2030 will come from, and who is accountable for the AI that now does their first jobs.

Executive Synthesis

Which hiring plans on your desk this week assume today's junior roles will still train tomorrow's experienced staff?

The evidence here says first jobs in AI-exposed work are shrinking in three countries while employers redesign the roles.

Three questions now decide how work changes. Who gets hired? in the US, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed occupations is about 19% below less-exposed peers (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026), and in France permanent hiring of young people fell 28.3% in the most exposed jobs (HEC Paris, in French, Oct 2026), yet 87% of UK employers expect AI to change graduate roles rather than remove them (People Management, May 2026). Who sets the rules? Ahead of any EU law, 108 collective agreements in European services already contain AI provisions (Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung and UNI Europa, Oct 2026). Who manages the AI? Only 24% of leadership teams agree on who is accountable for AI agents (Gartner, Oct 2026). Underneath, the job market has stopped moving: US quits are at 1.9% (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Sep 2026), so careers must be built inside organisations whose training has grown shorter (Learning and Work Institute, Sep 2026).

Some of this is settled: the EU's AI rules for hiring apply from 2 December 2027 (Official Journal of the EU, Jul 2026), and the UK's six-month qualifying period for unfair dismissal from 1 January 2027 (UK Department for Business and Trade, Oct 2026). Four things are not. Is the squeeze on first jobs AI or the business cycle, when Germany's labour research institute finds the extra fall for young workers not statistically significant (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Oct 2026)? Will Germany's metal round, where warning strikes can begin on 1 November, settle on job security (ms-aktuell, in German, Oct 2026)? Will the Commission's Quality Jobs Act, expected in December, bring binding rules or only guidance (Council of the EU, Oct 2026)? And will job-switching revive, now that switchers' pay growth again outpaces stayers' (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Oct 2026)?

Looking ahead to 2030, the US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects office and administrative support, a classic first job, to shed 752,100 jobs by 2035 (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Aug 2026); Germany's labour research institute expects employment to fall again in 2027 (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Sep 2026); and 42% of companies in a BCG survey expect AI agents to make business decisions on their own by 2030 (Boston Consulting Group, in German, Sep 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 9 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that AI, rather than the business cycle, is squeezing first jobs. Germany's labour research institute finds the cycle is the main driver of the fall in young people's jobs (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Oct 2026), and UK graduate hiring grew 4% in 2025-26 (Times Higher Education, Oct 2026). If hiring recovers in 2027, the first-job problem eases on its own, and redesigning junior roles becomes a choice rather than a necessity.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Redesign junior roles before cutting graduate intake. The experienced staff of 2030 are hired this year.
  2. Agree AI terms with worker representatives while you can still shape them. Collective agreements are setting the rules ahead of EU law.
  3. Name an owner for every AI agent, with HR in the room. Few leadership teams agree who is accountable.

Each is developed in the Strategic Implications, with alternatives in the Options.

1. Fewer young people are getting a first job, and employers are redesigning junior work rather than deleting it

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Employers' HR and early-careers teams, within budgets set by boards · Room to act: High: employers choose how junior roles are redesigned and how many juniors they hire

Young people are finding first jobs harder to get in the occupations most exposed to AI, and the evidence now comes from three countries rather than one. Most employers say they are changing what junior staff do rather than removing the roles. The risk is not today's headcount but tomorrow's: the routine work that trained juniors is being absorbed, and the experienced staff of the 2030s come from today's intake.

Sources: (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026); (HEC Paris, in French, Oct 2026).

  • In the US, employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in highly AI-exposed occupations was about 19% below that of less-exposed peers by June 2026, up from 15% a year earlier, while experienced workers show no comparable gap (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026).
  • In France, permanent hiring of 21 to 29 year olds fell 28.3% in the occupations most exposed to AI between late 2022 and early 2026, against 7.5% in the least exposed, and fell about 42% for junior developers and IT engineers (HEC Paris, in French, Oct 2026).
  • Worldwide, youth unemployment rose to 12.4% in 2025, and the clerical and administrative jobs that young people traditionally start in have declined for them since 2023 (International Labour Organization, Aug 2026).
  • Employers say they are redesigning rather than deleting: 87% of UK employers expect AI to change graduate and apprentice roles over three years, and 43% say it has already altered entry-level roles that have not yet been formally redesigned (People Management, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Internships are being cut faster than graduate jobs. Internship and placement hiring fell 8% while applications for internships rose 27% (Times Higher Education, Oct 2026), so the route by which students gain experience before a first job is narrowing first.

Outlook to 2030

The US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects office and administrative support, a classic first-job group, to shed 752,100 jobs by 2035, a fall of 4.0%, and says AI is likely to reduce demand for several of these occupations (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Aug 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the bottleneck for young people has moved from getting a first job to gaining the experience that used to come with it.

What it means: Plan the 2027 intake on the experienced staff you will need in 2030, not on this year's savings from AI.

Counter-argument

The fall may be more cyclical than technological. Germany's labour research institute finds no general employment collapse in AI-exposed occupations: the extra fall for 20 to 29 year olds is 1.2 percentage points and not statistically significant, while young people's employment in weak-cycle sectors fell 4.2% (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Oct 2026). UK graduate hiring grew 4% in 2025-26 (Times Higher Education, Oct 2026), and in Japan there are still 1.62 graduate openings for every job-seeker (Recruit Works Institute, in Japanese, Apr 2026).

On the System Map: R1 Experience drought is turning: AI absorbs junior tasks, fewer juniors are hired, and the pool of experienced staff thins. Leverage point 1 sits where junior roles are redesigned.

2. The job market has stopped moving, so careers must be built inside organisations whose training often does not pay off

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Employers, inside a labour market set by interest rates and demography · Room to act: Moderate: employers cannot restart the job market, but they decide how people move and learn inside

People are staying put. Hiring and quitting have slowed across the largest economies, and shrinking labour supply means this is not only a downturn. If careers cannot be built by moving between employers, they must be built inside them, and the evidence says most organisations are not set up to do it: internal moves are slowing, and training has become shorter and aimed at compliance.

  • US quits were unchanged at 3.1 million, a rate of 1.9%, in August 2026, with the hires rate at 3.3% (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Sep 2026); the Federal Reserve's estimate of the monthly job growth needed to keep unemployment steady in 2026 is fewer than 10,000, partly reflecting lower immigration (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Oct 2026).
  • UK vacancies fell to 691,000 in July to September 2026, close to their lowest level outside the pandemic since 2014 (Office for National Statistics, Oct 2026), and in Japan the ratio of openings to applicants held at 1.18 while new openings fell 3.4% on a year earlier (Japan's labour ministry, in Japanese, Oct 2026).
  • Inside organisations, 57% of employers made fewer lateral internal hires than a year earlier, and only 63% of employees see a path to advancement where they work (Recruiting News Network, Oct 2026).
  • UK training has become shorter and more compliance-led: health and safety is the most common reason for training, and average training fell from 7.8 days per trainee in 2011 to 5.7 days in 2024 (Learning and Work Institute, Sep 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A frozen market may itself be cutting training. The Learning and Work Institute links lower labour turnover to less induction training (Learning and Work Institute, Sep 2026), so the slowdown can reduce the very training that internal careers depend on.

Outlook to 2030

Germany's labour research institute forecasts employment falling by 210,000 in 2026 and 140,000 in 2027 despite economic growth, as the labour force shrinks (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Sep 2026), and the US Bureau of Labor Statistics projects total employment to grow only 3.5% from 2025 to 2035 (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Aug 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 most progression happens inside organisations, and employers that cannot move people internally lose them to the few that can.

What it means: Treat internal moves, and training that leads to a named role, as the main career route, and measure both.

Counter-argument

Job-switchers still earn more. The Atlanta Fed's tracker shows pay growth of 4.7% for people who changed jobs against 3.4% for those who stayed, in September 2026 (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Oct 2026), and another Atlanta Fed analysis reads 2026 as an improvement on 2025 (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Oct 2026). The market is slow, not shut, and a rebound after interest-rate cuts would reopen it.

On the System Map: R2 Frozen market is turning: fewer people leave, so fewer vacancies open, so fewer people can move. Leverage point 2 sits on training and internal moves, the route the frozen market leaves open.

3. Bargaining is setting the rules for AI at work before the law does

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: Employers with unions and works councils; the European Commission on the Quality Jobs Act · Room to act: High: employers can negotiate AI terms now or have them set later

The rules on how AI is used at work are being written in collective and company agreements before they are written in law. Agreements already limit dismissals, require worker input and fund retraining. For employers the choice is not whether AI terms will be set, but whether they shape them now or accept terms set later in a dispute or by legislation.

  • A study of European services found 108 collective agreements that already contain provisions on AI, at a time when around 80% of managers in Europe use algorithmic management tools (Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung and UNI Europa, Oct 2026).
  • In France, Malakoff Humanis agreed with four unions that deploying an AI system cannot on its own justify individual or collective economic dismissal, alongside an internal AI school and retraining (Option Finance, in French, Sep 2026); in the US, Kaiser Permanente's agreement with unions representing about 62,000 workers commits to worker input on AI (MIT Sloan School of Management, Jul 2026).
  • Germany's metal and electrical round, covering more than 3.7 million employees, opened on 7 October; IG Metall seeks a 5% rise, a safeguard of future and employment and, as a new item, profit-sharing (IG Metall, in German, Sep 2026), and warning strikes can begin from 1 November (ms-aktuell, in German, Oct 2026).
  • The EU's second-stage consultation on a Quality Jobs Act, which covers algorithmic management and the use of AI at work, closed on 28 September, with the Commission's proposal expected before the end of 2026 (Council of the EU, Oct 2026); European unions call for binding legislation (WageIndicator Foundation, Oct 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Profit-sharing enters the AI bargain. IG Metall's demand for profit-sharing in firms in a good economic position is a new item in its claim (IG Metall, in German, Sep 2026). If it is won, a share of productivity gains becomes something workers negotiate rather than wait for.

Outlook to 2030

The Commission plans to publish the Quality Jobs Act in December 2026 (Council of the EU, Oct 2026), and its leading option for algorithmic management is guidance on existing data-protection and AI rules rather than new law (Lovguiden, Oct 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the detailed rules on AI at work in much of Europe sit in sector and company agreements, with law setting the floor.

What it means: Agree AI terms with worker representatives while you can still shape them.

Counter-argument

Bargaining reaches only part of the workforce. Only nine EU states have collective bargaining coverage above 80%, Germany covers barely half of employees, and many national plans to raise coverage are little more than declarations of intent (Hans-Böckler-Stiftung, in German, Jun 2026). The Kaiser Permanente agreement's preamble sets no enforceable guardrails (MIT Sloan School of Management, Jul 2026), and Germany's metal employers call their sector's situation a structural crisis (ms-aktuell, in German, Oct 2026).

On the System Map: B1 Bargaining brake is priming: more AI at work raises concern, concern produces agreements, and agreements set conditions on how AI is used. Leverage point 3 sits on collective agreements.

4. New duties on hours, pay and contracts arrive before most national laws are ready

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: National parliaments and ministries in the EU; the UK government · Room to act: Moderate: employers can prepare on the EU and UK dates; the national detail is not theirs to set

Duties on pay, hours and employment status are arriving on fixed dates while the national laws that set their detail lag. The EU's pay transparency deadline has passed with most countries late, the platform-work deadline is in December, and the UK's employment reforms begin in January. Employers have to prepare for duties whose final shape is still being written.

Sources: (Baker Tilly, Jul 2026); (Littler, Aug 2026); (Council of the EU, Oct 2026); (UK Department for Business and Trade, Oct 2026); (Dutch government, in Dutch, May 2026); (Ius Laboris, Sep 2026); (Official Journal of the EU, Jul 2026).

  • Only four member states, Italy, Slovakia, Lithuania and Malta, met the 7 June 2026 deadline for the Pay Transparency Directive in full (Baker Tilly, Jul 2026). Germany's government said in July that it was still clarifying open questions on its bill (German Bundestag, in German, Jul 2026), and France's bill reached the Council of Ministers only on 10 September (French government, in French, Sep 2026).
  • The Netherlands aims for 1 January 2027, with employers of 150 or more staff reporting their 2027 pay gaps by 7 June 2028 (Dutch government, in Dutch, May 2026), but its parliament will not debate the bill until the second week of January 2027 (Ius Laboris, Sep 2026).
  • The Platform Work Directive, which presumes that platform workers are employees unless shown otherwise, must be in national law by 2 December 2026, yet several member states have published no draft bill (Littler, Aug 2026).
  • In the UK, the qualifying period for unfair dismissal falls to six months for dismissals from 1 January 2027, fire-and-rehire protections arrive the same month, and the right to guaranteed hours is due in 2027 (UK Department for Business and Trade, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

The EU's rules for high-risk AI systems, which include AI used in recruitment and in managing workers, now apply from 2 December 2027 (Official Journal of the EU, Jul 2026), and the first Dutch pay-gap reports are due by 7 June 2028 (Dutch government, in Dutch, May 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 pay-gap reports and checks on AI hiring tools are routine in Europe, and the employers that started early will have closed their gaps before publishing them.

What it means: Prepare pay-gap analysis and contract changes on the EU and UK dates, and track national laws country by country.

Counter-argument

Late national laws may delay the duty in practice. German legislation is not expected before early 2027, and no formal Commission letters of notice had been published by late June (Baker Tilly, Jul 2026). In the US the direction is reversed: Colorado replaced its AI law with one that drops impact assessments and risk-management programmes, starting on 1 January 2027 (Littler, May 2026).

On the System Map: binding duties sit outside the loops but act on two of them: they slow unchecked use of AI in hiring, and they prompt the agreements that turn B1 Bargaining brake.

5. AI agents are joining the workforce before anyone is accountable for managing them

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Chief executives, chief HR officers and chief information officers · Room to act: High: organisations decide who owns agents and how they are counted

Organisations are adding AI agents that carry out work, while cutting the management layers that would oversee them. Few leadership teams agree on who is accountable for agents, and responsibility is defaulting to technology teams. For HR and line managers the question is whether they help set the rules for digital workers now, or inherit them later.

  • A July 2026 Gartner survey of 297 chief HR officers found only 24% of leadership teams aligned on who should be accountable for AI agents, while organisations are also eliminating some middle management layers (Gartner, Oct 2026).
  • About two thirds of chief information and technology officers (66% worldwide) say they are held accountable for AI systems they do not fully control, and only 11% feel ready for the scale of agent deployment expected in the next year (Mynavi, in Japanese, Aug 2026).
  • Around 41% of employees say their companies trimmed management layers last year (Fortune, Jun 2026), and the average manager had 12.1 direct reports in 2025 (HCAMag, May 2026).
  • Active agents in Microsoft's workplace software grew 15x in a year, and the company says IT becomes the control plane for agent operations (Microsoft, May 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal No company yet counts its agents. Accenture's latest annual report counts about 814,000 people and nearly 110,000 AI and data practitioners, but no AI agents (Accenture annual report, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Gartner expects an average Fortune 500 company to have over 150,000 agents in use by 2028, up from fewer than 15 in 2025 (Gartner, Apr 2026), and in BCG's survey 42% of companies expect AI agents to make business decisions on their own by 2030, while only 5% have the full controls needed (Boston Consulting Group, in German, Sep 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 large employers report agents alongside staff, and the function that owns the agent register shapes how work is organised.

What it means: Name an owner for every agent, and put HR in the room where the rules for agents are set.

Counter-argument

Adoption is earlier than the forecasts suggest. In Germany only 11% of companies that use, plan or discuss AI already use agents (Bitkom, in German, Sep 2026), and Gallup finds engagement falling most among managers, who are critical for AI adoption (Gallup, Apr 2026); cutting managers may slow the take-up of agents rather than speed it.

On the System Map: R3 Flattening loop is priming: agents let firms cut management layers, and fewer managers push more work to agents. Leverage point 4 sits on agents in use, where ownership is assigned.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Redesign junior roles before cutting graduate intake

First jobs in AI-exposed occupations are shrinking in the US (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026) and France (HEC Paris, in French, Oct 2026), while 87% of UK employers expect AI to change graduate and apprentice roles rather than remove them (People Management, May 2026). HR and business leaders should decide which junior tasks AI now does and replace them with work that still builds experience, before they set the 2027 intake.

Action: HR directors to agree a redesigned junior role for each main entry route, and the 2027 intake, by 31 March 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 1. On the System Map: leverage point 1, junior tasks done by AI, where R1 Experience drought can be slowed.

Implication 2: Make internal moves the main career route, with training tied to a named role

With US quits at 1.9% (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Sep 2026) and UK vacancies close to their lowest outside the pandemic since 2014 (Office for National Statistics, Oct 2026), people are staying put; yet 57% of employers made fewer lateral internal hires (Recruiting News Network, Oct 2026), and UK training averages 5.7 days per trainee (Learning and Work Institute, Sep 2026). People leaders should open more vacancies to internal candidates and tie training to the roles it leads to.

Action: People leaders to report internal fill rates, and training linked to named roles, to the board from the first quarter of 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 2. On the System Map: leverage point 2, training and internal moves, the route R2 Frozen market leaves open.

Implication 3: Agree AI terms with worker representatives before a dispute or a law sets them

108 collective agreements in European services already contain AI provisions (Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung and UNI Europa, Oct 2026), Germany's metal round can move to warning strikes from 1 November (ms-aktuell, in German, Oct 2026), the Commission's Quality Jobs Act is expected in December (Council of the EU, Oct 2026), and pay transparency and platform-work duties arrive before most national laws (Littler, Aug 2026). Employers should agree a framework for AI at work, covering consultation, retraining and job security, before terms are set for them.

Action: Chief HR officers to open talks on an AI framework with unions or works councils, or with an employee forum where none exists, by the end of March 2027.

Decide Draws on Themes 3 and 4. On the System Map: leverage point 3, collective agreements, where B1 Bargaining brake can be set by design rather than by dispute.

Implication 4: Name an owner for every AI agent, and count agents alongside people

Only 24% of leadership teams agree on who is accountable for AI agents (Gartner, Oct 2026), 66% of technology chiefs say they answer for AI they do not fully control (Mynavi, in Japanese, Aug 2026), and Gartner expects an average Fortune 500 company to run over 150,000 agents by 2028 (Gartner, Apr 2026). Boards should require a register of agents, with a named human owner for each, set jointly by HR and IT.

Action: Chief executives to set up a joint HR and IT register of AI agents, with a named owner for each, by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 5. On the System Map: leverage point 4, agents in use, where ownership decides how far R3 Flattening loop runs.

Sector Passports

↗ Shift from todayFrom 108 European collective agreements with AI terms (Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung and UNI Europa, Oct 2026) to sector-wide agreements that carry training and AI rights from one employer to the next.

In 2030 people move between employers again, and sector agreements travel with them: training credits, rules on AI and pay scales are set across an industry rather than inside one firm. Employers compete on the work they offer rather than on terms, and a worker's record of skills moves with them. Employers that put money into shared training pools gain; those relying on loyalty to one firm lose staff.

Early indicators: The US quits rate rises above 2.4%; sector agreements add clauses on AI and training; portable training accounts appear.

Free Agents

↗ Shift from todayFrom job-switchers' pay growth of 4.7% against 3.4% for stayers (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Oct 2026) to an open market where people build careers by moving, under AI terms each employer sets.

In 2030 the job market is moving again and employers set the terms for AI alone. Skilled people move often and are paid for it, while agents and contractors carry much of the routine work. Careers are built across employers, and people without early experience find it hard to start one. Employers win talent with pay and interesting work, but hold it briefly.

Early indicators: Quits and vacancies recover; EU rules on algorithmic management stay as guidance; hiring on skills rather than degrees spreads.

Bargained Careers

↗ Shift from todayFrom one French insurer's accord that AI cannot on its own justify dismissal (Option Finance, in French, Sep 2026) to agreements at most large European employers pairing AI with retraining.

In 2030 few people change employers, and careers are built inside organisations under agreements with worker representatives. AI is introduced with consultation, retraining and job-security terms. Change is slower but steadier, and employers that invested in internal academies and internal moves keep their people. Firms without bargaining partners face pressure to create them.

Early indicators: Germany's metal round settles with a transformation clause; the Quality Jobs Act includes binding consultation rules; internal fill rates rise.

Company Ladders

↗ Shift from todayFrom 57% of employers making fewer lateral internal hires (Recruiting News Network, Oct 2026) to careers that depend on each employer's own choices about agents and management layers.

In 2030 people stay put and employers decide alone how AI and agents change work. Some build strong internal ladders; many cut junior intake and management layers and lean on agents. Engagement falls where the ladders are missing, and the thin junior intakes of the mid-2020s show up as shortages of mid-level staff.

Early indicators: Quits stay below 2%; graduate intakes fall again; management layers keep shrinking.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes each month. In October no future leads: after sitting in Company Ladders in August, the reading has moved towards the centre.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

Companies report their AI agents alongside their staff

Window: 2029 to 2034 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Headcount reporting counts people; agents are treated as software, not as part of the workforce.

Gartner expects an average Fortune 500 company to run over 150,000 agents by 2028 (Gartner, Apr 2026), yet Accenture's annual report counts about 814,000 people and no agents (Accenture annual report, Oct 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a listed company reports the number of AI agents it runs alongside its headcount.
  • By December 2029: a reporting standard or regulator asks for AI agents to be disclosed in workforce reporting.

What it touches today: How agents are budgeted, who owns them, and whether workforce plans count them.

Weak signal

Middle managers come back, to supervise the agents

Window: 2029 to 2035 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: The common view is that AI agents let companies remove management layers for good.

Gartner finds that agents systematically escalate ambiguity, judgement and recovery work to human employees (Gartner, Oct 2026), and managers already had 12.1 direct reports on average in 2025 (HCAMag, May 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a large employer creates a named management role for supervising AI agents.
  • By December 2028: a major survey finds average spans of control falling for the first time since 2022.

What it touches today: Plans to cut management layers, and how the supervision of agents is costed.

Weak signal

Workers win a contractual share of AI productivity gains

Window: 2030 to 2038 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: AI gains are expected to go to shareholders and to the scarce skilled staff who use AI best.

IG Metall has made profit-sharing a new item in its claim for the metal and electrical round (IG Metall, in German, Sep 2026), and Malakoff Humanis tied its use of AI to retraining rather than dismissals (Option Finance, in French, Sep 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2027: a major collective agreement includes profit-sharing or a productivity dividend linked to new technology.
  • By December 2029: a national government or EU measure encourages the sharing of AI gains in collective agreements.

What it touches today: How savings from AI are booked, and what is promised to staff when AI is introduced.

Option A · Least change

Cut intake and let AI absorb junior work

Reduce graduate and apprentice hiring as AI tools take over routine tasks.

Trade-off: Saves money now, but thins the pool of experienced staff the organisation will need by 2030, and invites disputes where AI terms are agreed.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Company Ladders; Free Agents

Option B · Middle course

Redesign junior roles, keep the intake

Keep hiring juniors, and rebuild their roles around supervised work that AI cannot do alone.

Trade-off: Costs more than cutting, but protects the experience pipeline in every future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Build an in-house academy for AI-assisted work

Train a larger cohort in-house, on the tools and tasks the organisation will use.

Trade-off: The biggest investment, and trained people may leave if the job market reopens.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Bargained Careers; Company Ladders

Options: Careers and training (people leaders and line managers)

Option A · Least change

Keep training as it is

Continue current programmes, which are largely led by compliance.

Trade-off: No new cost, but builds few careers while the job market stays frozen.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Free Agents

Option B · Middle course

Tie training to internal moves

Open more vacancies to internal candidates and fund training that leads to a named role.

Trade-off: A moderate cost that keeps people when they cannot move outside, and still helps if the market reopens.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Guarantee an internal route first

Offer every vacancy internally first, with paid retraining for those who apply.

Trade-off: Builds loyalty, but slows the hiring of scarce skills from outside.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Bargained Careers; Company Ladders

Options: AI terms and agents (boards and chief executives)

Option A · Least change

Deploy under management rights

Introduce AI tools and agents as management decisions, consulting only where law requires.

Trade-off: Fastest, but risks disputes and terms imposed later, and leaves agents without clear owners.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Company Ladders; Free Agents

Option B · Middle course

Agree a framework and name agent owners

Agree AI terms with worker representatives, and keep a register of agents with a named owner for each.

Trade-off: Takes time to negotiate, but gives stable rules and clear accountability.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Share the gains

Link the use of AI to profit-sharing or job guarantees, as some unions now seek.

Trade-off: Buys consent and loyalty, at a lasting cost if the gains are smaller than hoped.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Bargained Careers; Sector Passports

What We Are Not Planning For

Displacement

A general collapse in employment caused by AI before 2030

Stanford finds no widespread, economy-wide job displacement associated with AI (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, Aug 2026), and Germany's labour research institute finds no general employment collapse in AI-exposed occupations (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Oct 2026).

↺ Reinstated if unemployment rises across all age groups in AI-exposed occupations for two quarters while the economy grows.

Rebound

A return to the job-hopping market of 2021 and 2022

US quits are at 1.9% (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Sep 2026), and Germany's labour force is shrinking (Germany's Institute for Employment Research, in German, Sep 2026).

↺ Reinstated if the US quits rate stays above 2.4% for two quarters in a row.

EU law

A binding EU law on algorithmic management in force before 2028

The Commission's leading option is guidance on existing rules (Lovguiden, Oct 2026), and its proposal is not expected before December 2026 (Council of the EU, Oct 2026).

↺ Reinstated if the Quality Jobs Act proposal includes binding rules on algorithmic management and the Parliament and Council fast-track it.
