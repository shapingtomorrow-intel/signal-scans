Why This Matters Now

Rich countries are running out of easy ways to add workers: fewer people are arriving, fewer children are being born, and the people already here are getting older.

Governments are answering in different ways. America has cut immigration, and its workforce has stopped growing. Europe must decide what happens to the Ukrainians it sheltered, whose protection ends in early 2028. Japan and Korea have put limits on foreign workers. Through all of it, hospitals and care homes keep hiring, drawing staff from everyone else.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: plan on keeping the people you have rather than finding new ones, and check which of your staff hold a right to work with an end date.

Executive Synthesis

Which hiring plans on your desk this week assume there will be more people to hire next year?

The evidence in this briefing says the US workforce has stopped growing and Europe’s largest group of recent arrivals has a deadline.

Rich economies are running out of ways to add workers. In the US, net migration fell from 2.7 million in 2024 to 1.3 million in 2025, and the job growth needed to hold unemployment steady fell to fewer than 10,000 a month (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026). In Europe, protection for 4.43 million Ukrainians (Eunews, on Eurostat, Sep 2026) ends on 4 March 2028 (Council of the EU, Jul 2026). And health and care take a large share of whatever growth there is: German employment fell by 212,000 in a year while public services, education and health grew by 187,000 (Destatis, Germany’s statistics office, in German, Aug 2026). Part of Europe’s recent job growth also rests on one-off conversions of people already present, such as Spain’s 490,000 regularised workers (CaixaBank Research, in Spanish, Nov 2026); once those pools are converted, that source of growth ends.

Several things are open. Will the US labour force stay flat once population figures are revised? Will European governments widen the routes for Ukrainians before 2028, or extend protection again? Will care keep absorbing workers, or will budgets cap its hiring? And will Japan’s and Korea’s caps ever bind?

Looking ahead to 2030, the Congressional Budget Office projects that US deaths will exceed births from 2030 (Congressional Budget Office, Jan 2026), the US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) projects health care and social assistance will supply about 37.0% of new US jobs to 2035 (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Sep 2026), and England’s adult social care needs a further 410,000 posts by 2040 (LocalGov, on Skills for Care, Jun 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where readers can still choose their position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that the US workforce has stopped growing. Once a change to the population figures is removed, labour-force growth was still 0.2% (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026), and October’s payroll gain of 165,000 (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Nov 2026) is hard to square with no growth at all. If revised figures show net migration well above zero, the supply limit is looser than this briefing argues.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Re-base workforce plans on a labour supply that does not grow. Growth assumptions built on rising headcount no longer match the evidence.
  2. List staff whose right to work ends on 4 March 2028, and start conversions now. National routes are narrow and slow.
  3. Plan hiring against health and care, not alongside it. Care hires through downturns and will outbid others for the same people.

Each is developed below, with a decision posture, in the four Strategic Implications, and with alternatives in the Options.

1. America’s workforce has stopped growing

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The US administration and courts on enforcement, Congress on the law, employers on pay and retention · Room to act: Low on the numbers, high on retention: employers choose how they compete for a pool that is not growing

For a decade US employers could grow because the workforce grew, mostly through immigration. That has stopped. New hires now have to come from people already in work or already in the country, and the monthly jobs figures that used to signal a slowdown now signal a tight market.

Sources: (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026); (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Nov 2026). The break-even figures are Federal Reserve research estimates reported by the Congressional Research Service.

  • The workforce has stopped growing. The 12-month growth rate of the US labour force fell from -0.1% in January to -0.6% in September, or from 0.5% to 0.2% once a change to the population figures is removed (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026).
  • Lower immigration is the main reason. Census estimates net international migration fell from 2.7 million in 2024 to 1.3 million in 2025 (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026), and the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) expected only 570,000 in 2026, instead of 1.6 million (Congressional Budget Office, Jan 2026).
  • So the bar for a normal jobs month has collapsed. The job growth needed to hold unemployment steady fell from about 155,000 a month in 2023-2024 to 85,000 in 2025 and fewer than 10,000 in 2026 (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026); October’s gain of 165,000, with unemployment at 4.1%, sits far above it (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Nov 2026).
  • Employers feel it first where migrants worked. Among construction firms with craft openings, 88% say they are as hard or harder to fill than a year ago, and roughly 29% report an impact from immigration enforcement (Associated General Contractors of America, Sep 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Workers disappear on rumours. 12% of contractors say workers left or failed to appear because of actual or rumoured immigration actions (Associated General Contractors of America, Sep 2026), a loss that never shows up as a deportation figure.

Outlook to 2030

Population: CBO projects US deaths will exceed births from 2030, and that without immigration the population would begin to shrink that year (Congressional Budget Office, Jan 2026).

Growth: CBO projects potential growth over the next ten years 1.2 percentage points below its 1950-2025 average, mainly because the labour force grows more slowly (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026).

Politics: The AP projected that Democrats won both chambers of Congress (Scripps News, on the AP projection, Nov 2026), but the veto and the Senate’s 60-vote rule make major immigration reform unlikely soon (Envoy Global, Nov 2026).

What it means: Plan for a US workforce no larger in 2030 than today, and assume most new hires come from another employer.

Counter-argument

The flattening may be partly statistical. Once the population adjustment is removed, labour-force growth was still 0.2% (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026), the National Foundation for American Policy notes the measured decline may be overstated (National Foundation for American Policy, Sep 2026), and a payroll gain of 165,000 in October (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Nov 2026) is hard to square with a workforce that is not growing at all. If revised figures show net migration well above zero, the limit is looser than this theme argues.

On the System Map: loop B1 (pay draws people into work) is turning: participation across the OECD is at a record high while US labour-supply growth is near zero; leverage point 1 sits on retention.

2. Europe has until March 2028 to decide whether 4.4 million Ukrainians become permanent workers

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: EU member state governments, acting through the Council of the EU · Room to act: Moderate: employers can sponsor staff into ordinary permits now; governments decide how wide the routes are

About 4.4 million people from Ukraine live in the EU under temporary protection, and many of them work. Their status ends on 4 March 2028. National routes into ordinary residence exist, but they are narrow and open mainly to people already in stable work, so employers and public services that rely on these workers face a deadline few have planned for.

  • The end date is now set. EU countries agreed to extend temporary protection until 4 March 2028, and only for new applicants who comply with their military obligations in Ukraine (Council of the EU, Jul 2026); Eurostat counted 4.43 million holders at the end of July, 1,290,225 of them in Germany and 953,060 in Poland (Eunews, on Eurostat, Sep 2026).
  • The exits are narrow. Ireland’s transition scheme requires six months in work and a salary of at least EUR 29,432 (EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, Oct 2026); in Poland only 77,460 people have applied for the new three-year residence card, against an official estimate that 800,000 could (OKO.press, in Polish, Oct 2026).
  • Few plan to leave. Only 3% of refugees from Ukraine planned to return within the next twelve months, 28% were undecided, and over half were working (UNHCR, Jul 2026).
  • Work is rising, slowly. In Germany the employment rate of Ukrainian nationals rose by 4.2 points to 39.7% in June 2026 (IAB, Germany’s labour market research institute, in German, Sep 2026), and the EU Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA) finds that keeping and obtaining protection is becoming more difficult in some countries (EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Deadline: Temporary protection ends on 4 March 2028 (Council of the EU, Jul 2026), and Ireland’s transition scheme closes on 30 March 2027 (EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, Oct 2026).

Local: The European Committee of the Regions wants a phased, coordinated exit designed with cities and regions; its rapporteur says “Temporary protection was the right emergency response, but it cannot be the long-term answer.” (European Committee of the Regions, via Lovguiden, Oct 2026)

Precedent: Germany took in about 350,000 Bosnian war refugees in the 1990s as temporary guests; of the 37,000 still there in May 2000, none had a right to stay (Federal Agency for Civic Education, in German, Jan 2023). Unlike then, many Ukrainians now work, and the war continues.

What it means: Treat 4 March 2028 as a staffing deadline: identify who is affected and start conversions while the routes are open.

Counter-argument

The deadline may move again. Protection has already been extended for a further year (EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, Oct 2026), and with only 3% planning to return within a year (UNHCR, Jul 2026), governments may find another extension cheaper than changing millions of statuses at once. If the EU extends again in 2027, the cliff becomes a slope, though each extension still leaves employers with staff whose right to work has an end date.

On the System Map: loop B2 (deadline forces conversion) is priming: the March 2028 date pushes governments and employers towards ordinary permits, but uptake is still low; leverage point 2 sits on conversion routes.

3. Health and care are absorbing the workers every other sector wants

Near-Term

Weight: high · Who decides: Health systems, care commissioners and the governments that fund them · Room to act: High for other employers: they decide whether to compete head-on for the same people or design roles that do not

Health and care keep hiring through good times and bad, because ageing raises demand every year. When the workforce stops growing, that steady demand pulls people from every other sector. Care’s usual answer, recruiting abroad, is narrowing in Britain and elsewhere, and its budgets are under strain.

Source: (Destatis, Germany’s statistics office, in German, Aug 2026). Bars share one zero line, so the lengths compare directly.

  • In Germany, health, care and public services are the only growth. Employment fell by 212,000 in the year to the second quarter of 2026, while public services, education and health grew by 187,000 and manufacturing lost 159,000 (Destatis, Germany’s statistics office, in German, Aug 2026).
  • In the US, health care carried most of the year. It supplied about 3 of every 4 jobs added from September 2025 to September 2026 (Becker’s, on KFF, Oct 2026), and added 42,000 jobs in October, above its average monthly gain of 31,000 (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Nov 2026).
  • Britain’s care sector has lost both of its feeders. International recruits to adult social care in England fell to 30,000 in 2025/26, the lowest in four years, and posts filled by British nationals fell by 40,000 over the year, even as the vacancy rate fell to 6.2% (LocalGov, on Skills for Care, Jun 2026).
  • Pay is rising without the money to fund it. The UK minimum wage rises to £13.21 an hour from April 2027, and the Budget gives no ring-fenced uplift to council care fees (DAC Beachcroft, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Demand: BLS projects private health care and social assistance will add over 2.2 million jobs to 2035, about 37.0% of all new US jobs (US Bureau of Labor Statistics, Sep 2026).

Posts: England’s adult social care needs a further 410,000 posts by 2040 (LocalGov, on Skills for Care, Jun 2026).

Precedent: Japan’s care workforce fell for the first time since its care system began, then grew by only 487 people in 2024, to 2,126,227 (Kaigo Keiei no Kaiketsu, on Japan’s health ministry, in Japanese, Dec 2025). Britain is approaching the same limit with its overseas route narrowing.

What it means: Assume care will outbid you for staff in any downturn, and plan the roles that compete with it separately.

Counter-argument

Care’s pull may be weakening. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta finds health care’s share of US job gains fell to about 42% in the first nine months of 2026, with 10 of 13 sectors adding jobs (Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta, Oct 2026). And money, not people, may cap care hiring: the UK Budget raises pay without new funding for care fees (DAC Beachcroft, Oct 2026). If budgets ration care, it stops absorbing workers, and the shortage shows up as unmet care instead.

On the System Map: loop R1 (fewer workers, more unmet care) and loop B4 (budgets cap care hiring) are both priming: care keeps hiring while its feeders narrow and its wage bills outrun budgets; leverage point 3 sits on separate hiring plans.

Decision link: Strategic Implications 3 and 1.

4. Asia’s ageing economies are setting hard caps on foreign workers

Near-Term

Weight: medium · Who decides: Japan's Cabinet and immigration agency, Korea's employment ministry, Taiwan's labour ministry · Room to act: Low on the caps, moderate on retention and on which sectors and sites rely on foreign workers

Japan and South Korea used to let foreign-worker intake follow employers’ demand. Both now set numbered limits, and both are trying to keep the workers already there rather than rotate new ones in. For employers in the region, and for the countries that supply them, foreign labour has become a quota to plan around.

  • Japan’s intake now has a ceiling. The Cabinet set caps of 805,700 Specified Skilled Workers and 426,200 under the new training route, 1,231,900 in total (LNEWS, in Japanese, Jan 2026); when one sector’s intake was suspended about two months later, the government’s own expert panel called it an administrative mistake and asked for a review of other fields expected to reach their limits (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, in Japanese, Sep 2026).
  • Total numbers keep rising anyway. Japan had a record 4,281,026 foreign residents at the end of June 2026, up 3.8% in six months (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, in Japanese, Oct 2026).
  • Politics wants a total limit. Ministers will compile a basic policy on accepting foreign nationals within the fiscal year (Cabinet Office of Japan, in Japanese, Jul 2026), and the coalition partner Ishin wants it to limit the number accepted (Kyodo News, in Japanese, Jun 2026).
  • Korea has cut its quota and wants to keep workers longer. It set 80,000 places for its main foreign-worker visa in 2026, 38% fewer than in 2025 (Newsis, in Korean, Dec 2025), releases them in rounds with a 10,000 reserve for sectors that run short (Herald Korea, in Korean, Jun 2026), and plans to let workers who meet requirements stay on without the compulsory departure (Herald Korea, in Korean, Apr 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Caps: Japan’s new training route, which replaces its technical intern programme, starts in April 2027 (East Asia Forum, Oct 2026), inside the caps set in January (LNEWS, in Japanese, Jan 2026).

Taiwan: Taiwan had 873,347 migrant workers in March under a 35% cap on the share of foreign workers, and its first workers from India may arrive by the end of 2026 only if demand and India’s implementation allow (Newtalk, in Chinese, Apr 2026).

What it means: Treat access to foreign workers in Asia as a quota that can close mid-year, and plan staffing on permits actually issued.

Counter-argument

The caps may not bind for years. Japan’s foreign population is still rising by 3.8% in six months (Immigration Services Agency of Japan, in Japanese, Oct 2026), and an East Asia Forum analysis argues the tightening “is better understood as policy normalisation than a hawkish departure” (East Asia Forum, Oct 2026). Korea’s cut tracked weak visa take-up as much as policy (Newsis, in Korean, Dec 2025). If caps sit above demand, they are a ceiling nobody reaches.

On the System Map: loop B3 (shortages push caps up) is priming: one sector has hit its cap and reviews are asked for, but no cap has been raised; leverage point 4 sits on where employers source workers.

5. Fewer children are being born almost everywhere, including in the countries that send migrants

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: Families, and governments through family policy; no one controls it quickly · Room to act: Low on the trend, high on how long plans assume today's source countries will supply workers

Fertility has fallen below the level that keeps a population stable in most of the world, and it is now falling fastest in the lower- and middle-income countries that supply migrant workers. This changes nothing in a year, but it sets the size of the pool that rich countries will recruit from in the 2030s and 2040s.

  • Most people now live in low-fertility countries. Over 70% of the world’s population lives where fertility is at or below replacement, and 2.8 billion people live where older adults outnumber children (Population Reference Bureau, Oct 2026).
  • The fall has reached the countries that send migrants. “As of 2026, humanity is likely below replacement fertility.” Its authors find the fall concentrated in low- and middle-income countries (National Bureau of Economic Research, Sep 2026), and India’s fertility rate was estimated at 1.9 in 2024 (Down To Earth, May 2026).
  • Europe hit a new low. EU fertility fell to 1.34 births per woman in 2024, the lowest since 2001 (Eunews, on Eurostat, Oct 2026).
  • Governments are trying to push back. China’s State Council called for policies to stabilise the number of new births (Xinhua, on China’s State Council, in Chinese, Oct 2026), and South Korea’s births rose 13.0% in August, with 193,783 born from January to August, the most since 2019 (Korea Economic Daily, in Korean, Oct 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal Schools are the first institutions to shrink. Vermont has grouped its 119 school districts into 18 committees that will put mergers to local votes in 2028 (VTDigger, Sep 2026), and more than 1,000 US public schools closed in 2025-26, the fourth annual rise (CT Mirror, Sep 2026).

Outlook to 2030

Population: The Population Reference Bureau projects world population rising from 8.24 billion in 2026 to 9.62 billion in 2050 (Population Reference Bureau, Oct 2026), so the pool of young adults narrows long before total numbers fall.

Korea: Korea’s National Assembly Budget Office projects fertility of 0.9 this year, settling at 0.92 by 2045 (Seoul Shinmun, in Korean, Sep 2026).

United States: CBO projects US fertility of 1.58 in 2026 falling to 1.53 in 2036 (Congressional Budget Office, Jan 2026).

What it means: Treat recruitment from any one country as having a shelf life, and test plans that run past 2035 against a smaller pool of young workers.

Counter-argument

Births can rebound, and populations keep growing for decades. Korea’s births have risen for three years running (Korea Economic Daily, in Korean, Oct 2026), and the Population Reference Bureau notes that 58 of the 121 low-fertility countries are still projected to grow by 2050 (Population Reference Bureau, Oct 2026). The economists who find the world below replacement also say no single cause explains the fall (National Bureau of Economic Research, Sep 2026), so it may not run as far or as fast everywhere.

On the System Map: no loop yet: fewer births in source countries shrink the pool of young adults able to migrate, which lowers net migration slowly; leverage point 4 sits on where employers source workers.

Hover over a status for its meaning: Turning means every step in the loop has current evidence, so it is running now. Priming means the parts are in place but the circle has not yet closed at scale.

The Scenario Matrix’s horizontal axis asks whether foreign workers are rationed or admitted on demand, which is whether loop B3 (shortages push caps up) ever releases the caps; its vertical axis asks whether people at home are drawn into work, which is whether loops B1 (pay draws people into work) and B2 (deadline forces conversion) deliver.

What it means: the link that ties the five themes together is that every source of new workers now runs through people already present: those drawn in by pay, those converted from temporary status, and those kept longer under caps. The levers an organisation controls are whom it keeps, whom it helps convert, how it plans around care, and which countries and routes it relies on.

Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Re-base workforce plans on a labour supply that does not grow

US labour-force growth is near zero (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026) and health and care take a large share of new jobs (Becker’s, on KFF, Oct 2026). Finance and HR leaders should replace headcount growth assumptions with a flat-supply case, cost what it takes to keep staff, and set pay budgets on that basis rather than on hiring volume.

Action: Chief financial officers and HR directors to put a zero labour-supply growth case and a costed retention programme into the 2027 plan, by 31 March 2027.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 3. On the System Map: leverage point 1, retention, which eases the hiring pressure that loop B1 (pay draws people into work) answers only slowly.

Implication 2: List every employee whose right to work ends on 4 March 2028, and start conversions now

Protection for 4.43 million Ukrainians ends on 4 March 2028 (Council of the EU, Jul 2026), and the routes out are narrow: Ireland requires six months in work and a minimum salary (EU Agency for Fundamental Rights, Oct 2026), and Poland has had 77,460 applications against an official estimate that 800,000 could apply (OKO.press, in Polish, Oct 2026). Employers in host countries should identify affected staff and help each one onto the route that fits, while it is open.

Action: HR leaders with staff in EU host countries to list employees under temporary protection and file for ordinary permits where a route exists, by 30 June 2027.

Decide Draws on Theme 2. On the System Map: leverage point 2, conversion routes, which closes loop B2 (deadline forces conversion) with retained staff.

Implication 3: Plan hiring against health and care, not alongside it

In Germany only health, care and public services added jobs in the past year (Destatis, Germany’s statistics office, in German, Aug 2026), and England’s care sector needs 410,000 more posts by 2040 (LocalGov, on Skills for Care, Jun 2026). Workforce planners should separate roles that compete with care employers for the same people and give them their own pay, training and design plan.

Action: Workforce planners to split 2027-2028 hiring into roles that compete with health and care and roles that do not, with a separate plan for the first, by 30 June 2027.

Prepare Draws on Themes 3 and 1. On the System Map: leverage point 3, separate hiring plans, where care’s pull on other employers lands.

Implication 4: Track Asian intake caps and fertility in source countries as limits on where you recruit after 2030

Japan has capped its two worker routes at 1,231,900 (LNEWS, in Japanese, Jan 2026), Korea cut its main quota by 38% (Newsis, in Korean, Dec 2025), and India’s fertility is below replacement (Down To Earth, May 2026). Strategy teams should note which countries and quota routes their long-range workforce plans rely on and review them each year.

Action: Heads of strategy to list the source countries and quota routes the 2030 workforce plan relies on, and review them at each annual planning round from 2027.

Monitor Draws on Themes 4 and 5. On the System Map: leverage point 4, sourcing choices, which limits exposure to loop B3 (shortages push caps up) and to the shrinking pool of young adults abroad.

The Inside Track

↗ Shift from todayJapan’s cap of 1,231,900 (LNEWS, in Japanese, Jan 2026) and the 4 March 2028 deadline hold, and conversions of people already present become the main source of new workers.

In 2030 rich countries admit foreign workers by quota and price, and grow their workforces from people already inside: regularised migrants, refugees moved onto work permits, older workers and carers returning to work. Employers that invested in keeping and converting staff are staffed; those that relied on new arrivals are not. Pay rises fastest where conversion is hardest.

Early indicators: Work-based routes for protected and undocumented residents widened; participation at new highs; quotas unchanged.

Both Taps Open

↗ Shift from todayUS net migration recovers from 1.3 million in 2025 (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026) towards earlier levels, while participation at home keeps rising.

In 2030 governments have reopened intake to meet demand and kept drawing in older and inactive people at home. Labour shortages ease across most sectors; care still competes hardest for staff. Wage growth slows and the politics of migration stays contested, but employers can hire.

Early indicators: Quotas raised mid-year; visa processing times fall; older workers’ employment rates keep rising.

Hard Ceiling

↗ Shift from todayUS labour-force growth, 0.2% at best today (Congressional Research Service, Oct 2026), turns negative and stays there, and care vacancies rise again.

In 2030 intake is rationed and the people at home who could work do not: carers stay home, older workers retire, refugees lose status. Employers compete for a shrinking pool, care is rationed by waiting lists, and growth slows. Firms that automated or moved work abroad cope best.

Early indicators: Temporary protection ends without wide conversion; care waiting lists lengthen; job vacancies rise while employment falls.

Back to the Old Lever

↗ Shift from todayKorea reverses its 38% quota cut (Newsis, in Korean, Dec 2025) and others reopen visas, while participation at home stalls.

In 2030 governments meet shortages by admitting more foreign workers but do little to bring people at home into work. Employers can hire, but the pool depends on sending countries whose own young populations are shrinking, and on politics that can turn again.

Early indicators: Quotas raised; care visas reopened; participation flat or falling among older workers.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes each month. In November no future leads yet.

  • By March 2029: CBO projects net immigration through 2030 below the 570,000 it projected for 2026; evidence: cbo.gov.

Emerging

Ageing countries compete to keep foreign workers, not just to attract them

Window: 2030 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Migration policy is argued as a question of how many to let in, not how many will choose to leave.

Korea plans to let foreign workers stay on without the compulsory departure (Herald Korea, in Korean, Apr 2026), and Taiwan’s employers want longer stays so the workers they train do not leave for other countries (Newtalk, in Chinese, Apr 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2028: Japan, Korea or Taiwan offers foreign workers already in the country a faster route to permanent residence or a retention payment; evidence: the government’s announcement.

What it touches today: Contract lengths, housing and career paths for foreign staff, which decide whether they stay.

Options: Staffing when the workforce stops growing (employers)

Option A · Least change

Hire as before and pay more

Keep recruiting from outside and win people with higher pay.

Trade-off: Quick, but raises costs across the business and loses to care employers in any downturn.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Both Taps Open; Back to the Old Lever

Option B · Middle course

Keep and redesign

Hold on to existing and older staff through hours, workload and progression, and convert temporary staff to permanent status.

Trade-off: Slower and needs management time, but holds up whichever future arrives.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Move work to where people are

Automate roles or move them to sites and countries with workers to spare.

Trade-off: The biggest upfront cost and transition risk, but frees the business from local supply.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Hard Ceiling; The Inside Track

Options: Ukrainian staff before March 2028 (employers and national governments)

Option A · Least change

Wait for another extension

Do nothing until the EU decides whether to extend protection again.

Trade-off: Costs nothing now; if no extension comes, staff lose the right to work on one day.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Both Taps Open; Back to the Old Lever

Option B · Middle course

Sponsor eligible staff into ordinary permits now

Use the national routes that exist, starting with staff who meet the work and salary tests.

Trade-off: Administrative cost, and it leaves out staff who do not meet the thresholds.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Open a work-based route for everyone in work

Governments create one route to residence for every protection holder with a job.

Trade-off: Politically costly and harder to reverse, but keeps the largest number of trained workers.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Inside Track; Both Taps Open

Options: Competing with health and care for workers (employers outside care, and care commissioners)

Option A · Least change

Compete head-on

Match care’s pay and hours for the roles that overlap.

Trade-off: Keeps staff in the short run, but bids up wages for both sides.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Back to the Old Lever

Option B · Middle course

Share the pipeline

Train jointly with care employers, offer flexible or return-to-work roles, and share recruits across seasons.

Trade-off: Needs partners and trust, but widens the pool for both.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Stop competing for the same people

Redesign overlapping roles so they need fewer people, through automation or different skills.

Trade-off: Costly to redesign, but removes the contest with care.

⊞ Fits scenarios: Hard Ceiling; The Inside Track

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle’s evidence should put on the agenda, written to test assumptions rather than confirm them.

  1. If we could not grow headcount from outside hiring for four years, which parts of the business would we shrink first?
  2. Who in our organisation holds a right to work that expires before 2030, and who owns the plan to keep them?
  3. Which of our roles compete with hospitals and care homes for the same people, and are we paying or designing our way out?
  4. If school closures are the first sign of smaller cohorts, which of our sites and services depend on local cohorts that are already shrinking?
  5. Which of our current activities looks least wise in these futures?
