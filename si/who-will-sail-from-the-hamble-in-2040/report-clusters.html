Why This Matters Now

The people who will sail from the Hamble in 2040 are mostly in their twenties and thirties today, and fewer of them are likely to own a yacht.

Keeping a boat costs more each year, and younger adults hold far less wealth than the retirees clubs have long relied on. Racing is coming back, but mainly in one-design fleets and leagues where the boat is supplied. Around the club, rising seas and new rules to protect the Solent's seabed and water are moving towards the river, and boats are beginning, slowly, to leave diesel, which will ask more of the marina's power supply.

The one thing to take into the next meeting: decide what the club will offer a keen sailor who does not own a boat, and how that membership pays its way.

Executive Synthesis

Which of the club's plans to 2040 assume that tomorrow's members will own the boats they sail?

The evidence here says keeping a boat is getting harder for younger people, while racing on supplied boats is growing in Britain, France and Germany.

Since July, when this series moved the club’s horizon to 2040, one thing has sharpened: the club’s future depends on people who may never own a yacht. Running costs, not purchase prices, now stop people buying: brokers say a £20,000 boat that costs £5,000 a year to park makes buyers stop (Marine Industry News, May 2026), and nearly two thirds of 20 to 24 year olds still live with their parents (Resolution Foundation, Jun 2026). Racing is coming back, but to a lower level: Cowes Week returned to over 640 boats in 2026 after falling below 500 (Yachting World, Aug 2026), against more than 1,000 in its peak years (YACHT, Jul 2026). Around the club, the western Solent's draft strategy projects seas over 1 metre higher by 2120 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026), and Chichester Harbour made the area's first prohibited anchorage in April (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026).

Some of this is settled: the Climate Change Committee's pathway has half of leisure boats on electric drive by 2050 (Marine Industry News, reporting the Climate Change Committee, Mar 2025), and France will halve its boat tax on electric motors from 2027 (French environment ministry, in French, Jul 2026). Four things are not. Will young adults, the most active group on the water (All At Sea, Jul 2026), join a club that supplies the boat? Will supplied-boat leagues last, when Germany's is relaunching after difficult years (YACHT, in German, Oct 2026)? Will anchoring limits reach the Hamble, where seagrass has been replanted for the first time in nearly 100 years (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026)? And will Ofgem let marinas earn a return on charging (Ofgem, Oct 2025)?

Looking ahead to 2040, the Pensions Policy Institute projects the share of pensioner households owning their home falling from 79% to 64% by 2044 (Property118, reporting the Pensions Policy Institute, Aug 2026); the Royal Yachting Association aims to double active participation in water activities over the next decade (Marine Industry News, Sep 2026); and the Environment Agency is planning the western Solent's coast on a 100-year horizon (Environment Agency, Jun 2026). None of this is certain, and each is a place where the club can still choose its position: section 10 sets out the options.

Where this analysis could be wrong

The weakest assumption is that cost will keep the next generation from owning. Coastal berths remain largely full, with waiting lists in high-demand regions (British Marine, Jan 2026), used boats are getting cheaper (YACHT, Aug 2026), and home ownership among 16 to 34 year olds has risen over the past decade (Property118, reporting the Pensions Policy Institute, Aug 2026). If younger owners do arrive, the club's model of members and moorings holds, and a supplied fleet becomes an addition rather than the future.

The decisions that cannot be deferred this cycle

  1. Build a route into membership for sailors without a boat. The next generation may sail rather than own.
  2. Map which moorings, anchorages and buildings are exposed to 2040. Water levels and rules are both moving.
  3. Plan the marina's power before electric boats need it. Grid capacity and fire rules take years.

Each is developed in the Strategic Implications, with alternatives in the Options.

1. Owning a boat is becoming too expensive for the next generation

Immediate

Weight: high · Who decides: The Executive Committee, on membership categories, fees and what the club supplies · Room to act: High: the club sets its own membership model and prices

The cost of keeping a boat, rather than buying one, is now what stops people owning. Moorings, marina fees, insurance and maintenance keep rising, while younger adults hold far less wealth and live with their parents for longer. The appetite for the water is there; what is changing is the form it takes, towards cheaper, shared and more flexible ways to sail. The July edition saw the membership thinning; this edition looks at what the next members will want instead.

  • On the River Hamble, a Crown Estate ‘G’ mooring costs £281.38 per metre a year in 2026, with harbour dues of £270.61 for a boat of 9.8m to 12.2m, before any marina, insurance or maintenance costs (Hampshire County Council, Jan 2026).
  • Brokers say smaller boats are not selling because ownership costs too much, that a £20,000 boat that costs £5,000 a year to park makes buyers stop, and that shared ownership and boat clubs may matter more for younger buyers (Marine Industry News, May 2026).
  • Marina operators report affordability challenges among an ageing customer base, and almost half expect their local market to weaken over the next 12 months, although coastal berths remain largely full (British Marine, Jan 2026).
  • In German-speaking markets, used boat sales have fallen more than 40% in some months and listings now take 52 days to sell rather than 34, as ageing owners sell and pandemic buyers find they cannot afford the running costs (YACHT, Aug 2026).
  • Nearly two thirds (63%) of 20 to 24 year olds now live with their parents, and the wealth gap between people in their 20s and those in their 60s is just over £500,000 (Resolution Foundation, Jun 2026).
  • Cruising to France, a large part of what owning a cruising yacht on the Solent is for, is patchy again: since the European Union's new entry and exit checks ended their introductory period, French Channel ports have changed how British boats check in, port by port; more now accept emailed forms, but Boulogne has stopped, crew changes need the full process and some ports do not return the forms (Cruising Association, Aug 2026).

Outlook to 2040

The Pensions Policy Institute projects the share of pensioner households owning their home falling from 79% in 2024 to 64% in 2044, with private renting trebling to 18% (Property118, reporting the Pensions Policy Institute, Aug 2026), so fewer retirees will have the spare capital clubs have long relied on. The Royal Yachting Association aims to double active participation in water activities over the next decade (Marine Industry News, Sep 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 a growing share of new members sail boats they do not own, and by 2040 a club's strength is measured by the boats it supplies as much as by the berths it lets.

What it means: Plan membership for people who want to sail without owning a boat.

Counter-argument

Appetite and demand are strong. 82% of 16 to 34 year olds took part in a watersport or coastal activity in 2025, the highest of any age group (All At Sea, Jul 2026); coastal berths remain largely full, with waiting lists in high-demand regions (British Marine, Jan 2026); and home ownership among 16 to 34 year olds rose from 31% to 42% in a decade (Property118, reporting the Pensions Policy Institute, Aug 2026). Cheaper used boats may also bring new owners in (YACHT, Aug 2026).

On the System Map: loop R1 (dearer boats, fewer owners) is turning: running costs stop buyers, owners age and sell, and the income that keeps fees down thins; leverage point 1 sits on the boats the club supplies.

2. Racing's future lies in shared, one-design boats

Immediate

Weight: medium · Who decides: The sailing committee and the classes; national leagues and event organisers · Room to act: Moderate: the club decides what it hosts and supplies, not where classes go

Racing is recovering, but to a lower level than its peak, and the growth is in one-design fleets and in formats where the boat is supplied. The fastest-growing keelboat class is still privately owned, often with professionals aboard, but leagues that race on supplied boats now run in Britain, France and Germany, and they open racing to people who will never own a keelboat.

Sources: (YACHT, Jul 2026); (Yachting World, Aug 2026).

  • Cowes Week returned to over 640 boats and 5,000 sailors in 2026, after falling below 500 entries at its lowest point; growth came mostly from one-design fleets, with 60 XODs and 38 J/70s, and short-series racing was offered as a good value alternative (Yachting World, Aug 2026).
  • The British Keelboat League gives up to 15 club and university teams around 20 races each in the host club's six RS21s, with races of about 15 minutes, and its February event sold out with 13 teams (British Keelboat League, Jul 2026).
  • France's national club league is sailed on J80s supplied by the organiser and allocated by draw (French Sailing Federation, in French, Oct 2026), and Germany's sailing league has contracted twelve J/70s for its senior and junior leagues (YACHT, in German, Oct 2026).
  • Women are a growing share of the fleet: more than one third of Cowes Week competitors in 2026 were women, the organiser says (Cowes Week Ltd, Aug 2026), and around 20% of boats were helmed by women (Yachting World, Aug 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal The shared-fleet model is costly to keep going. Germany's sailing league is relaunching for 2027 after what its board calls good and difficult times; first-division clubs pay 14,100 euros a season, and 50 cents of every sponsorship euro will go to cutting member fees (YACHT, in German, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2040

The Royal Yachting Association's ten-year strategy aims to double active participation in water activities (Marine Industry News, Sep 2026), and SailGP aims for more than 30 million viewers per event by 2030, which may draw new people to the sport (YACHT, Jul 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 club racing on the Solent splits between owner fleets with professionals and supplied-boat leagues, and by 2040 the clubs that run their own fleets hold the middle of the racing calendar.

What it means: Decide whether the club will own and run a racing fleet, or rely on members' boats.

Counter-argument

One-design growth is not the same as shared boats. The fastest-growing keelboat class, the Cape 31, is privately owned with up to three professionals aboard, and its first Gold Cup drew 28 boats from eleven countries (YACHT, Oct 2026). A former Cowes Week regatta director says the event has settled at around 500 or 600 boats and that sailing is suffering falling participation worldwide (YACHT, Jul 2026), and owner fleets still fill the Round the Island Race, with more than 700 boats entered before its deadline (Yachts and Yachting, Jul 2026).

On the System Map: loop R2 (supplied boats bring new sailors) is priming: leagues on supplied boats bring in new teams, and teams need fleets, but no Solent club yet runs one at scale; leverage point 2 sits on young members joining.

3. Higher seas and stricter rules are changing the Solent

Near-Term

Weight: high · Who decides: The Environment Agency, harbour authorities, the water regulator and Defra · Room to act: Low on the rules, moderate on preparation: the club can map its exposure and take part in consultations

The water around the club is changing in two ways at once. Seas are rising and the coast is being re-planned, and the rules that protect the Solent's water and seabed are tightening. Neither has yet reached the Hamble in a way that stops boats, but both are moving towards it, and the club's moorings, anchorages and waterside buildings sit in their path. July placed the club's estate on a moving shoreline; since then the first binding protection rule has arrived in the Solent.

Sources: (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026); (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026); (International Bar Association, Jul 2026); (Environment Agency, Jun 2026); (Portsmouth City Council tender notice, Jan 2026); (ITV News Meridian, Oct 2026).

  • The Environment Agency's draft strategy for Hurst Spit to Lymington projects sea levels rising by over 1 metre by 2120, with properties at risk rising from 35 to more than 500 and saltmarsh and mudflats lost to coastal squeeze (Environment Agency, Jun 2026); the future of the moorings behind Hurst Spit cannot be guaranteed, and full funding is unlikely to come from central government (Yachting Monthly, May 2026).
  • Chichester Harbour made a prohibited anchorage from 27 April 2026 to protect a native oyster restoration site in the Solent Seascape Project (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026), and seagrass has been planted on the Hamble for the first time in nearly 100 years (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026).
  • Southern Water logged 485 dry day spills between January and May 2026, against 418 in the whole of 2025 (ITV News Meridian, Oct 2026), and the Clean Water Bill will abolish Ofwat and create a single regulator (International Bar Association, Jul 2026).
  • Registry records show work being commissioned around the club: Associated British Ports has awarded a contract to deepen the channels at the Port of Southampton and the Nab (Associated British Ports tender notice, Aug 2026), and the region's coastal engineering framework, worth £10,350,000 excluding VAT, runs only to 30 November 2026 (Portsmouth City Council tender notice, Jan 2026).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A sailing club asks for bathing water status. Warsash Sailing Club, across the river, is testing the Hamble to bathing water standards and considering asking for Kipper Pond to be designated as bathing water, citing 19 combined emergency outflows into the river (Warsash Sailing Club, Jul 2026).

Outlook to 2040

The Environment Agency takes a 100-year horizon and aims to adopt its western Solent strategy by 2027 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026). In France, a decree of February 2026 raised the number of communes that must plan for coastal retreat from 126 to more than 370, with homes and business premises expected to be affected by 2050 (Weka, in French, Mar 2026). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 seabed protection brings anchoring limits to more of the Solent, and by 2040 decisions on which waterside facilities around the Hamble are defended, moved or allowed to flood are being taken.

What it means: Know which of the club's moorings, anchorages and buildings are exposed, and to what.

Counter-argument

The water may get cleaner and the restrictions may stay light. Southern Water says it met its target for reducing storm overflow spills and plans around £7.8bn of investment in its region (Water Magazine, Jul 2026), the Solent's restrictions so far protect small, marked sites (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026), and no anchoring or mooring rule has been announced for the Hamble's new seagrass (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026).

On the System Map: loop B1 (protection closes anchorages) is priming: anchoring damages restored seabed, protection closes the area, and fewer anchorages mean fewer boats there; leverage point 3 sits on the moorings and anchorages available.

4. Boats are starting to leave diesel, and marinas will need the power

Longer-Range

Weight: medium · Who decides: Government and Ofgem on targets and charging rules; insurers on battery risk; the club on its marina · Room to act: Moderate: the club controls its marina's power plans and safety rules, not the pace of change

Leisure boats will leave diesel slowly, but the direction is set, and the first fast chargers are already on the Solent. For a club that runs a marina, the hard part is not the boats but the power: grid capacity, the rules on charging for electricity, and the fire risk of large batteries moored alongside other yachts. July set out a charging and low-carbon fuel pathway for the marina; this edition adds the rules that will decide its cost.

  • The Climate Change Committee's pathway has half of the UK's inland and leisure fleet switching to electric drive by 2050 and the other half to biofuels (Marine Industry News, reporting the Climate Change Committee, Mar 2025); the government's maritime strategy aims for at least a 30% cut in maritime emissions by 2030 and will consider requiring zero or near zero emissions from vessels at berth (British Marine, Apr 2025).
  • Ofgem is reviewing the rule that resold electricity cannot be charged above what the reseller paid, naming marine craft as a specific area (Ofgem, Oct 2025); British Marine says the rule lets marinas recover installation costs but not make any profit, so they have no incentive to invest (British Marine, Nov 2025).
  • Marine insurers describe yacht fires as low-frequency but high-severity events, with losses spreading to adjacent yachts and marina infrastructure, and warn that breaching battery standards may lead to disputes or withdrawal of cover (International Union of Marine Insurance, Jun 2026).
  • Rules nearby are moving: Amsterdam's emission-free zone for pleasure boats stands while the Dutch watersports federation appeals (Watersportverbond, in Dutch, Mar 2026), and France gives a 50% reduction in its boat tax for electric and hydrogen motors from 1 January 2027 (French environment ministry, in French, Jul 2026).
  • Registry records show batteries and shore power arriving first in commercial shipping: the European Union has committed Horizon Europe funding to a battery ferry with high-capacity shore charging (EU grant record, Jul 2026), and shore power for DP World Southampton has been put out to tender (London Gateway Port tender notice, Jun 2025).

Early indicators

  • Weak signal A fast charger arrives next door. Lymington Harbour has installed a 75kW fast charger, part of a network that received £3.2 million through the government's UK SHORE programme and has delivered eight new charging sites on the south coast (Marine Industry News, Oct 2026).

Outlook to 2040

The government's strategy runs to zero lifecycle emissions from maritime fuel by 2050 (British Marine, Apr 2025), and the Climate Change Committee sees biofuels as an essential transitional fuel (Marine Industry News, reporting the Climate Change Committee, Mar 2025). On this briefing's reading, by 2030 the first members keep electric day boats and tenders at the marina, and by 2040 charging is expected on every pontoon, while cruising yachts and offshore boats still run largely on diesel or biofuel.

What it means: Plan the marina's grid capacity and battery safety now; the boats will follow at their own pace.

Counter-argument

Batteries may do less of the work than expected. A worked example puts a cruising yacht's electric range at about 20 to 25 nautical miles, against 300 to 500 on diesel (Practical Sailor, Mar 2026), and Falmouth Harbour now sells hydrotreated vegetable oil, a renewable diesel, to leisure boats from a £50,000 refuelling facility (Biofuels Digest, Aug 2026).

On the System Map: loop R3 (chargers bring electric boats) is priming and loop B2 (fire risk slows batteries) is priming; leverage point 4 sits on shore power and grid capacity.

System Map

Read together, the four themes are not separate pressures. Five loops connect them. Dearer boats, fewer owners (R1): running costs stop people owning, and thinner club income pushes costs onto those who remain. Supplied boats bring new sailors (R2): boats the club supplies bring in young sailors, who make league teams that need fleets. Protection closes anchorages (B1): anchoring damages restored seabed, and protection closes the area. Chargers bring electric boats (R3), while fire risk slows batteries (B2). Strategic Implications

Implication 1: Open a route into membership for sailors without a boat

Brokers expect shared ownership and boat clubs to matter more for younger buyers (Marine Industry News, May 2026), the wealth gap between people in their 20s and those in their 60s is just over £500,000 (Resolution Foundation, Jun 2026), and leagues in France race on boats the organiser supplies (French Sailing Federation, in French, Oct 2026). The club should offer a costed way to sail and race without owning a boat, alongside its owner membership.

Action: The Executive Committee to decide by 31 March 2027 whether the club will own or lease a small keelboat fleet for members without boats, with a membership category priced to match.

Decide Draws on Themes 1 and 2. On the System Map: leverage point 1, boats the club supplies, which slows loop R1 (dearer boats, fewer owners) and feeds loop R2 (supplied boats bring new sailors).

Implication 2: Carry young sailors from training into adult racing on supplied boats

The British Keelboat League gives club and university teams around 20 races each in supplied RS21s (British Keelboat League, Jul 2026), women are a growing share of Cowes Week competitors (Cowes Week Ltd, Aug 2026), and Germany's experience shows a supplied fleet needs sponsorship to keep fees down (YACHT, in German, Oct 2026). The sailing committee should give every young sailor a team and a boat to race after they age out of junior classes.

Action: The sailing committee to enter or host supplied-boat league racing for members aged 18 to 30 in the 2027 season, with a costed plan agreed by 31 March 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 2. On the System Map: leverage point 2, young members joining, where loop R2 (supplied boats bring new sailors) turns.

Implication 3: Map the club's exposure on the water and ashore to 2040

Seas in the western Solent are projected to rise over 1 metre by 2120 (Environment Agency, Jun 2026), the area's first prohibited anchorage arrived in April (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026), and pollution reporting is tightening to 2030 (ITV News Meridian, Oct 2026). Flag officers should know which moorings, anchorages, slipways and buildings are exposed, and speak for them in the consultations that will decide them; the exposure map is the first part of the to-2040 estate plan the July edition called for.

Action: Flag officers to commission an exposure map of the club's moorings, anchorages and waterside buildings, and register the club for the Hamble's coastal and harbour consultations, by 30 September 2027.

Prepare Draws on Theme 3. On the System Map: leverage point 3, moorings and anchorages available, where loop B1 (protection closes anchorages) acts on the club.

Implication 4: Plan the marina's power and battery safety before electric boats arrive in numbers

Half of leisure boats are on the electric path by 2050 (Marine Industry News, reporting the Climate Change Committee, Mar 2025), Ofgem has not yet decided whether marinas may earn a return on charging (Ofgem, Oct 2025), and insurers may withdraw cover where battery standards are breached (International Union of Marine Insurance, Jun 2026). The marina should know its grid capacity and set battery and charging rules before it needs them, as the first steps of the charging pathway set in July.

Action: Marina management to obtain a grid capacity assessment and adopt battery charging and fire rules for the pontoons by 31 December 2027, and decide on a first charging point once Ofgem rules on resale.

Prepare Draws on Theme 4. On the System Map: leverage point 4, shore power and grid capacity, where loop R3 (chargers bring electric boats) can be started safely.

The Owners' Club

↗ Shift from todayFrom marina operators reporting an ageing customer base (British Marine, Jan 2026) to a club that kept its moorings and its owners but not the next generation.

In 2040, members still own most of the boats, and the Solent is used much as it is today. The club thrives on berths and moorings, but its members are older and fewer, and the waiting list has shortened. Racing runs on members' boats; young sailors who cannot afford a keelboat drift to other clubs and other sports.

Early indicators: Owners keep buying; no anchoring limits reach the Hamble; supplied-boat leagues stay small.

The Sailing Hub

↗ Shift from todayFrom leagues racing on boats the organiser supplies (French Sailing Federation, in French, Oct 2026) to a club whose fleet, not its berths, is the main reason people join.

In 2040 most new members sail boats the club owns or leases. The water is open, so the club runs fleets, coaching, leagues and cruising days for members who pay for use rather than berths. The membership is younger and larger; owners remain, but as one group among several.

Early indicators: Membership categories without a boat grow; club fleets are booked out; supplied-boat leagues expand.

The Guarded Harbour

↗ Shift from todayFrom the area's first prohibited anchorage to protect oysters (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026) to owners working round closed anchorages and moorings at risk.

In 2040, members still own their boats, but the Solent is far more restricted. Anchorages are closed to protect the seabed, some moorings are lost to higher water and realigned shores, and the costs of defending waterside facilities fall on clubs and marinas. Owners pay more for less water, and only the committed stay.

Early indicators: More prohibited anchorages; coastal strategies choose retreat near moorings; berth costs rise faster than inflation.

The Managed Fleet

↗ Shift from todayFrom seagrass replanted on the Hamble (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026) to club fleets of electric boats sailing in protected, managed water.

In 2040 the club supplies most boats, many of them electric, and they sail in water managed like a park, with marked zones, permits and cleaner rivers. Sailing is easier to start and harder to do anywhere you like. The club's licence to operate depends on being seen as a careful steward of the river.

Early indicators: Permits tied to seabed protection; bathing water status on the Hamble; electric club boats charged at the marina.

Which future is gaining momentum: the Scenario Stress Test scores twelve leading indicators on these two axes. In October no future leads: the reading sits firmly on the side of restricted water, and back near the centre on whose boats members sail.

Potential Surprises to 2040

Emerging

Moorings and anchorages are allocated by environmental permit

Window: 2031 to 2038 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Moorings are treated as a matter of waiting lists and fees, with nature protection limited to small, marked sites.

Chichester Harbour has made a prohibited anchorage to protect a native oyster site (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026), and seagrass is being restored on the Hamble (Hampshire and Isle of Wight Wildlife Trust, Jul 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By June 2028: a Solent harbour authority makes a mooring or anchoring permit conditional on a seabed protection rule; evidence: that authority's local notice to mariners or byelaw.
  • By December 2029: a byelaw or protected area restricting anchoring is proposed for part of the central Solent; evidence: the Marine Management Organisation or Defra.

What it touches today: Where members anchor and moor, and how the club speaks for them.

Weak signal

The Hamble becomes a designated bathing river

Window: 2031 to 2036 · Probability band: Low (under 20%)

Why it would surprise: The river is seen as a working harbour whose water quality is the water company's problem, not a place to swim.

Warsash Sailing Club is testing the Hamble to bathing water standards and considering a designation request (Warsash Sailing Club, Jul 2026), while Southern Water's dry day spills rose in 2026 (ITV News Meridian, Oct 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a club or community group applies for bathing water designation on the River Hamble; evidence: Defra's bathing water consultation or the Hamble Estuary Partnership papers.
  • By December 2029: a stretch of the River Hamble appears on the official list of designated bathing waters; evidence: Defra or the Environment Agency.

What it touches today: Launching, junior sailing and events, which would sit inside a bathing water's rules.

Weak signal

A marina fire forces battery rules on every berth

Window: 2031 to 2036 · Probability band: Low-Medium (20 to 40%)

Why it would surprise: Battery fires are seen as rare, and each boat's insurance as the owner's own affair.

Marine insurers say yacht fire losses spread to neighbouring yachts and marina infrastructure, and that breaches of battery standards may lead to withdrawal of cover (International Union of Marine Insurance, Jun 2026).

Signposts to watch:

  • By December 2027: a UK marina insurer or The Yacht Harbour Association issues battery charging rules for berth holders; evidence: the insurer's or association's published guidance.
  • By June 2029: a UK marina makes proof of compliance with battery standards a condition of a berth; evidence: the marina's published berthing terms.

What it touches today: Berthing terms, fire plans and the marina's own insurance.

Each option is graded against all four futures and a wild card in the Scenario Stress Test: three hold in every future (add a membership with access to club boats; enter and host supplied-boat leagues; map exposure and plan grid capacity and battery safety), three are bets, strong in some futures and weak in others, and three backfire in at least one future (stay a club for boat owners; run racing around members' boats; wait for rules and demand).

Options: Membership and boats (flag officers and the Executive Committee)

Option A · Least change

Stay a club for boat owners

Keep recruiting owners, with membership and berthing priced as now.

Trade-off: No new cost, but the membership ages and shrinks as fewer people own boats.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Owners' Club; The Guarded Harbour

Option B · Middle course

Add a membership with access to club boats

Lease or buy a small keelboat fleet for members without boats, with a membership category priced to match.

Trade-off: A moderate cost that holds in every future and opens the club to younger sailors.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Become a sailing hub

Make club-run fleets, coaching and racing the centre of membership, with owners as one group among several.

Trade-off: The biggest change, and costly if members keep buying their own boats.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Sailing Hub; The Managed Fleet

Options: Racing and young sailors (the sailing committee)

Option A · Least change

Run racing around members' boats

Keep the calendar built on owner fleets and visiting classes.

Trade-off: Strong while owners persist, but young sailors leave when they age out of junior boats.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Owners' Club; The Guarded Harbour

Option B · Middle course

Enter and host supplied-boat leagues

Field club teams in supplied-boat leagues, host league rounds, and give young sailors a route into them.

Trade-off: A modest cost that keeps young sailors racing in every future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Own a one-design fleet and run a year-round league

Buy a fleet of one class and run a league open to every member, winter and summer.

Trade-off: The biggest investment, and a class can fall out of favour.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Sailing Hub; The Managed Fleet

Options: Marina and shore (marina, clubhouse and operations)

Option A · Least change

Wait for rules and demand

Change power supply, defences and anchoring arrangements only when rules require it.

Trade-off: Cheapest now, but changes made late cost more, and the club has no voice in the decisions.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Owners' Club; The Sailing Hub

Option B · Middle course

Map exposure and plan grid capacity and battery safety

Map what is exposed to water and rules, assess grid capacity, and set battery and charging rules.

Trade-off: A modest cost that prepares the club for any future.

⊞ Fits scenarios: All four

Option C · Biggest shift

Invest ahead: charging on every pontoon and adapted waterside buildings

Install charging across the marina and adapt the most exposed buildings now.

Trade-off: Expensive, and early if electric boats and restrictions arrive slowly.

⊞ Fits scenarios: The Managed Fleet; The Guarded Harbour

What We Are Not Planning For

Collapse

A collapse in demand for berths on the Solent before 2030

Coastal berth occupancy remains high and waiting lists persist in high-demand regions (British Marine, Jan 2026).

↺ Reinstated if south coast marinas report falling occupancy and the end of waiting lists for two years running.

Ban

A ban on petrol and diesel engines in leisure boats on the Solent before 2040

The Climate Change Committee's pathway still has half of the leisure fleet on biofuels in 2050 (Marine Industry News, reporting the Climate Change Committee, Mar 2025), and the government's strategy only considers rules for vessels at berth (British Marine, Apr 2025).

↺ Reinstated if a UK government or harbour authority proposes an emission-free zone for leisure boats in the Solent, as Amsterdam has in its canals.

Closure

The loss of the Hamble as a sailing river before 2040

The coastal strategies now being written plan on a 100-year horizon (Environment Agency, Jun 2026), and restrictions so far protect small, marked sites (Chichester Harbour Conservancy, Apr 2026).

↺ Reinstated if a coastal strategy for the Hamble proposes withdrawing defences from frontages that hold moorings or club facilities.

Questions For The Next Strategy Meeting

Five questions this cycle's evidence should put on the agenda.

  1. What could a keen sailor who does not own a boat do at the club today, and what would it cost them?
  2. How many of the young people we train are still sailing with us at 25, and in whose boats?
  3. Which of our moorings, anchorages and waterside buildings are exposed to higher water or new protection rules, and who speaks for them?
  4. How much power could the marina supply to charging boats, and what are our rules for batteries on the pontoons?
  5. Which of our current activities looks least wise in these futures?
