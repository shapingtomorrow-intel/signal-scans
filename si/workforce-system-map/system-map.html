How Hiring, Careers, AI Rules and AI Agents Act on Each Other

22 October 2026 · Companion to the Decision Intelligence briefing of 22 October 2026, Who Gets Hired? Who Sets the Rules? Who Manages the AI? · Horizon: to 2030 Built from the briefing’s 50 verified sources. Links drafted by analysis; to be challenged and judged by people before use.

The Decision Intelligence briefing says what is changing in who gets hired, who sets the rules for AI at work and who manages the AI. This System Map shows why the pieces move together: which changes feed each other, which push back, and where an employer’s effort changes how the whole system behaves rather than one part of it.

Read it by following the arrows. A + means the two move in the same direction; a − means they move in opposite directions. Where the arrows close into a circle, they form a loop: an R loop reinforces itself, so change feeds on change, and a B loop balances, pushing back against change. Hover over a loop label or an arrow for its explanation and evidence.

2. The four loops this quarter

Reinforcing loops run in both directions: the same circle that compounds decline would compound recovery. The status on each card is this cycle’s reading, and is what the monthly Change Tracker updates. Turning means every step in the loop has current evidence, so it is running now; Priming means the parts are in place but the circle has not yet closed at scale. Hover over a status for its meaning.

Turning

R1 Experience drought

AI takes over junior tasks, fewer juniors are hired, fewer people learn on the job, and a thinner pool of experienced staff pushes firms to lean harder on AI for routine work. It is turning: employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed US occupations is about 19% below that of less-exposed peers, up from 15% a year earlier, and in France permanent hiring of young people fell 28.3% in the most exposed jobs.

Watch: the gap for young workers in AI-exposed jobs, and graduate and internship hiring surveys.

Turning

R2 Frozen market

Fewer people leave their jobs, so fewer vacancies open, so fewer people can move. US quits held at 1.9% in August, and UK vacancies fell to 691,000. Shrinking labour supply in Germany and lower immigration in the US mean this is more than a downturn.

Watch: the US quits rate against 2.4%; vacancies; whether switchers keep out-earning stayers.

Priming

B1 Bargaining brake

This is the system’s reply to fast change: more AI at work raises concern, concern produces agreements, and agreements set conditions on how AI is used. 108 collective agreements in European services already contain AI provisions, and Germany’s metal round puts job security on the table. Where there is no bargaining partner, the brake does not exist.

Watch: Germany’s metal round after 31 October; the Quality Jobs Act in December.

Priming

R3 Flattening loop

Agents let firms cut management layers, and fewer managers push more coordination work to agents. Around 41% of employees say their companies trimmed management layers last year, and managers had 12.1 direct reports on average in 2025.

Watch: management layers and spans of control; whether any firm names managers of agents.

What the map shows that the briefing alone cannot
  1. Two loops pull on the same thread. R1 Experience drought and R2 Frozen market both run through the pool of experienced staff, one through junior hiring and the other through training and internal moves. An employer that cuts junior intake and training together weakens the pool twice, and while both loops turn, neither the graduate market nor the job market will refill it.
  2. Agreements are the only brake on the map. B1 Bargaining brake is the one balancing loop. Where there is no union, works council or employee forum, nothing slows the pace at which AI absorbs junior tasks or agents replace managers, which is why the scenario axis on who sets AI terms matters so much.
  3. Flattening reaches the experience pipeline through coaching. R3 Flattening loop looks like a question about management costs, but its link from management layers to training and internal moves means that fewer managers also means fewer people teaching juniors, so the flattening loop feeds the experience drought.

3. Four leverage points

Each point is numbered on the map, and each ties to a Strategic Implication already in the briefing, so the map changes the reasoning behind the plan rather than adding a second plan.

  1. 1

    Redesign junior roles

    Junior tasks done by AI is where R1 starts. Rebuilding junior roles around supervised work that AI cannot do alone keeps intake, and the experience it creates, flowing.

    DecideBriefing Implication 1: a redesigned junior role for each main entry route, and the 2027 intake, by 31 March 2027

  2. 2

    Tie training to internal moves

    Training and internal moves is the route R2 leaves open. Training that leads to a named role turns a frozen market into internal careers rather than stalled ones.

    PrepareBriefing Implication 2: internal fill rates and role-linked training reported to the board from early 2027

  3. 3

    Agree the terms

    Collective agreements are where B1 brakes. An agreed framework for AI at work sets the brake by design rather than by dispute.

    DecideBriefing Implication 3: talks on an AI framework with worker representatives by the end of March 2027

  4. 4

    Name an owner for every agent

    AI tools and agents in use is where R3 starts. A register of agents, each with a named human owner, decides whether flattening leaves work unsupervised.

    PrepareBriefing Implication 4: a joint HR and IT register of AI agents by 30 June 2027

4. How the map explains the scenarios

The two axes of the briefing’s Scenario Matrix 2030 are the map’s two dominant uncertainties: how strongly B1 Bargaining brake works, that is whether AI terms are agreed with workers or set by employers alone, and whether R2 Frozen market keeps careers inside organisations or the open market moves again. The Scenario Stress Test tracks which of the four is gaining momentum.

Careers built: inside firms → open market
Open market · agreed terms

Sector Passports

R2 eases as people move again; B1 is strong and set across sectors, so training and AI terms travel with workers.
Open market · employer terms

Free Agents

R2 eases but B1 is weak: AI and agents spread fast, and R1 bites hardest on people without early experience.
Inside firms · agreed terms

Bargained Careers

R2 holds people inside; B1 is strong, so agreements slow R1 and R3 and fund internal moves.
Inside firms · employer terms

Company Ladders

R2 holds people inside and B1 is weak: R1 and R3 run unchecked unless each employer builds its own ladder.
AI terms: agreed with workers → set by employers

Read this way, the scenarios stop being four stories and become four states of the same system, each with early indicators already on the map.

7. How an employer would use it

The value of a System Map comes as much from arguing over the arrows as from the finished picture. This draft is a starting point:

Challenge sessionOne facilitated session of about 90 minutes in which HR, operations and worker representatives test every arrow, add what the evidence missed and agree the leverage points. Their corrections become part of the map.
Country and business lensesThe same structure, shaded by country or business unit: the loops are shared, but their strength differs, so one country’s bargaining brake and another’s frozen market can sit on one map.
Other topicsSkills shortages in a sector, the move to hybrid work or the spread of AI in one profession can be mapped the same way, so leadership sees one method across the portfolio.
