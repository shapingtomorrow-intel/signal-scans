What's been found this cycle

Updated 23 September 2026 · brings together the seven deliverables published between 27 August and 23 September 2026

This page pulls the current cycle's findings into one place, in plain language. Each finding links to the deliverable behind it.

The picture in five points

  1. Money stopped being the constraint. Permission took its place. Budget growth across European and Canadian defence halved to 2.1% in 2026 while the commitments themselves hardened into dated programmes. What now sets delivery pace sits in recruitment offices, permitting queues, test-range calendars and data-rights clauses.
  2. Rearmament is being written into civilian law. France has legislated priority for military orders over civil ones. Germany is ending the employer's right to refuse a reserve call-up. A German health-security draft would route up to a thousand patients a day through civilian hospitals.
  3. Sourcing rules now ask questions many suppliers cannot answer. From 1 January 2027 a US contractor can no longer plead that no domestic source exists, and must trace parts back to the raw material. A suspended Chinese export rule returns on 10 November and catches goods by value rather than by sector.
  4. Grey-zone attack has become an operating condition, and it is largely uninsured. Arson has reached the new arms plants, drones cross the eastern flank routinely, and satellite positioning is being jammed daily. Insurers are redrawing the war exclusion while state terrorism pools do not cover sabotage linked to a state.
  5. The boom is funding very few winners. Defence-technology capital and exits are both at records, but conversion into contracted programmes of record is visible only at the very top of the market.

What it means if you are not a defence company

  • Screen your bill of materials against the returning Chinese export rule by value, not by whether the product looks like a defence product.
  • Give permission risk one named owner. Permitting, test access, engagement authority and data rights compound, and none of them sits with the function that carries delivery risk.
  • Quantify the share of your workforce that carries a reserve liability, starting with Germany, before the employer veto ends.
  • Ask your broker how a loss caused by jamming or by deniable sabotage would be characterised under your current wordings.
  • Treat any market size built on NATO's 1.5% tier as an estimate with no denominator behind it.

Actions from this cycle

The Action Triggers reads across the whole cycle and turns it into actions, each with an owner and a date.

  1. Decide Screen the bill of materials against the 0.1% Chinese export threshold before 10 November. Procurement, with the COO, by 9 October 2026.

1. What you are allowed to source

What to doDecideDirectionAccelerating
What was found
  • The binding change in US defence sourcing is evidentiary rather than material.
  • Executive Order 14415 stops non-availability waivers for covered materials from 1 January 2027 unless a contractor files an accepted mitigation plan.
  • Failure to qualify a domestic source no longer counts as non-availability, and prime contractors and subcontractors at any tier must submit an indentured bill of materials traced to raw-material origin.
  • Announced US government non-equity investment in rare earth projects reached $7.6 billion from the start of 2025 through June 2026, a 321 percent increase on the $1.8 billion announced before it.
  • A suspended Chinese export rule returns on 10 November 2026 and sets exposure by a 0.1% value test rather than by end use, so it reaches magnets and coatings inside turbines, motors, scanners and drives.
What it means

Two rules now ask the same question from opposite directions: what is in this product and where did it come from. Neither is answered by a purchase order, and screening takes weeks.

What to watch next

Whether implementing guidance narrows the Chinese value test, and whether any survey shows how many firms outside defence have screened their bills of materials at all.

2. Firm orders, slower growth, and who gets chosen

What to doPrepareDirectionBudget growth stableAllocation contests accelerating
What was found
  • European rearmament has converted into costed, dated programmes that place a decade of guaranteed demand on an industrial base shared with civil manufacturing.
  • Growth halved to 2.1% in 2026 from 4.6% in 2025, so a fixed line with selection attached replaced an expanding one.
  • Brussels has started choosing: five projects of common interest carrying a 190 billion euro ambition against 325 million euro of seed money.
  • NATO recorded a $139 billion increase in defence spending from European Allies and Canada in 2025, and SAFE is a 150 billion euro loan instrument for member states.
  • About 30% of the spending pledge sits in a defence-related tier with no agreed definition and no reporting line.
What it means

Commitment is no longer the variable. Allocation is. Suppliers should expect contests over who is selected rather than over how much is available.

What to watch next

The Council decision on the five projects and the October 2026 EDIP call, and any agreed NATO definition for the 1.5% tier.

3. The claims landing on civilian capacity

What to doPrepareDirectionAccelerating
What was found
  • Germany's Reserve Strengthening Act ends the employer's consent to reserve call-ups, with the reserve required to more than triple by 2033.
  • Its own impact statement records that annual compliance costs for business reduce slightly, and names no other cost. The chambers of commerce say business does not share that assessment.
  • Replacement reimbursement is capped at 30 days and paid only when an equivalent replacement is hired. Call-up notice moves from four weeks to eight.
  • France has legislated priority for military orders over civil ones, with strategic stockpiles attached, and a German health-security draft would route up to a thousand patients a day through regional hubs.
  • Recruitment remains the harder constraint: Germany asked about 298,200 people to volunteer and received roughly 530 commitments.
What it means

An employer outside defence can now lose staff, queue position or hospital capacity by statute rather than by contract. A better price does not resolve a queue set by law.

What to watch next

The German health-security draft, and whether the French priority mechanism is triggered in practice.

4. Grey-zone attack as an operating condition

What to doPrepareDirectionAccelerating
What was found
  • Russian drones breached Romanian airspace more often in the first eight months of 2026 than in the previous four years combined, and the North Atlantic Council met on 12 August to say so.
  • Arson has reached the plants Europe is paying to build, and their protection has no settled owner: the CER Directive's designation round covers eleven sectors that do not include defence manufacturing.
  • One night's fire at a Polish site caused damage of at least 15 million złoty. The only EU money on offer is capped at 500,000 euro per project.
  • Deniable sabotage falls between two boundaries being drawn by different hands. Insurers are drafting which hostile acts do not count as war, while state terrorism pools certify terrorism rather than state-linked sabotage, and the US backstop expires at the end of 2027.
  • Premium income for the political violence class is estimated at around $1.5 billion against insured loss estimates already approaching double that.
What it means

The exposure is commercial before it is military. A plant, a cable or an aircraft can be damaged by an actor nobody will name, under a policy that was written for war or for terrorism and covers neither.

What to watch next

The first marine or aviation wording that separates jamming-induced loss from hostile action, and any designation that brings defence plants inside a critical-entity regime.

5. Capital, concentration and who owns the repair data

What to doMonitorDirectionStable
What was found
  • Defence-technology capital and exits are both at records while distribution is extremely concentrated.
  • First-half 2026 exit value reached roughly $72bn excluding SpaceX, ahead of every prior full year, but conversion into contracted programmes of record is visible only at the very top.
  • In Europe the sector took 43% of all deeptech funding and 13% of total venture investment in 2025, a share that has tripled in three years, and the top five start-ups account for almost 60% of the capital raised since 2021.
  • The FY27 Senate NDAA would make government-purpose rights the default for technical data and software, reaching a sustainment pool carrying about half of a legacy platform's lifetime profit.
What it means

The risk here is concentration rather than famine, and the margin question is who owns the repair manual rather than who wins the build.

What to watch next

The conference text on data rights rather than the headline budget, and whether conversion to programmes of record broadens beyond the top of the market.
