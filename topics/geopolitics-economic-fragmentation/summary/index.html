This page pulls the current cycle's findings into one place, in plain language. Each finding links to the deliverable behind it.

The picture in three points

  1. Supply is now rationed by governments, not priced by markets. Diesel exporters meter what they release, the US and the EU are keeping metal scrap at home, China matches Western sanctions lists, and US research funding follows trade blacklists. The binding constraint has moved from cost to permission.
  2. Coercion is turning inward on allies and hardening into law. Washington has moved from duties to outright import bans on Canadian goods, and Europe rations steel to its own partners. A new US sanctions law lets the President tariff buyers of Russian energy.
  3. Close US partners are forming ties with each other that bypass Washington, inside clubs of like-minded countries. Canada, the EU, Australia, Japan and South Korea are making trade, defence and energy deals with each other, while rules on minerals, carbon and digital trade are set by groups rather than universal bodies.

Key risks and opportunities

Key risks

  • This winter's diesel will go to buyers with contracts, reserves or a government deal, not to the highest bidder. Exporters review their curbs every few weeks, so buyers without government-to-government supply must bid for whatever is released. See Theme 2 →
  • A supplier chosen for its reliability can be switched off by its own politics. Importers who replaced Russian and Middle Eastern diesel with American cargoes now face a supplier whose export decisions may follow its election calendar. See Theme 1 →
  • Every new sanctions listing now carries an automatic counter-cost. China answers Western entity listings with matched lists of Western firms, so European defence and dual-use suppliers are exposed each time a new package is drafted. See Theme 5 →
  • Political risk is spreading from tariff lines to coups, seizures and rewritten mining rules. A coup attempt in Niger, asset seizures and new mining codes mean a project's exposure now depends on local politics, not only on trade rules. See Theme 6 →

Key opportunities

  • Early deals with Washington buy cheaper access to the US market. The UK cut its US medicine tariff by agreeing to pay more for new medicines, and drug makers with pricing deals gained certainty while those that wait pay the default. See Theme 5 →
  • Partners who pool fuel are better shielded when exporters ration supply. Shared stockpiles and joint buying among allies give members a cushion that buyers acting alone lack, and the model could spread to other partnerships. See Theme 3 →
  • Mineral suppliers outside China can be paid for being a second source. Western floor-price contracts and government purchasing are building a separate, higher price for rare earths from outside China, rewarding producers with approved origins. See Theme 4 →
  • A carbon price that can be proven at the border becomes an export asset. Exporters in countries whose carbon schemes are recognised by the UK or the EU keep relief at the border that competitors elsewhere lose. See Theme 4 →

What it means if you do not work in trade or energy

  • If your business runs on lorries, ships or farm machinery, ask your suppliers where their fuel comes from and what they would do if one exporter stopped selling.
  • When you choose a new supplier or market, weigh the politics of friendly governments as well as rivals, since allies now use tariffs and export limits too.
  • Before taking on a research, licensing or joint-venture partner abroad, check whether they sit on a government list that could later stop the work.
  • If you sell to French public bodies or rely on French grants, check whether your cash plans would cope with a budget that pays existing commitments but holds new starts.
  • If you buy medicines or run health services outside the US, expect some new treatments to arrive later or cost more as governments trade pricing for lower US tariffs.

1. The US may keep its diesel at home, and Europe and Latin America would pay most

What to doDecideDirectionNot yet tracked
What was found
  • US diesel passed $6.50 a gallon in September with stocks at a four-decade low, and President Trump said he may support a ban or cap on exports.
  • Weekly US distillate exports fell 17.5% to 1.331 million barrels a day in the week to 18 September, the lowest since March, which suggests voluntary cuts may already be under way.
  • The US supplies about 32% of the EU's diesel imports from outside the EU, and Chile relies on American diesel for about two-thirds of what it uses.
  • The US Energy Information Administration expects American diesel stocks to stay below their recent five-year low through much of 2027.
What it means

A formal ban is opposed by the Energy Secretary and the industry, so a quiet squeeze is the likelier path. The pressure to keep diesel at home looks set to outlast the elections.

What to watch next

Whether Washington imposes a formal ban or cap before the elections, and whether weekly exports keep falling after them.

2. Fuel exporters are putting their home markets first

What to doDecideDirectionGulf shipping risk stable
What was found
  • Two very large crude carriers were reported attacked in Hormuz on 1 September, and flows are recovering but not secure.
  • Russia, China, India, Thailand and Kazakhstan now meter diesel exports through rolling bans, permits that depend on home stocks, and levies reset every fortnight.
  • Global stocks fell by 507 million barrels between February and August, and diesel is where the shortage bites hardest.
  • Russia has banned most diesel exports since July and India has taxed them since March, adjusting its levy every fortnight as conditions change.
What it means

The marginal cargo now depends on a decision taken in another capital, for domestic reasons. Contracts, reserves and government-to-government supply matter more than finding the cheapest cargo.

What to watch next

Whether China cuts its export permissions for the last quarter of the year, or Russia extends its producer diesel ban into the winter.

3. Europe, Canada, Japan, South Korea and Australia are building ties that bypass Washington

What to doPrepareDirectionCanada-US rupture accelerating
What was found
  • From 29 September five US proclamations banned imports of Canadian alcohol, dairy and motorcycles, adding to 50% duties on about $20 billion of Canadian goods from 22 August; Canada answered with counter-tariffs from 8 September.
  • Only 35% of Canadians now call the US a reliable partner, and Canada suspended trade talks in August.
  • Canada joined the EU's defence buying programme, and the EU and Australia concluded a trade deal that should be in force around the end of 2027.
  • Japan and South Korea agreed to share oil and fuel with each other.
What it means

These ties sit beside US links rather than replacing them, and the US still takes most of Canada's exports. What changes is that each partner now has somewhere else to go.

What to watch next

Whether Canada and the US resume talks or widen the import bans, and whether the EU-Australia agreement is signed on schedule.

4. A new order is forming from clubs of like-minded countries

What to doMonitorDirectionElection reversals stableMinerals truce stable
What was found
  • France's 2027 budget now runs on constitutional dates rather than a vote count: the ordinance route opens on 17 December, the session closes on 28 February, and a special law that runs on costs at least 0.5 point of GDP.
  • Brazil's 2027 budget leaves its next president little room: mandatory items take 91.7% of primary spending, and the country's fiscal watchdog says meeting the surplus target needs a R$35.7 billion contingency.
  • The World Trade Organization could not agree even basic items this year while clubs keep expanding; the UK and the EU now test whether an exporter's carbon price counts at their border, and Türkiye created a voluntary top-up so its exporters can qualify.
  • The G7 wants no single outside supplier to provide more than 60% of its rare earths and magnets by 2030, while Brookings and BISI judge the US-China truce fragile and the underlying dependency unchanged.
What it means

Membership is becoming a commercial asset: the clubs that recognise a country's carbon price, minerals or digital rules decide what its exporters pay. Budget rules and deadlines, more than election results, limit how far governments can change course.

What to watch next

Whether France falls back on an ordinance or special law for its budget, whether Brazil's vote becomes a market shock, and whether The truce rollover becomes a standing minerals arrangement.

5. Governments now grant access to markets, supplies and research partners by list, quota or condition

What to doPrepareDirectionSanctions escalation accelerating
What was found
  • US exports of black mass and tungsten scrap from recycling now need a government exception, and the EU has drafted a default ban on metal waste exports from 21 May 2027; the International Energy Agency expects recycling rates to rise from around 10% to close to 20% by 2040.
  • The US tariff on patented medicines is 100%, capped at 15% for the EU, Japan, South Korea and Switzerland; 26 manufacturers covering 89% of the branded drug market hold pricing deals, and the UK moved from 10% to 0%.
  • The Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act, signed in September, lets the President tariff top buyers of Russian energy; China already answers Western listings with its own, naming 14 EU entities on 24 July, a day after the EU's 21st sanctions package.
  • From FY2027 the US National Science Foundation will bar its funds from collaboration with anyone on twelve restricted-party lists, and 30 universities were ordered to audit their partnerships.
What it means

Access is now administered, so a rate or permission can change by notice, as the UK's medicine tariff did. Even recycled metal is becoming a managed good held at home, and the cost often lands abroad.

What to watch next

Whether the US extends its scrap export order before it lapses, and whether the first tariff under the sanctions Act spares China.

6. Resource projects now depend on a queue of sovereign consents, not only on a licence

What to doMonitorDirectionCoups and unrest acceleratingResource nationalism stable
What was found
  • Hundreds of soldiers attacked positions in Niamey on 29 August, and protest activity has been rising for two years.
  • African governments are rewriting mining codes and seizing assets, and host demands for equity and revenue lengthen the time before new capacity outside China can loosen Chinese control of supply.
  • The US DFC approved $414.2 million for Niger's Dasa uranium mine, but disbursement waits on a permit extension, repayment approvals, a direct agreement and an export route that only Niger and its neighbours can supply.
  • Lenders with sway over the government gain a seat at the table; junior miners with a single export route and no such lender carry the exposure.
What it means

Country risk recurs at every step: a settled dispute still leaves the next permit, agreement and border decision to negotiate, and instability in the capital can stall each one.

What to watch next

Whether Niger publishes an extension of the Dasa mining convention and permit that matches the loan term, and whether a signed direct agreement is disclosed.
