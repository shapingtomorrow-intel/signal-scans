Enforcers bargain with Google as cyber reporting goes live and deadlines close in

Brussels is in dialogue with Google after its July fine, cyber incident reporting became mandatory on 11 September, and a flat EU duty on small parcels applies. Ten signals, three new, three cooled a stage.

Edition 5, previous cycle: “Enforcement and liability bind as Europe’s simplification push runs out” (18 September 2026).

The consensus read on regulation this year has been retreat and delay. This cycle's movement is bargaining and live duties. Brussels ordered Google to end its self-preferencing (European Commission, July 2026), and the Commission now says it is in dialogue with Google and will assess its changes (The Next Web, October 2026), so the 60-day clock (PPC Land, July 2026) has become a negotiation. Cyber incident reporting became mandatory on 11 September (McCann FitzGerald, September 2026), and a temporary €3 parcel duty has applied since 1 July (European Commission, June 2026). Pay transparency, software liability and conduct rules outside the EU cool from Accelerating to Stable. On this evidence, the contested steps ahead are December's transposition deadlines rather than new orders.

Pattern narrative

Regulators negotiate before they punishEvolving Risk

Regulators are holding powers they have not yet used to the full. The Google decisions carried fines of €460 million and €430 million and an order to end the conduct (European Commission, July 2026), with 60 days to comply (PPC Land, July 2026). The Commission says Google has started changing search results (The Next Web, October 2026), and Kluwer reads such closed-door agreements as a trade of accountability for speed (Kluwer Competition Law Blog, September 2026). The AI Office has been able to enforce since 2 August (European Commission, July 2026), with wider oversight under the Omnibus (European Commission, July 2026), yet this cycle's evidence shows no case. In Tokyo, a developer coalition warns Japan could become an outlier unless its regulator moves to formal enforcement (MLex, August 2026), and the CMA is still consulting (Competition and Markets Authority, June 2026).

Deadlines bind before national laws arriveTransformation Driver

The dates that bind arrive before national law does. Pay transparency's 7 June deadline passed with only a few member states ready (Morgan Lewis, June 2026); Greece has since finalised its law while others draft towards 2027 (L&E Global, July 2026). Platform work follows on 2 December (Littler, August 2026) and software liability on 9 December, with Hungary the only adopter in the June count (Jones Day, June 2026). The Employment Rights Act reaches British employers in tranches, from 30 October (Osborne Clarke, July 2026). On this evidence, late transposition transfers the obligation rather than removing it.

One channel, one fee, one standardEmerging Opportunity

Uniform EU instruments are replacing national patchworks. Cyber reporting runs through one platform, with an early warning within 24 hours and a full notification within 72 hours (European Commission, September 2026), and other parties can become manufacturers and fall under the duty (McCann FitzGerald, September 2026). The €3 parcel duty applies the same rules to all operators and countries (European Commission, June 2026), though one vendor reports freight seeking other routes (Bigblue, October 2026). The revised sustainability standards await publication after scrutiny closed on 4 September (Linklaters, July 2026). The designs are settled; the open questions sit in application.

Signals gaining momentum

  1. Cyber Resilience Act reporting duties live. A first published action over a missed 24-hour warning would show how broad the Act's reach is.
  2. EU platform enforcement decisions. The verdict on Google's changes decides whether the order alters the product or ends in settlement.
  3. EU flat duty on low-value parcels. One more quarter of data separates falling volumes from freight finding other routes.

Collision 1speeds up +2

Brussels fines on Google become the yardstick Tokyo and London face

Platform enforcement → Outside-EU rules

On its own the Google decision is a European competition story. Read with the UK consultation and the developer complaint in Tokyo, it sets the standard each regulator is judged by, which is why the weakest enforcer draws the pressure.

Watch: Whether the Japanese regulator opens a formal case; whether the CMA publishes a steering decision after its consultation.

Collision 2speeds up +2

Delayed AI rules make product liability the first binding test for AI software

AI Act enforcement → Software liability

The Omnibus pushes the high-risk AI obligations to December 2027. The revised liability directive must be transposed by December 2026 and already names software and AI-driven technologies. On this evidence, claims rather than regulators will test AI products first.

Watch: National liability laws published before the December deadline; the first claim naming AI-driven software as a defective product.

Collision 3speeds up +1

Mandatory incident reports hand claimants a record of known software flaws

Cyber reporting → Software liability

Since 11 September manufacturers have to report actively exploited vulnerabilities through one platform. The liability regime treats software as a product. Together they turn a security duty into potential evidence of a known defect, for the same manufacturers.

Watch: Whether liability laws adopted nationally create disclosure routes to those reports; the first claim citing one.

Implications

Four dates order the watch list: the Commission's assessment of Google's changes (The Next Web, October 2026), the UK trade union tranche on 30 October (Osborne Clarke, July 2026), platform work on 2 December (Littler, August 2026) and software liability on 9 December (Jones Day, June 2026). Employers in states without a pay transparency law warrant planning for the standard to arrive through courts first. Makers of products with digital elements warrant checking whether they count as a manufacturer under the reporting duty (McCann FitzGerald, September 2026).
