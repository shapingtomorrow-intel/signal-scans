The distance premium unwinds; new cost floors form on land

Suez traffic is back to around two-thirds of pre-crisis volume and container spot rates keep sliding, while US truckload linehaul rates rise at double digits, the US port-fee pause now runs to 9 January 2027 and shipping's carbon price heads to a divided December session.

The consensus read on moving goods is that cheap capacity is back: almost all major carriers have returned to the Red Sea, with Suez traffic at around two-thirds of pre-crisis volume (SupplyChainBrain, November 2026) and October canal receipts up 94% year on year (EnterpriseAM Egypt, November 2026). The sharper movement is where the costs went. The Suez rebuild, Stable last cycle, is now Accelerating, and the war premium it unwinds at sea is reappearing on land: US truckload linehaul rates, new to this register, rose 10.1% year on year in September (FreightWaves, October 2026), Washington's port fees are paused only to 9 January (gCaptain, November 2026), and shipping's carbon price returns to a divided adoption session on 4 December (The Maritime Executive, September 2026). The question for the reader is which cost floor binds their 2027 contracts first.

Pattern narrative

War and fees keep the cost floorEvolving Risk

Three signals sit here, and each is a cost floor set by something other than demand. The war is now priced as durable: the IMO tracked 131 confirmed incidents near the strait, 35 of them in October, the highest monthly total of the conflict, yet freight and insurance costs near 10 times pre-war now read as the adapted normal rather than a crisis premium (Reuters via Cyprus Mail, November 2026). The port-fee reprieve is dated, not resolved: USTR suspended liability again from 10 November through 9 January 2027 to leave room for negotiation (Federal Register, October 2026). And on US highways the floor is capacity itself: linehaul rates excluding fuel reached their highest reading in four years while shipments grew just 1.9% (FreightWaves, October 2026).

The capacity cycle turnsTransformation Driver

Four signals sit here, and the market-facing sources argue a supply-driven softening: the orderbook above 14m TEU, 42% of the fleet, delivers into slowing demand (WorldCargo News, September 2026), and with 60% of Asia-Europe capacity routed via the canal again the spot composite has slipped to 4,389 dollars per 40ft, Hormuz and a possible US West Coast strike the named risks that could yet prop rates (Daily Cargo News, November 2026). Evergreen returned on 8 November, leaving Zim the only holdout, a return the canal authority frames as revenue repair (EnterpriseAM, November 2026). The two Tier 1 releases argue their own stories, and we read them as the swing's context rather than its evidence: Rotterdam reports a resilient nine months while constrained deep-sea terminals move cargo to other ports until new capacity opens from end 2026 (Port of Rotterdam Authority, October 2026), and Beijing marks 140,000 cumulative rail trips with border handovers up 12.8% this year (NDRC, October 2026).

Rulebooks and robots reset costsEmerging Opportunity

Three signals sit here, each on its own clock. Shipping’s carbon price goes to a December vote with both readings in play: Carbon Brief says the framework survived the spring talks and the committee will try to adopt it in December (Carbon Brief, May 2026), while the September talks ended with US opposition unmoved and design questions deferred (The Maritime Executive, September 2026). Aviation’s scheme has the opposite problem, demand without supply: authorised credits would cover at most a quarter of up to 200 million tonnes of first-phase demand, with only 11 countries positioned to supply (Reuters via Global Banking & Finance Review, November 2026). On the road, Aurora calls the quarter a commercial inflection point: it has passed 500,000 commercial driverless miles (DC Velocity, September 2026) and expects to reach 20 trucks a week in early December, with room to scale further (Aurora Innovation, October 2026).

Signals gaining momentum

  1. Suez rebuild releases capacity. Full normalisation, now a carrier decision rather than a war outcome, would complete a five-percentage-point cut to ship demand growth and force 2027 contract strategy early.
  2. Orderbook hits the water. A failed December rate push would confirm the line cannot hold and make idling or scrapping the next watchable response.
  3. US truckload repricing on supply. A 22nd straight month of rate growth into the 2027 bid season would turn a data series into a budget line.
  4. CORSIA's credit squeeze. Authorisation decisions by supplier governments over the next months decide whether airlines pay 12 dollars or recovery-grade prices for the same tonne.

Watch: Waiting times and the transhipment share at Rotterdam as re-timed loops arrive from December.

Collision 3speeds up +1

Rising truck rates strengthen the case for driverless fleet orders

Truckload repricing → Driverless trucks

Watch: Whether Aurora reaches 20 trucks a week in early December and a 500-truck commitment converts to orders.

Implications

Five dates order the watch list. The IMO working group returns on 23 November and MEPC 85 ends on 4 December (The Maritime Executive, September 2026): a decision point for carbon compliance strategy either way. Port-fee liability returns at a minute past midnight on 10 January unless the pause is extended again (gCaptain, November 2026), so a reinstatement scenario for China-built tonnage stays worth costing. CORSIA compliance purchases fall due by the end of January 2028 against scarce eligible supply (Reuters, November 2026), which rewards early procurement. Rotterdam's new terminal capacity arrives from the end of 2026 (Port of Rotterdam Authority, October 2026); until then, weekly blank-sailing counts (Daily Cargo News, November 2026) are the single best early signal of whether returning Suez capacity swamps the rate floor.
